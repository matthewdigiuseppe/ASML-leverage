## Clayton, Maggiori and Schreger: precise answers

Sources: the Econometrica text is behind a login. I read NBER WP 31852 ("November 2023, Revised 2024"), which is already set in Econometrica format with running heads and page numbers. I also read the published Supplement (econometricsociety.org, file 23206SUPP.pdf). Theorem numbering differs slightly between versions. The supplement calls the constant-price and no-externality assumptions "Definitions 1 and 2", while the WP calls them 3 and 4. Cite the published version by section and equation, not by definition number. Page numbers below are WP pages.

### A Framework for Geoeconomics (`clayton2026framework`)

1. **Is the hegemon's extraction bounded by the target's participation constraint? Yes.** A targeted firm accepts the hegemon's take-it-or-leave-it contract only if $V_i(\Gamma_i) \ge V_i(S_i)$ (Sec. 3.1, eq. (3), WP p. 17). The authors call this constraint "the limits to the hegemons' power" (intro, WP p. 3). The hegemon's power over a firm (Micro-Power) is the **slack** in this constraint when the hegemon applies its threats but demands nothing. A firm with such slack is a "pressure point" (Definition 1; WP pp. 14 and 17). With constant prices and no externalities, transfers are set so the constraint binds (Corollary 1, WP p. 25). With externalities, the hegemon may choose to leave slack, for example to friendly sectors (discussion of Prop. 3, WP p. 23). **The paper's "slack principle" should credit this.** Two differences matter for the ASML model:
   - In the baseline the outside option is the target's value **without the hegemon's joint threat**, not its value after being cut off. Power comes from **raising the inside option**: the hegemon's coordinated enforcement adds value, and it then extracts that value.
   - Threats to cut off a target that refuses (the "do what I say or else" threats) are in Supplement B.3.1. There the participation constraint becomes $V_i(\Gamma_i) \ge V_i^o(P_i)$. In a Markov equilibrium such outside-option threats are "partially self-defeating", because the same threat lowers future continuation values. The US threats in the ASML model (security withdrawal, penalties on ASML) are outside-option threats in CMS's terms. The hegemon also cannot commit to future contracts (Markov; Sec. 3.1, WP p. 15).
2. **Are threats pooled across activities? Yes.** "Joint threats" are trigger strategies in which a deviation in any one relationship triggers exclusion from many (intro, WP pp. 2-3; Sec. 3). Proposition 1 (WP p. 18) shows that it is weakly optimal for the hegemon to merge all feasible threats into one "maximal joint threat" against every firm it contracts with. The pooled threats enforce the target's on-path promises to its suppliers. They relax the target's incentive constraints, and the hegemon then charges for the surplus they create. They are not threats that punish refusal of the hegemon's demand. Supplement B.3.7 sketches military enforcement as another way to raise enforceability, and it can be either a complement to or a substitute for joint economic threats. That is the closest the framework comes to the security channel.
3. **Competing hegemons (in this paper, not the fragmentation paper).** Supplement B.2, "Hegemonic competition for dominance", Proposition 5, studies two hegemons that compete over transfers, with constant prices and no externalities. If pressure points are "isolated", an equilibrium exists in which each hegemon offers its maximal joint threat with no wedges, and firms accept **both** contracts. Transfers depend on overlap:
   - With no overlap in the hegemons' threats, each extracts its own contribution.
   - With full overlap, the hegemons bid transfers down to zero, as in Bertrand competition, and firms keep the full surplus.
   - From a firm based in one hegemon's country, the other hegemon extracts until the firm is indifferent. The firm's own hegemon's threats become its outside option.

   This is the nearest formal precedent for the ASML rival bidder. It differs from the ASML model in two ways. The contracts are non-exclusive, while the ASML client chooses one alignment. And the hegemons supply enforcement rather than bidding for a client's policy. The intro's claim that no earlier work considers "a dependent ally ... in a race in which a rival great power would pay for access" still holds for that combination. The literature section, however, should acknowledge Supplement B.2.
4. **Control rights.** WP footnote 10 (p. 27): "We think of purchasing a controlling stake as a way to bypass the participation constraint since then the hegemon can simply dictate the actions." The footnote adds that the stake's private market value is below its social value to the hegemon, which gives a rationale for investment screening such as CFIUS. This bears directly on Sec. 5 (preventive demands for control rights). **Check that the footnote survives in the published Econometrica text before citing it**; I could not access that text.

### A Theory of Economic Coercion and Fragmentation (`clayton2026theory`)

1. **Participation constraint.** In this paper threats do work on the outside option. A target that rejects the hegemon's offer loses access to the hegemon's inputs (Sec. 2.2, eqs. (2)-(3), NBER p. 13: $V_{in}(\tau) - T_{in} \ge V^o_{in}$). The participation constraint "traces the limits of hegemonic power" (p. 13). The optimal contract holds the target to its outside option (intro p. 2; Lemma 2 in the general model).
2. **Commitment to limit coercion.** Sec. 2.6, "A Hegemonic View of International Organizations", Proposition 6 (pp. 20-22). The hegemon commits to extract only a share µ of the target's inside option. For µ small enough, with many countries, the commitment raises the hegemon's welfare relative to no commitment. The resulting allocations match the non-cooperative equilibrium without a hegemon, and foreign countries are also better off than under full fragmentation. If µ is too high, the outcome reverts to full fragmentation (App. A.3.3). Without commitment, each country fully insulates itself, and the resulting fragmentation is Pareto inefficient (Sec. 2.5). The authors read the result as showing that a hegemon values rules that bind only itself, and that the liberal order is "an incarnation of hegemonic economic statecraft" (pp. 3 and 22). Proposition 12 gives the general version. **Use:** this contrasts with the ASML paper's compact (Sec. 5), where the **client** binds itself. Cite CMS for the patron-side version and for why a patron might accept limits on its own coercion.
3. **Credibility with large targets.** Footnote 10 (p. 12) and App. A.4.3 discuss credibility. Punishing an atomistic firm costs the hegemon nothing. In a repeated game, though, an incentive constraint on carrying out punishments would bind more tightly "if instead the hegemon were to contract with a small number of large entities". **Use:** this supports deriving credibility rather than assuming it, which the paper does for a single large firm and a single large ally.
4. **Competing hegemons: not modelled.** Footnote 23 (p. 25) says that multiple hegemons competing, or hegemons emerging endogenously, are "beyond the scope of this paper that takes the existence of one hegemon as given". The empirical section measures US and Chinese power separately, each in a narrow version and a coalition version. The "American Coalition" includes the euro area. Footnote 28 (p. 32) says that defensive coalitions pursuing coordinated anti-coercion would improve a bloc's bargaining position. The paper should not attribute any competing-hegemon result to this paper. The two-hegemon result is in the framework supplement (B.2).

## Flags for the authors

1. **Banks (2000)** is in APSR, not Economic Theory. Use the corrected entry.
2. **`lacy2004theory`** does not support "States that link issues can trade concessions across them." Rewrite as suggested in the table.
3. **`clayton2026framework`**: the pooled threats are enforcement threats that raise the inside option, and outside-option threats are "partially self-defeating" (Supp. B.3.1). Say this when the paper contrasts its own threats with CMS's. Credit CMS for treating power as slack in the participation constraint, and acknowledge the two-hegemon extension (Supp. B.2).
4. **Control rights**: if CMS WP footnote 10 is in the published version, it is the most direct economics precedent for Sec. 5's preventive demand for control rights.
5. **Prop. "Time to pain" (`06_extensions.tex`, Sec. 6.2)**: Fernandez and Glazer (1991), Haller and Holden (1990) and Busch and Wen (1995) show that when a player chooses its disagreement action each round (strike or holdout, here interrupt or service), complete-information bargaining can have many subgame-perfect equilibria, including inefficient delay. Part (a) states a single limit share $p^*$ and says only that "payoff-equivalent" delay equilibria also exist. The proposition should say whether $p^*$ is the unique subgame-perfect payoff or the stationary one, and it should cite these papers. The proof scripts should confirm which.
6. **Prop. "Protocol bounds" (e)**: cite `binmore1989outside` for the outside-option principle. For the remark about Europe exiting after its own demand is rejected, cite `shaked1994opting`, after checking its text. Cite `ponsati1998rubinstein` if the United States is also given a walk-away option.
7. **Prop. "Leverage equals the exit premium" (memo Lemma 1, bidding)**: `dekel2008vote` Prop. 2 is a published precedent for both the price formula and the refinement needed to pin it down.
8. **Fragmentation paper status**: it was published online by the JPE on 15 October 2026 with no volume or pages yet. Update the bib entry when the issue is assigned, and check the page and section references above against the JPE version.
