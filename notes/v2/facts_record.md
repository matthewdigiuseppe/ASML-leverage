## Synthesis for the model

**Instrument.** The record has four kinds of US instrument, and each case uses a different one:

- **Revocable status privileges** are the main one. These are Supplement No. 4 (exclusion from the SME FDP and de minimis rules), VEU status for the Korean fabs, Country Group A:5 for the UAE, and entity-list exemptions for Nexperia. Washington mostly coerces by granting, withholding or withdrawing a status that BIS can change by rule, not by threatening a new sanction.
- **Allied national licensing** carries the controls themselves. The Netherlands (2023, 2024, 2025) and Japan (2023) used national lists defined by parameters and named no country. The Dutch texts cite security and the Netherlands' "uitzonderlijke positie", never the US. Japan said it was "not aligning" with the US. Formal autonomy is preserved even when timing and substance follow US moves. This fits the slack principle: allies keep form and timing and concede on substance.
- **Ad hoc conditionality** appears in the Nexperia minutes and the Gulf chip approvals.
- **Explicit security-for-access trades** appear in the 2026 UAE rule.

**Linkage.** Explicit linkage between security conduct and export-control status appears in one case, and as a reward: BIS's July 2026 UAE rule justifies A:5 status and licence-free AI chips by the UAE's role in Operation Epic Fury and its Major Defense Partner status. The negative mirror image appears in 2026 security policy but is not tied to chips. Germany lost 5,000 troops after Merz's criticism, and Spain drew a trade cut-off threat after denying bases. So the US now links security behaviour to economic and military rewards and punishments, but no public record links European chip controls to troop posture. Korea's VEU loss and the US-Korea trade deal were negotiated in parallel, with no documented link. The Nexperia minutes show issue-specific conditionality: the CEO's removal was expected as the price of an entity-list exemption. That is linkage within the export-control domain, not across domains. For the model, cross-domain linkage is a feasible and recently demonstrated US instrument, but it has not been used against the Netherlands. The US security threat therefore belongs off the equilibrium path, or as a latent type.

**Compensation.** Compensation is rare and comes as status, not side payments. The Netherlands and Japan were placed on Supplement No. 4 after aligning (2023 to 2024). That is the only visible "payment", and it is revocable. Korea got annual licences to keep operating, not to expand: a partial restoration of the status it lost, not compensation. The Gulf states got chip access in exchange for anti-diversion commitments and matching investment in the US, so there the client pays. No case shows the US paying an ally cash or market access to adopt controls. This supports modelling the US offer as conditional retention of a status (the "slack" the ally keeps) rather than a transfer, and it is consistent with the Europe 2031 line "nothing in return".

**Counter-threats.** China's counter-threats are real, executed, and increasingly legal in form:

- Rare-earth controls (April and October 2025) with suspensions that expire on fixed dates (10 November 2026, now politically extended to 10 January 2027 but not yet re-gazetted).
- The Nexperia export ban, executed within four days of the Dutch order.
- Decree 834 (aimed at foreign firms that cut off Chinese counterparties) and Decree 835 (aimed at those who implement foreign extraterritorial measures).

Decree 835's first use was against the Commission's FSR probe of Nuctech, not against the US. China is therefore willing to aim its new legal tools at EU institutions, and an ASML servicing cutoff in China would fall squarely under Decree 834 Art. 15 and Decree 835's prohibition orders. Japan faced explicit retaliation threats in 2024 and Toyota lobbied over minerals. This is the third-party cost channel the model should include. Allies' counter-threats against the US are weak: Taiwan publicly rejected 50-50, and the Netherlands objects formally to MATCH's extraterritoriality, but neither ally has threatened a cost on the US.

**Outcomes.**

- *Netherlands.* It complied on substance with a lag, kept national form, and is now resisting only extraterritorial servicing mandates (MATCH).
- *Nexperia.* The Dutch government took the step the US wanted, China retaliated, and the Dutch suspended the order once the US-China deal at Busan (which also suspended the US Affiliates Rule) delivered relief. The resolution came through great-power bargaining over the ally's head.
- *Korea.* It accepted the loss of VEU and lives with annual licences.
- *Japan.* It aligned on sales in 2023 and has resisted servicing restrictions since 2024.
- *Taiwan.* It rejected the 50-50 demand.
- *Germany.* It reportedly declined to join on Zeiss (2024) and took no listed measure until the EU-wide entry of November 2025.
- *Gulf.* It traded security cooperation and investment for chip access.

The common pattern is that allies concede where the US controls a revocable status they value, and resist where compliance would transfer a domestic asset (Taiwan's fabs, servicing revenue) without a matching status reward. The US, for its part, has traded its own controls (the Affiliates Rule suspension) in bilateral deals with China. That supports giving the US a type that values transactional gains, which lowers the credibility of its alliance-wide denial demands.
