## Farrell and Newman (2019): the two conditions, with pages

All quotations are from the CC BY published version.

1. **Network topology, plus jurisdiction over the hubs.** "Asymmetric network structures create the potential for 'weaponized interdependence' ... states with political authority over the central nodes in the international networked structures through which money, goods, and information travel are uniquely positioned to impose costs on others" (p. 45). The authors explain variation "as a function of the structural topography of the network combined with domestic institutions and norms". The first condition: "only those states that have physical or legal jurisdiction over hub nodes will be able to exploit the benefits of weaponized interdependence" (p. 56). Hubs sit mostly in the United States, so only it, "the European Union [EU] and, increasingly, China" enjoy these benefits (pp. 56-57). Also on p. 56: "In some cases, states have sole jurisdiction over the key hub or hubs ... In others, the hubs may be scattered across two or more jurisdictions, obliging states to work together." "Where there are one or a few hubs, it becomes far easier for actors in control of these nodes to block or hamper access to the entire network."
2. **Domestic institutions.** "If states are to exploit hubs, they require appropriate legal and regulatory institutions. Depending on domestic configurations of power and state-society relations, they may lack coercive capacity" (p. 57). States can be "structurally positioned to shape hub behavior but lack the institutional resources" to use the panopticon or chokepoint effects (p. 57). National laws and norms can also forbid some kinds of weaponization; EU privacy law is the example (p. 57). Institutions are "sticky" and "cannot costlessly be transformed" (pp. 57-58).
3. **Combined expectation.** "States that have jurisdictional control over network hubs and enjoy sufficient institutional capacity will be able to deploy both panopticon and chokepoint effects ... Where control over key hubs is spread across a small number of states, these states may need to coordinate with one another" (p. 58).
4. **EU application.** "the EU has fragmented instruments of financial regulation, which means that it has not been able to exercise control over SWIFT, except when its member states have agreed unanimously on formal sanctions under prodding from the United States." The EU can still leverage control "in domains where it has both influence over key hubs and well-developed institutions (e.g., in the area of privacy)" (p. 75).
5. **Limits and responses.** Weaponization is hard where there are no network asymmetries. Targeted states may isolate themselves, "turn network effects back on their more powerful adversaries", or reshape networks (p. 76). The ZTE episode prompted China to rethink its reliance on foreign chipmakers (p. 77).

Caveat for the paper: F&N's evidence covers financial messaging and the internet. Supply chains appear only as "anecdotal evidence" (p. 46, n. 14) and in the conclusion (pp. 75, 77). Treating ASML as a hub in their sense is an extension; the paper should present it that way, not as their finding. The paper's reading in `06_extensions.tex` line 19 matches pp. 57-58 and 75. Europe meets the network condition for EUV but fails the institutional one, and the hub sits inside US-controlled networks.

## Answers to the four questions

**Jurisdictional reach and nesting.** F&N (p. 56, p. 58) say that hubs spread across jurisdictions force the states involved to coordinate. Kalyanpur and Newman show that jurisdiction is itself a policy choice: pooling jurisdiction can blunt a rival's extraterritorial pressure. Bown (WP pp. 27-28) documents the US use of the FDPR to reach foreign firms by controlling their inputs. That is the cheap instrument in Prop. "Nested chokepoints" (a). Allen (2022) adds the US-persons channel, which matters for servicing. Allen, Benson and Putnam (2023) show that export control is national within an EU Wassenaar frame, which is why the Dutch licence, not an EU decision, is the first gate. Chen and Evers (pp. 194-195) show that firms resist and route around controls, which is the $y$ against $f^F$ margin.

**EU capacity as a geoeconomic actor.** Meunier and Nicolaidis (2019) and Bauerle Danzman and Meunier (2024) both reject the view that the EU cannot act: it built a geoeconomic toolkit after 2017. F&N (p. 75) give the limiting case: where instruments are fragmented, the EU acts only by unanimity under US prodding. Together these support the paper's split. The EU has instruments such as the ACI and investment screening, but export control and emergency powers over firms stay national, and EU procedures are slow.

**How China coerces.** Lim and Ferguson (2022) is the direct cite: informal, deniable measures targeted at firms and sectors over a specific act. Gholz and Hughes (2021) show that even a dominant supplier's embargo can be absorbed through market adjustment, so $r$ may be smaller and more transient than supply shares suggest. Kastner (2016) is about influence through dependence, with clearer effects on economic than on political issues. It should support the promise side ($\omega$), not coercion. Meunier and Nicolaidis's phrase about China trying to "divide and rule through targeted investments" supports member-specific offers.

**Council consensus norms and blocking minorities.** About 81-82% of legislative acts passed with no recorded negative vote or abstention: 1994-2002 in Heisenberg's data, 1994-2006 in Häge's. Contested adoptions were therefore roughly one in five, varying from 3% to 26% a year in the EU-15 period (implied by Häge's 74-97% range). What this implies for the model:
- Häge shows that consensus is the by-product of negotiators forming blocking minorities before any vote. So the paper's object, the cheapest blocking coalition $\Phi(\cdot)$, is the right one even though recorded vetoes are rare.
- Novak shows that recorded votes understate opposition, because ministers avoid being seen to lose. The absence of contested votes does not mean no blocking coalition was available, and a public 2029-style split with named opponents is atypical.
- Häge predicts that lower thresholds, as under Lisbon, raise contestation. The 1994-2006 rates may overstate consensus today. `hagemann2017government` is a lead for later data, but its numbers have not been checked.

## Warnings and items for the authors

1. **ACI voting stage (not checked in this pass; verify before relying on it).** My understanding of Regulation (EU) 2023/2675 is as follows. The Council determines that coercion exists by qualified majority, on a Commission proposal. The response measures, however, are Commission implementing acts under the comitology examination procedure, and blocking them takes a qualified majority against, not a blocking minority. If this is right, Prop. "Nested chokepoints" (c) applies to the determination step only, and the 2029 arithmetic in `06_extensions.tex` line 19 should say so. Check against the Regulation's Arts. 5, 8 and 18 before revising.
2. **Council data are legislative and pre-Lisbon.** The Heisenberg and Häge rates cover legislative acts in 1994-2006. An ACI determination is a non-legislative implementing act adopted under the post-2014 double majority. Treat the base rates as indicative.
3. **Allen (2022) does not mention ASML or the Netherlands.** If a draft cites it for Dutch controls, switch to `allen2023japan`.
4. **Bown pagination.** Pinpoints above use WP 20-16 pages. The journal version (pp. 349-388) paginates differently.
5. **Year conventions.** Lim and Ferguson (online 2021, print 2022), Gholz and Hughes (online 2019, print 2021), Häge (online 2012, print 2013), and Hagemann et al. (online 2016, print 2017) are all given with their print-volume year in the bib file.
6. **Miller chapter numbers are unverified.** Cite the book without chapter pinpoints, or check the printed contents first.
