# Referee report (IPE, alliance politics, EU institutions, economic statecraft) and cut plan

Tag: ipe. Manuscript: "Owning the Chokepoint: ASML, Alliance Dependence, and the Limits of Europe's Leverage over Washington" (paper/main.tex). Read: sections 1 to 10 and Appendix C, the previous round's Referee 2 and Referee 3 reports, resolutions.md, style_brief.md, integration_notes.md, draft_issues.txt, and the gap memos in notes/review that integration_notes.md draws on. I did not edit the paper.

## 1. Summary and verdict

The paper asks why the Netherlands, home of the only maker of EUV lithography machines, has aligned its export controls with Washington since 2019 and received little for it, while still resisting some demands (servicing of tools in China, the extraterritorial reach of the MATCH Act). The answer is a three-player model. A security-dependent holder bargains with its patron while the patron's rival bids. The holder's leverage is bounded by its exit premium, and the patron's demands are bounded by the holder's slack, a budget that all of the patron's conditional threats share. Conditionality is a costly choice, and a patron makes its guarantee more conditional than deterrence needs only to create slack it can spend. A wasting slack gives the patron a preventive motive to demand control. The empirical section reads the Dutch record, Nexperia and nine comparison cases through the model and concludes that the record supports the jurisdictional channel and leaves the security channel untested.

This is a much better paper than the one Referees 2 and 3 saw. The real 2019 to 2026 record is now the puzzle. Security conditionality is micro-founded (a crisis-cost distribution, a loyalty premium, an access term and a public stake), graduated (core guarantee, posture and enablers, AI access, firm penalties) and chosen at an assurance cost. The record is read with real candour: the paper says in so many words that jurisdiction alone can make the premium negative and that the guarantee's conditionality on chip policy is untested. The coding of Supplement No. 4 as a threat left unexercised is right and is applied consistently. The institutional account of Article 12(2), EU001, the two ACI stages, the delegated-act route, Decree 835, the Blocking Statute and Nexperia is now accurate.

Three problems remain, and the cut is the occasion to fix them.

First, the paper's own headline has not caught up with its evidence. The abstract and the conclusion still put the conditional part of the US guarantee at the centre of what decides Europe's leverage, while the evidence section shows that the negative premium needs no security term, and the verified orders of magnitude in integration_notes.md show the AI instrument larger than the security term at central values. The paper should say what it found.

Second, the Council analysis answers the wrong institutional question. It is built on formal blocking arithmetic, while the verified record shows that the binding constraint is Commission gatekeeping, and that the Union's countermeasure practice is to arm and then suspend on a US concession. That record also overturns one policy recommendation: the paper proposes an ACI response measure as the standing rule that would make interruption of servicing credible, but the ACI's timing (urgent acts only to suspend, committee-gated escalation) is exactly the after-offer timing under which the paper's own Corollary 5 says interruption is never used.

Third, several verified late findings are missing: Dutch two-level politics, Japan as a case of China punishing a security act while sparing toolmakers, US control precedents that all sit on US soil, and the orders of magnitude. Each one sharpens an existing result, and together they need about as many words as the cut frees from duplicated material.

Recommendation: major revision, of a kind that is mostly rewriting and cutting rather than new theory. The paper clears the bar at IO in substance if the changes below are made. I am fairly confident they can be.

## 3. Front end

Framing. The introduction now leads with the scenario for one paragraph and turns to the record in the second. That is within the resolutions, but the first paragraph still carries the scenario's plot in detail (the holding company, the Tier 3 threat, the 2028 episode). Cut it to three sentences and let the second paragraph open with the record. The quotation "the only card Europe still holds" should be attributed to the scenario's summary page, where it appears; the narrative itself says "Europe has one last card to play".

The bidding paragraph of the introduction ends by setting ASML's China sales beside "a bidding account predicts a price". That sentence brings back, in the introduction, the very mapping both referees rejected: ASML's revenue in China is not China's credible offer for Dutch exit. The same coding reappears in the comparison table, where the Netherlands' "rival bid" cell is ASML's China revenue share. The fix is to say what a bidding account needs (a rival offer conditioned on exit) and that the record does not show one.

Security conditionality. The micro-foundation is good, and it is graduated. Two points remain. First, the corrected sufficient condition for Proposition 4(d) has not reached the main text: "when Washington would almost surely defend a loyal Europe" does not make the density nonincreasing on the relevant interval (the appendix drafter's counterexample is in draft_issues.txt). The wording appears three times. Second, the silicon shield is offered without the literature that doubts it; one sentence fixes this.

Chinese punishment. Setting untargeted punishment of alignment to zero is now the right call, and the verified Japan episode is the best evidence for it. After Prime Minister Takaichi's Taiwan statement of 7 November 2025, China listed eighty Japanese entities, none of which makes chipmaking equipment or photoresists. Beijing punished a security act, with a demonstration motive, and spared the holders whose inputs it needs. Washington paid Tokyo nothing for absorbing it, which is what Proposition 3(a)(v) leads us to expect of an ally with slack. This belongs in the model's paragraph on Chinese punishment and in the Japan row of the cases table.

Who decides. The gatekeeper, firm and Council remarks are useful, but they still do not feed back into the slack principle or the control results. I would not ask for a three-level game in a paper being cut by a third. I would ask for one paragraph stating which results carry over when The Hague's own premium replaces Europe's, and which do not (the Council matters only for US-facing measures; the guarantee's conditional part that Washington can withhold from the Netherlands alone is the posture and bilateral-enabler part).

Dutch two-level politics. This is absent, and it is the most natural IPE material for the paper's breakdown and self-binding results. The verified record: parliament adopted the Bamenga motion (13 October 2026, 110 to 40, signed by members of all three coalition parties) asking the cabinet to push for harmonised EU export control and joint European action when third countries press member states; it adopted the Piri motion (22 January 2026, 103 to 46) to keep the ACI in reserve over Greenland, against the caretaker cabinet's advice; within any right-leaning majority the first position is robust and the second is not. The cabinet "deelt in algemene zin de veiligheidszorgen" of partners and calls national security "doorslaggevend", while opposing MATCH's extraterritorial form. Public trust in the United States has collapsed (Pew, spring 2026). MATCH has stalled in procedure, and ASML-funded lobbying on it is large. In the model, the Bamenga vote is direct evidence that the sovereignty cost of Europeanised self-binding is low (Remark 1) and that a dual key carries a cost near that of full transfer. The cabinet's statements are evidence for the shared-threat rival (a large intrinsic cost of exit) that the paper's own rival table lists. The seat arithmetic tells us which way a government change would move the private sovereignty cost in Proposition 6. The current text uses none of this and says only that the cabinet is a minority coalition.

Scope. The list in Section 9.1 mixes four scope conditions with three maintained assumptions. Separate them. Condition (iii), a rival that bids, is met only weakly by every holder in the table: China values the tools and spares their makers, but I see no record of a Chinese offer conditioned on any holder's exit. That is worth saying, because it is one reason the premium is negative.

## 4. Empirics

Is the record read honestly? Mostly yes. Section 8.3 says the negative premium needs no security term and that the disputes since 2023 could not reveal conditionality even if it were large. The introduction says the same. The abstract does not state any empirical claim, and the conclusion says Europe's position "depends on how conditional the US guarantee is on chip policy", which is the claim the evidence section declines to make. Fix both.

The tolerated servicing hedge. The paper reads Washington's tolerance of servicing in China until 2026 as rationing of Europe's slack. That conflicts with Proposition 3(b), under which Washington polices a hedge with flow jurisdiction whenever a jurisdictional block is credible, without spending slack. The reading needs one more step: before MATCH there was no credible jurisdictional block on servicing done by non-US persons in China (and after Decrees 834 and 835, stopping it carries a Chinese counter-threat), so the only route was linkage, which would have spent slack on a low-value demand. MATCH is then the stake that would make jurisdiction credible. Said this way, the servicing episode becomes the paper's cleanest illustration of Proposition 3(b) rather than a loose fit.

Shared interest. The paper dismisses the shared-interest account on timing: a holder aligning for its own reasons "need not wait for US rules". It would wait if unilateral controls handed sales to Japanese competitors. That is the coordination problem Farrell and Newman attach to hubs spread across jurisdictions, and it fits the timing as well as coercion does. The Dutch cabinet's own statements (security concerns shared in general terms; national security decisive) are evidence for a large intrinsic cost of exit. The honest statement is that timing does not separate the accounts and that the Woo records described in Section 8.5 could.

Coding rules. The line between a transfer and a threat left unexercised is drawn at Washington's ex post optimal provision to an aligned ally. That is the right line in the model, but a coder cannot observe it. Add observable criteria: a benefit is baseline provision when it is extended to allies that took no comparable step, when it is justified by the recipient's own capacity or alliance status, and when it is revocable by rule; it is a transfer when it is created in response to the holder's step, withheld from comparable allies and framed as compensation. Supplement No. 4 meets all three baseline criteria, which makes the coding transparent rather than asserted.

The comparison table. It shows two things clearly: no holder was paid in cash, and the currency of US management is revocable status; and resistance clusters on the installed base and on signed deals. Both fit the model. But the table cannot test the threshold, because every in-scope holder has, on the paper's reading, a nonpositive premium. The paper should say so and name the one positive-premium contrast it already discusses (Manas). Two rows are outside the class the table is about. The UAE row reverses the roles (Washington holds the chokepoint) and the Germany and Spain 2026 row is not a holder case; both are already discussed in the text and should leave the table. The Taiwan row should become a row on control rights, using the verified record: Washington sought relocation (TSMC's US commitment rising to USD 165 billion) rather than equity, and the control rights it has taken (MP Materials, US Steel, Intel) all attach to assets on US soil, where enforceability does not depend on a foreign government. That is direct support for the enforceability logic of Corollary 3(c), and it says that the scenario's controlling vote in a Dutch company has no precedent. The Japan row should run to 2026 and include the Takaichi episode. The Netherlands row should stop coding ASML's China revenue as a rival bid.

Orders of magnitude. Dropping the break-even figure was right, but the paper now gives the reader no sense of which terms in the premium are large. integration_notes.md asks for orders of magnitude, and the verified memo supplies them: the AI instrument from about EUR 4 billion to 130 billion a year depending on whether European AI gains are limited by adoption or by frontier capability; the conditional part of the core guarantee about EUR 12.5 billion a year at a conditionality of 0.10 and a stake of EUR 125 billion; China's input lever from about EUR 3 billion to 77 billion. One caution the drafters must respect. The memo's headline ("if alpha exceeds about EUR 12bn a year, Europe has no leverage ... and the security guarantee is not what decides that") was computed with China's input lever entering the premium as a punishment of alignment. The v2 model sets that punishment to zero and treats China's lever as counter-threats on concessions. In the paper's own model, therefore, the honest statement is a comparison of conditional parts (the AI instrument is several times larger than the security term at central values), with China's lever bounding counter-threats and with no measured value of China's offer. My replacement text below is written that way.

Nexperia. The narrative is careful and now sits inside the model with its requirements stated. It is also long; the cut below keeps the reading and drops procedural detail.

## 5. Institutional accuracy

- Article 12(2) of Regulation 2021/821: correct. Licences for ASML's China-bound shipments are Dutch decisions; other member states have non-binding consultation rights.
- EU001 and Article 12(7): correct. EUV and the most advanced immersion tools ship to US fabs under EU001; a member state may bar an exporter only for doubts about its ability to comply. The background correctly notes that tools left on the Dutch national list still need a Dutch licence to leave the EU. Missing is the consequence the eu_inst strand flags as an inference: moving the advanced immersion tools to Annex I by Delegated Regulation 2025/2003 made the China-facing control harder to reverse and also removed a unilateral Dutch licence over US-bound shipments of those tools. That bears on self-binding (Remark 1) and on Europe's hold-up options, and should be stated, hedged as our reading.
- ACI: correct as written (Council determination by qualified majority within eight to ten weeks; response measures as Commission implementing acts under the examination procedure with a no-opinion clause; in the appeal committee the Commission may adopt unless a qualified majority votes against; recital 85). What is missing is Article 12(5) (urgent, immediately applicable acts only to suspend or amend response measures) and Statement C/2023/1341, which repeats the recital 85 commitment. These are what make escalation slow and de-escalation fast.
- Delegated-act route: correct (Article 17(2), urgency under Article 19, Council objection by reinforced qualified majority, so eight members or more than 35 percent of population keep the act in force; the Netherlands must still deny the licences).
- Decree 835: substantively correct (Malicious Entity List, prohibition orders, private suits, fines; first application against the Commission's FSR probe of Nuctech on 15 May 2026). It is still cited to a law-firm alert, and refs.bib carries two keys for the same alert (reedsmith2026decree and reedsmith2026decree835). Name the instrument by its title, cite the official text if it can be obtained, and delete the duplicate key.
- Blocking Statute: correct (annex lists US sanctions on Cuba and Iran, none on export controls; Bank Melli on proportionality). The paragraph is longer than the point needs.
- Nexperia: correct and properly hedged (Goods Availability Act order of 30 September 2025, Enterprise Chamber, Chinese export ban, Busan suspension of the Affiliates Rule, Dutch suspension on 19 November). The open TODO on the Enterprise Chamber sentence can be closed: kst32637_717 is now in refs.bib.
- The Council's revealed record: absent. Member states gave positive committee opinions to US-facing trade countermeasure packages in April and July 2025; each was suspended after a US move (the 90-day tariff pause, the Turnberry agreement, the withdrawal of the Greenland tariff threat), and the July 2026 suspension has no end date. These were rebalancing measures voted in the Trade Barriers Committee, not ACI measures, and the text must say so; they follow the same comitology logic. Hungary changed government on 9 May 2026, so the sentence treating Hungary's support as doubtful is out of date.

## 8. Required changes, ranked, with anchors and fixes

1. [essential] 09_implications.tex, anchor "A response measure under the Anti-Coercion Instrument". The paper recommends an ACI response measure as the standing rule that makes interruption credible. Under the ACI, escalation runs through committees and the Commission's commitment to avoid any predominant position, while urgent acts may only suspend or amend measures (Art. 12(5)); the 2025 to 2026 record is arm then suspend on a US concession. An interruption the Commission can lift after any US offer has the timing of Corollary 5(b), under which interruption is never used. Replacement for the two sentences beginning at the anchor:

   "The Anti-Coercion Instrument is a poor vehicle for such a rule \citep{eu2023aci}. Response measures pass through the examination and appeal committees, the Commission has committed itself to avoid going against any predominant position among member states \citep{commission2023acistatement}, and urgent, immediately applicable acts may only suspend or amend measures (Art.~12(5)). Escalation is slow and gated by committees. De-escalation is fast and in the Commission's hands. An interruption adopted this way could be lifted after any US offer, which is the timing of \cref{cor:interrupt}(b), under which interruption is never used. A credible rule would need a legal basis that the Commission cannot suspend on a US concession, and on our reading the Union's current instruments offer none. Even such a rule helps only for servicing Washington cannot compel, a small share for US-sited fabs (\cref{sec:holdup})."

2. [essential] 07_decide.tex, anchor "Informal norms and procedural defaults pull the outcome away from the formal arithmetic, in opposite directions." The Council section rests on formal blocking arithmetic; the binding constraint is Commission gatekeeping, and the revealed record (positive opinions on US-facing packages, each suspended after a US move) is missing. Move Remark 4 and the Council table to the appendix and replace this paragraph and the remark with:

   "With enforceable vote-contingent payments, Washington defeats a countermeasure by buying the cheapest blocking minority before the Council's determination. Members whose defence depends most on US posture join at no cost but cannot block alone (\cref{app:proofs} states the result and gives the arithmetic). The formal threshold is not what binds. The Commission has committed itself to avoid going against any predominant position among member states \citep{eu2023aci, commission2023acistatement}, so a measure that large states oppose is unlikely to be tabled. The Union's practice toward Washington shows the pattern. Member states gave positive committee opinions to commercial rebalancing measures against US goods in April and July 2025, voted in the Trade Barriers Committee rather than under the Anti-Coercion Instrument. Each package was suspended after a US move: the tariff pause of April 2025, the Turnberry agreement of July 2025 and the withdrawal of the Greenland tariff threat in January 2026. The suspension of July 2026 has no end date \citep{commission2025suspension786, commission2025rebalancing1564, commission2025suspension1727, commission2026suspension1893}. The Union arms its countermeasures and suspends them on a US concession."

   Keep one sentence on the October 2024 EV vote only if space allows. Replace "Hungary's support is doubtful" (which moves with the table) with "Hungary changed government on 9 May 2026, so the group is not a fixed bloc."

3. [essential] 10_conclusion.tex, anchor "Whether it meets the third condition depends on how conditional the US guarantee is on chip policy". This contradicts Section 8.3, which shows the premium negative without any security term. Replacement for that sentence:

   "Whether it meets the third condition turns first on Washington's jurisdiction over ASML's inputs and staff, which on the record keeps the premium negative without any security term, and then on the AI instrument, which on our orders of magnitude is larger than the conditional part of the guarantee (\cref{sec:record}). How conditional the guarantee is on chip policy is the quantity our evidence measures least well."

4. [essential] 00_abstract.tex, anchors "We verify each result by brute-force computation." and "Europe's leverage over Washington therefore depends on the conditional part of its dependence and on China's credible promises." The abstract states no empirical finding and reads as if security conditionality were the decisive term. Delete the first sentence (move the claim to the introduction or the title-page note). Replace the second with:

   "The record from 2019 to 2026 fits a negative exit premium produced by US jurisdiction over ASML's inputs and staff. Whether the US guarantee is conditional on chip policy remains untested."

5. [essential] 08_evidence.tex, anchor "The negative premium needs no security term." No quantities remain after the calibration was dropped. Insert after the paragraph that begins with the anchor:

   "\paragraph{Orders of magnitude.} The terms of \cref{eq:Q} can be bounded, not estimated, and we report orders of magnitude in euros a year. Denying Europe US frontier AI would cost it roughly EUR 4 billion to 130 billion: the low end if European gains from AI are limited by adoption, the high end if they are limited by frontier capability \citep{acemoglu2024simple, filippucci2025g7, epoch2026eci}.
