# Fact check: historical cases and the EU toolbox (tag: history)

Checked 28 October 2026. Read first: `notes/review/eu_inst.md`, `asml_facts.md`, `security.md`, `china_dep.md`, `e2031.md`, and `notes/v2/lit_security.md`.

**How the checking was done.** The historical items rest on the full TEI text of the relevant *Foreign Relations of the United States* (FRUS) volumes. These came from the Office of the Historian's public repository (github.com/HistoryAtState/frus) and were parsed document by document. Each document is cited by its history.state.gov URL.

The other sources were fetched directly:

- EU legal texts, by CELEX number, through the Publications Office (Cellar);
- Dutch statutes from wetten.overheid.nl;
- ASML's FY2025 Form 20-F and its Exhibit 8.1 from SEC EDGAR;
- Hansard (via api.parliament.uk);
- the NATO declaration on nato.int;
- 15 CFR 772.1 from eCFR;
- the Bundesbank's archive page.

Five WebSearch calls were used, for Spain at The Hague, the Trump quote, the October 2024 BEV vote, the date of the Blessing letter and the 1977 German moratorium. Four sources were blocked or restricted: the IAEA site (INFCIRC/237), the Bureau Toetsing Investeringen site, the archive.org lending copies of Mastanduno (1992) and Gavin (2004), and Google Books.

Status key: **verified** means the fact matches the cited primary or high-quality source. **Corrected** means the premise in the task was wrong and the row gives the right version. **Unverified** means no adequate source was found, and the fact should not go in the paper as stated.

Existing keys reused, with no new entry written: `gavin2004gold`, `zimmermann2002money`, `gray2012commercial` (all in `notes/v2/bib_security.bib`), `mastanduno1992economic` and `asml2026annual` (both in `paper/refs.bib`). The key `eu2023aci` already appears in `notes/review/e2031.md`, but its note there says the article text was not retrieved. The bib file below re-issues it under the same key with the retrieved text and the official citation. Do not add a second key for that regulation.

## Synthesis: what the cases imply for the model

| Case | Instrument used | Linkage invoked? | Compensation? | Counter-threat? | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Offset, 1966-67 | US threat to cut troops in Germany, backed by Congress (Mansfield resolution) | Yes, explicitly. Johnson tied troop reductions to German non-payment (1.5) | Partial and indirect. The US accepted bond purchases and monetary cooperation instead of matching military purchases, and paid the UK £7 million in extra orders to close the trilateral deal (1.11, 1.14) | Germany's latent lever, conversion of dollars into gold, was given up rather than used (1.12, 1.13). Erhard warned that a successor might be less loyal (1.4) | Germany paid in monetary coin (a $500 million bond purchase and the gold pledge); the US still redeployed up to 35,000 under dual-basing; Erhard's government fell |
| CoCom differential, 1957 | UK unilateral defection from a stricter multilateral control | No. The US did not tie the issue to security | The US made concessions to keep a smaller differential, which failed (2.1) | Yes: credible unilateral exit backed by a majority of members (12 of 16) | Differential abolished. | FRG-Brazil, 1975-78 | High-level US pressure, "major crisis" warning, letter from Carter | Not by Washington explicitly. **1. Allies concede on the forward margin and keep sunk deals.** In the Brazil case Bonn gave up future reprocessing transfers but not the signed contract. In 1957 the UK conceded nothing. In 1967 Germany kept most US troops in place but paid in a different currency. In the ASML game, The Hague's natural concession is on new licences rather than the installed base. This fits the model's split between new sales and servicing. (The Dutch non-renewal of DUVi servicing licences in China at end-2024, recorded in `security.md`, cut the other way, so the pattern is a prior, not a law.)

**2. Explicit security linkage worked when the ally depended on the patron and had no exit.** Johnson's linkage in 1966-67 extracted a monetary concession. Germany surrendered its own counter-lever, gold conversion, which the Bundesbank later regretted, citing fear of troop withdrawal. Without such linkage, the UK in 1957 defected at no cost and Washington chose not to sanction allies (NSC 5704/2). Spain in 2025 held out against an explicit trade-for-defence threat that Washington could not easily carry out against a single EU member. The model should make the credibility of linkage depend on (a) the ally's security dependence and (b) whether the punishment instrument can be aimed at the ally alone.

**3. Side payments go to third parties and come in non-budget currencies.** In 1967 the US paid the UK, and in 1977 it offered Brazil, not Bonn, a fuel guarantee. Germany paid through the Bundesbank, not the budget. For the model, transfers need not run between the two principal bargainers. The US can buy off the pivot (here, a large Council member or the Dutch government) with instruments outside the dispute.

**4. The domestic cost of conceding can be decisive.** Erhard's coalition broke weeks after the September 1966 visit, and contemporaries tied the collapse to the offset dispute. A European government that concedes ASML leverage under pressure faces a comparable audience cost. This supports a government-survival term in the European payoff.

**5. The EU's formal counter-coercion tools are slow, collective and weakly enforced:**

- The ACI requires a positive Council determination by QMV within about 8 to 10 weeks after an examination of normally up to 4 months. Measures need a positive committee opinion, with only a discretionary "may adopt" in the appeal committee.
- The Blocking Statute's Annex covers US sanctions on Cuba and Iran, not US export controls, so it does not reach the FDPR without a new delegated act.
- *Bank Melli* shows that enforcement runs through national courts and member-state penalties, and is bounded by proportionality toward firms with large US exposure (Telekom earned about half its group turnover in the US, per the referring court). A firm like ASML, with 18.7% of staff and US-incorporated subsidiaries in the US, would be in the same position.
- **Model implication:** treat European counter-threats executed through firms as low-credibility. Only state-executed denial (licensing) is credible.

**6. Default rules decide outcomes.** The BEV duties passed with only 10 supporting states because comitology for trade defence adopts unless a qualified majority opposes. The ACI deliberately reverses this at committee stage (no opinion means no adoption). The delegated-act route under Reg. 2021/821 (for example, removing the US from EU001) returns to a pro-Commission default, since objection needs reinforced QMV. The paper should not cite the BEV vote as evidence that an ACI measure against the US could pass with a minority. It shows that the Commission can act when the procedure puts the burden on opponents.

**7. Ownership is triple-locked.** Any US move to take control of ASML would face:

- the preference-share foundation (firm level, against unagreed bids);
- Vifo screening by the Dutch minister, triggered at 10% of voting rights because semiconductor equipment is "very sensitive" technology, whatever the acquirer's nationality;
- the Commission's FSR review if third-country financial contributions exceed €50 million.

This makes the scenario's 2031 ownership ultimatum a multi-veto game. The Netherlands holds the decisive formal screen.

**8. ASML's US footprint is a hostage that points both ways.** The US campus in Wilton, the light-source plant in San Diego (Cymer), and field offices beside every major US fab are operated by US-law entities and staffed by US persons. A European threat to withhold servicing of US fabs would partly have to be executed through people and entities under US jurisdiction. That is our inference, since the employing entity of US field service is unverified (7.6), and it lowers the threat's credibility further.
