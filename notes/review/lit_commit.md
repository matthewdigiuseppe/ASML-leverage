## Synthesis (edited)

The credibility literature answers the question of where ASML leverage comes from more sharply than the Europe 2031 epilogue does. The epilogue locates leverage in indispensability. Bargaining theory says indispensability is neither necessary nor sufficient. Under the outside option principle (Shaked and Sutton 1984; Binmore, Shaked and Sutton 1989; Muthoo 1999), a threat to walk away moves the split only when the alternative beats what a player would get by continuing to bargain. Menu-auction and common-agency models (Bernheim and Whinston 1986; Grossman and Helpman 1994) make a parallel point about rent. What a principal pays the agent is pinned down by what the agent would do without that principal's offer. So a contested agent collects roughly what the losing principal would pay, not what the winner stands to lose. Bulow and Rogoff (1989) make the point most bluntly for sovereign debt: the ability to credibly threaten more draconian penalties may be of no benefit to lenders. For ASML, the US stake in the chokepoint adds nothing to Europe's rent unless Europe can credibly interrupt supply while bargaining, which would turn that stake into an inside option. We build the model around this distinction. Indispensability is treated as a claim about inside options, and that claim has to pass a sequential-rationality test.

That test is where Europe fails. Schelling (1960) showed that bargaining power can rest on the power to bind oneself. Schelling (1966) added that compellence is harder than deterrence, because the target must act visibly and under a deadline. The scenario's 2029 Anti-Coercion Instrument episode is compellence: an attempt to reverse the inference cap and win Tier 1 status.

The procedure it runs through has two gates, and only the first defaults to inaction:

1. **Determination.** Under Regulation (EU) 2023/2675, the Council must find by qualified majority, on a Commission proposal, that coercion exists (Art. 5). Without that majority nothing further happens.
2. **Response measures.** These are Commission implementing acts under the examination procedure (Arts. 8(1) and 18(2)). A committee "no opinion" blocks adoption at this stage. But Article 18(2) then invokes Article 5(4), third subparagraph, of Regulation 182/2011. That lets the chair send the draft to the appeal committee, and under Article 6(3) of the same regulation the Commission may adopt if the appeal committee also gives no opinion. Once coercion has been determined, blocking a countermeasure therefore takes a qualified majority against it.

Meunier (2000) argues that restrictive EU rules strengthen the Union in conservative negotiations (defending the status quo) and weaken it in reformist ones. We adopt a version of this as a central proposition: EU institutions are an asymmetric commitment technology, and the asymmetry sits mainly in the Article 5 determination. They make Europe's promises to Washington credible and make its threats against Washington hard to start. The ACI also binds Europe's promises mechanically. Article 12(2) obliges the Commission to suspend response measures for as long as the third country suspends its coercion, so Washington can switch off a European countermeasure by pausing its own.

Dal Bó (2007) supplies the mechanism for 2029: an outsider who can reward decisive votes differently can manipulate a committee at no cost. The scenario's own vote arithmetic shows that the buying has to go further than the text says. The Council's 2026 population figures give the following:

- The states the scenario names as opposed (the Netherlands, Ireland, Poland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania) hold 14.9 per cent of EU population.
- Italy, which abstains, holds 13.2 per cent.
- Together these seven states hold 28.1 per cent. A blocking minority needs 35 per cent of population or 13 states.

If the other twenty states voted in favour, the measure would pass. It fails only if unnamed states also withheld support. That is what Groseclose and Snyder (1996) lead us to expect when a counter-bidder exists, because the cheapest secure coalition is larger than the minimal one.

Washington has already designed this kind of discrimination. The January 2025 AI Diffusion rule put ten EU states in its favored group. Those ten hold 70.5 per cent of EU population but fall short of the 15 states a qualified majority needs. It left seventeen states outside, including Poland and the Baltic states. BIS announced the rule's rescission and stopped enforcing it on 13 May 2025, two days before the compliance date. It is a precedent for design, not an instrument in force. Segal (1999) and Segal and Whinston (2000) generalize the logic to divide-and-conquer contracting, which also helps explain why Japan and Korea stay out of a middle-power coalition.

The second pillar is dynamic. ASML is a wasting asset on two clocks. Chinese indigenization erodes the rival bid, and US stockpiling of tools and maintenance know-how erodes Europe's inside option. Fearon's unpublished paper on bargaining over objects that influence future bargaining power (draft dated October 1996) fits better than the preventive-war models of Fearon (1995) and Powell (2006). In 2031 the object being bargained over is control of the asset that generates leverage. Fearon shows that the game has a unique subgame perfect equilibrium. Bargaining is efficient when the map from holdings to the odds of winning is continuous, with gradual appeasement ("salami tactics") when resources can be transferred continuously. Bargaining can break down when that map is discontinuous.

That yields two predictions we should formalize:

- **Divisible concessions produce salami tactics.** This fits the run of Dutch extensions. DUV immersion systems needed a Dutch export licence before 2025, metrology and inspection systems were added on 15 January 2025, and in the scenario the remaining DUVi exports and servicing go in 2028.
- **A lumpy transfer of a controlling vote is a discontinuity that can cause breakdown.**

Krainin (2017) adds that slow but persistent shifts are enough, and that the war may come only after a delay. That fits an ultimatum in 2031 after years of gradual erosion. Chadefaux (2011) pushes against inevitability. Under complete information, shifts in power never lead to war when states can negotiate over the determinants of power. War then requires third parties, domestic politics or fungibility problems. All three are present here: China, Dutch sovereignty costs and lumpy control rights.

Even so, Chadefaux tells us the model should let Europe offer a divisible substitute for control, traded for renewable compute access. The obvious candidate is binding, Europeanized export control, and current law does not yet supply it. Commission Delegated Regulation 2025/2003 (in force 15 November 2025) put a DUV immersion entry (3B501.f) on the EU dual-use list. But individual licences are still granted by the competent authority of the member state where the exporter is established (Regulation 2021/821, Art. 12(2)), so The Hague still decides ASML's licences. A substitute that Washington could rely on would have to move the licensing decision itself, or bind it by rule.

Schwarz and Sonin (2008) point the same way: without commitment, a continuous stream of transfers appeases better than a lump sum. Europe should therefore seek renewable tier status rather than a one-off capital injection. The US demand for a one-off transfer of control is exactly the lump sum that solves America's commitment problem at Europe's expense.

The commitment problem hurts Europe twice. First, as a durable-goods monopolist (Coase 1972; Bulow 1982), Europe cannot commit not to sell later. Second, it cannot commit to the selling mechanism that would let it charge a threatened non-buyer. In Jehiel, Moldovanu and Stacchetti (1996), the seller extracts payments even from agents who do not get the object, and it can do so because it commits to the auction.

Bulow's renting remedy has a direct analogue. Leverage persists in servicing and installed-base management, which are flows, rather than in machine sales, which are stocks. That is why the scenario's US claim to have acquired maintenance know-how through Taiwanese partners is the decisive move. The hold-up literature (Klein, Crawford and Alchian 1978; Grossman and Hart 1986) predicts the US response. When appropriable quasi-rents are large and contracts cannot be enforced, the exposed party integrates and takes the residual control rights. Williamson's (1983) hostages are Europe's best counter, because reciprocal exposure is what supports exchange when commitments are otherwise not credible.

Delegation offers a partial remedy, and the central-bank literature carries over directly. Rogoff (1985) and Lohmann (1992) imply that an EU export authority with sovereignty-weighted preferences and a costly override would make threats credible while keeping an escape clause for security emergencies. That clause matters because a crisis that needs US defense is the shock most likely to trigger an override. Keefer and Stasavage (2003) warn that delegation adds credibility only when several veto players must agree to reverse it, and the effect grows with their polarization.

The ACI already contains half of this design. Terminating response measures also runs through the examination procedure (Art. 12(4)), so a measure is sticky once adopted, although the Commission can suspend or amend it on urgent grounds for up to two months (Art. 12(5)). What the ACI lacks is the other half: the Article 5 determination requires a qualified majority to start. A delegation scheme must therefore make the threat the agent's default, so that a qualified majority is needed to stop it rather than to start it.

Where the literature disagrees, we take sides.

**The Schelling conjecture.** Tarar (2001) shows that a generalized version holds when one side is highly constrained and the other is not. With incomplete information, though, there is an equilibrium in which delay leaves both sides worse off than if neither were constrained. Leventoğlu and Tarar (2005) show that when both sides face similar costs of backing down, public demands become a prisoner's dilemma. The 2031 ultimatum, with a public US Tier 3 threat and a European refusal, fits the pessimistic case.

**Signaling.** Slantchev (2005) shows that when a costly move also raises one's own probability of winning, bluffing is possible in equilibrium. US stockpiling is therefore not a clean signal of the kind in Fearon (1997), where leaders never bluff.

**Wars of attrition.** Drawing on Maynard Smith (1974), Fudenberg and Tirole (1986), Alesina and Drazen (1991) and Bliss and Nalebuff (1984), we read 2031 as two nested attrition games. In the first, the US and Europe fight over who goes without first. In the second, the Netherlands and the other member states fight over who pays for confrontation. The second game does more damage.

**Indispensability.** Wagner (1988) warns against the whole indispensability framing. Asymmetric dependence yields influence only if trading economic resources for political concessions leaves both sides better off than bargaining over the economic gains alone. Linkage to defense meets Wagner's condition for the United States far more easily than linkage to frontier AI does for Europe.

## Modeling implications

1. **Separate outside options from inside options.** China's bid is an outside option and moves Europe's rent only when it binds (the outside option principle; menu auctions). The US stake V enters Europe's payoff only through an inside option: a sequentially rational interruption of ASML supply or servicing during bargaining. Add an explicit interruption move with a cost to Europe, and state the result in the form of Bulow and Rogoff (1989): extra threat severity is worthless unless the threat binds.

2. **Common agency with credibility constraints.** Model the alignment contest as common agency (Bernheim-Whinston; Grossman-Helpman) over the menu A, H, P. Add credibility constraints on negative contributions: US security conditionality, Tier 3 and the FDPR, and Chinese rare-earth coercion. In Grossman-Helpman contributions are paid on the equilibrium path; here threats have to be ex post rational.

3. **Two clocks.** The rival bid decays as China indigenizes (W_t = m^t W). The US cost of interruption falls with stockpiling and acquired maintenance know-how (s_t). Derive Europe's leverage window and the US preventive window, and use Krainin's (2017) multi-period condition so that slow shifts can trigger a delayed ultimatum.

4. **The 2031 demand as bargaining over an object that shapes future bargaining power** (Fearon 1996). A majority-vote transfer of control is a discontinuity that can cause breakdown. Add a divisible alternative (a veto limited to China-bound sales, or EU-level licensing in exchange for renewable compute access) and show when it restores efficient bargaining (Chadefaux 2011). Under current law the November 2025 EU listing does not provide this, because licensing stays national. The divisible alternative is therefore a reform, not a status-quo option.

5. **Stream versus lump-sum transfers** (Schwarz and Sonin 2008). Europe should demand renewable tier status, a stream. The US prefers a lump sum: a capital injection plus control. Predict which form each side proposes and when lump sums fail to be self-enforcing.

6. **Flow versus stock.** ASML's lever has two parts, following Bulow (1982) and Coase (1972). The flow is installed-base servicing, parts, software and upgrades, which work like rental. The stock is new tool sales. US acquisition of servicing know-how turns flow leverage into stock leverage that the US has already bought. The key parameter is how long the US fleet can run without ASML servicing.

7. **The ACI as a two-gate procedure.**
   - Gate 1 (Art. 5) needs a qualified majority in favour in the Council, with inaction as the reversion point.
   - Gate 2 (Arts. 8 and 18) needs, after an appeal-committee referral, only the absence of a qualified majority against.

   Use Dal Bó (2007) pivot-contingent offers at each gate. The US's cheapest block is at gate 1 (a blocking minority), and blocking at gate 2 requires a qualified majority against. Comparative statics: automaticity or reverse QMV at gate 1 raises compellent leverage, while restrictive rules raise deterrent leverage (Meunier 2000). Include Art. 12(2): a US suspension of the coercive measure mechanically suspends the EU response, which gives the US a cheap way to defuse a measure and reimpose pressure later.

8. **Calibrate the blocking coalition.** The scenario's named blockers plus Italy hold 28.06% of 2026 population and 7 states, short of the 35% or 13 states a blocking minority needs. In the model the US must buy at least one more large state or six more small ones. With China as a counter-bidder, Groseclose and Snyder (1996) predict that the US buys more than the minimal blocking minority.

9. **Holder's paradox as a volunteer's dilemma.** The Netherlands bears the private cost of carrying out threats, while the gains are an EU public good. Embed an intra-EU war of attrition (Bliss-Nalebuff; Alesina-Drazen). Show that delay rises with the number of members and with cost asymmetry, and that the US targets the holder (Tier 1 for the Netherlands in the scenario).

10. **Delegation stage.** An EU export authority with preference weight b on leverage and an override cost c (Rogoff; Lohmann). Credibility rises with the number of veto players needed to override (Keefer-Stasavage). The cost is lost flexibility when a security shock makes US defense pivotal. This yields a trade-off: delegation that makes threats credible weakens promises to stay Atlantic. The ACI's Art. 12(4) already makes termination sticky, so the binding constraint is the start rule at gate 1.

11. **Stockpiling as a hybrid signal and 2031 as attrition.** US stockpiling both sinks costs and raises the US disagreement payoff (Slantchev 2005), so bluffing about self-sufficiency is possible. Model 2031 as a war of attrition with private costs (Fudenberg-Tirole; Ponsatí-Sákovics 1995). Breakdown (Tier 3 carried out while Europe keeps ASML) occurs with positive probability. With outside options on both sides, Ponsatí and Sákovics (1998) imply a continuum of equilibria, including some with delay, so equilibrium selection is substantive.

12. **Pre-negotiation public commitment** (Leventoğlu-Tarar 2005). If both sides commit publicly, the result is a prisoner's dilemma and impasse, and secret bargaining improves welfare. Public US tier rules raise US audience costs (m_ai) and lower bargaining efficiency.

13. **Wagner's (1988) linkage condition.** Linking ASML to AI access or security has value only if it beats separate bargaining for both sides. Because Europe's defense dependence is large, linkage pays mainly for the US. Report when linkage to China (rare earths, legacy chips) turns Europe's China dependence into a tax on the alliance rather than a source of rent.

14. **Compellence versus deterrence through the status quo.** Europe's leverage is higher when it defends European ownership against a US demand than when it uses ASML to extract Tier 1 (Schelling 1966).

15. **Exogenous deadlines** (Fershtman-Seidmann 1993). The US stay of the Affiliates Rule runs to 9 November 2026 and China's suspension (MOFCOM Announcement 70) to 10 November 2026. Add periodic renewals of servicing licences. These concentrate concessions at the deadline. Brinkmanship through probabilistic degradation of servicing (Schelling 1960; Schwarz-Sonin) is Europe's only threat that does not require ex post rationality.

## Open questions

1. **Search-dependent checks.** The WebSearch budget was exhausted before this strand began. A follow-up with search should confirm legal commentary on the ACI appeal-committee route (for example the Commission's own Q&A). Our reading of Art. 18(2) with Arts. 5(4) and 6(3) of Regulation 182/2011 comes from the legal texts alone.

2. **Works not verified and therefore not cited:** Crawford (2003) *Pivotal Deterrence*, Slantchev (2011) *Military Threats*, Slantchev (2003) APSR on the principle of convergence, Powell (1988) APSR on nuclear brinkmanship, Spaniel (2019) *Bargaining over the Bomb*, and Lake (2009) *Hierarchy*. Crawford's pivotal deterrence is especially relevant to a weak third party holding leverage over two rivals.

3. **Metadata and characterizations to confirm by hand:**
   - Morrow (1991) end page 933 comes from standard citation; Crossref and Semantic Scholar give only the start page.
   - The characterizations of Fershtman and Seidmann (1993), Lohmann (1992), Coase (1972), Klein, Crawford and Alchian (1978) and Bliss and Nalebuff (1984) rest on the titles and on our knowledge of the papers, because no abstract was retrievable.
   - Meunier's directional claim (conservative versus reformist) comes from the article's argument; the abstract states only that the distinction matters.
   - The page on which Vernon (1971) uses "obsolescing bargain" was not checked.

4. **Do the November 2026 deadlines pass?** The US stay runs to 9 November 2026 and the Chinese suspension to 10 November 2026. No BIS Federal Register document extending the stay had appeared by 27 October 2026. A sibling strand reports a 28 September 2026 MOFCOM statement extending the Kuala Lumpur arrangement to 10 January 2027, without mention of export controls; this strand did not verify it.

5. **AI Diffusion rescission.** BIS announced the rescission on 13 May 2025. Whether a formal rescission or replacement rule has since been published in the Federal Register was not checked here.

6. **US fleet endurance.** What is the empirical half-life of US and TSMC EUV fleet operation without ASML field engineers, parts and software updates? This parameter decides whether the scenario's US stockpile claim is credible.

7. **Legal feasibility of delegation.** Article 12(2) of Regulation 2021/821 assigns licensing to the member state of establishment, and Article 207 TFEU and member-state security competence bound what the Union can take over. Could export and servicing decisions on ASML be moved to an EU-level body with automatic triggers?

8. **ASML as a player.** Should ASML be modeled with preferences distinct from the Dutch state (shareholders, US-origin suppliers such as the San Diego light-source unit, and its servicing workforce in China)?

9. **Other models of a depreciating chokepoint.** Is there formal work on economic coercion with a depreciating chokepoint beyond Axelrod and Iliev (2014), for example on export controls as wasting assets or on sanctions timing? A targeted search is needed.

10. **Loaded-but-suspended retaliation.** Does the EU practice of adopting rebalancing measures against the US and then suspending them repeatedly (2025/1564, 2025/1727, 2026/295, 2026/1893) raise or lower the credibility of future EU threats? Our reading is that it lowers it, in line with Kydland and Prescott's time inconsistency, but this is an empirical question.

11. **The scenario's arithmetic.** The scenario does not name enough states to block a qualified majority. In the model, should we fill the gap with unnamed states (Spain alone would be enough at 10.86%), or treat the authors' vote as a modeling simplification?
