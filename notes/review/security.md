## Synthesis (edited)

Our reading of the 2017 to October 2026 record supports one position, which the model has to test. The security relationship is probably Washington's most reliable source of leverage over the Netherlands and the EU on ASML, more reliable than tariffs. Its force, however, depends on Europeans believing that compliance actually buys protection. Both halves belong in the model.

**Dependence is real and expensive to unwind.** Burilkov and Wolff (2025, Kiel Policy Brief 183, presented jointly with Bruegel) estimate that defending Europe against Russia without the US would take about 50 additional brigades (300,000 soldiers), at least 1,400 new main battle tanks, 2,000 infantry fighting vehicles, about 2,000 long-range drones a year and about EUR 250 billion a year in extra spending. That is about 1.5% of EU GDP, taking defence spending to 3.5 to 4% of GDP. The IISS (May 2025) puts the procurement cost of replacing US conventional capabilities at $226 to $344 billion, and more than $1 trillion including 25-year life-cycle costs. It expects about a decade to match US ground capabilities, with a short-term "window of vulnerability" before that. Nuclear dependence cannot be replaced within any relevant horizon. FAS estimates that 100 to 120 B61 bombs are deployed at six or seven bases in five or six countries, the Netherlands among them. The Royal Netherlands Air and Space Force relies solely on the F-35, which depends on Lockheed's cloud infrastructure for software updates, logistics and mission data files. In February 2026 Dutch defence state secretary Gijs Tuinman said "Just like your iPhone, you can jailbreak an F-35." The Netherlands therefore both holds the chokepoint and is among the most security-exposed members. We treat this as a candidate explanation, not an established one, for the Dutch concessions of 2023 and 2024 (in 2024 Washington reportedly threatened unilateral action if the Netherlands and Japan did not curb servicing) and for the Dutch climb-down in Europe 2031.

**The linkage is now explicit in US doctrine and deals.** The National Security Strategy (cover dated November 2025, p. 12) says the US "will stand ready to help, potentially through more favorable treatment on commercial matters, technology sharing, and defense procurement" those "counties [sic] that willingly take more responsibility for security in their neighborhoods and align their export controls with ours." Its Europe section (p. 27) lists "Building up the healthy nations of Central, Eastern, and Southern Europe through commercial ties, weapons sales, political collaboration, and cultural and educational exchanges" and "Cultivating resistance to Europe's current trajectory within European nations." The 21 August 2025 EU-US Joint Statement records the EU's intention to "adopt and maintain technology security requirements in line with those of the United States" to "avoid technology leakage to destinations of concern," with the US to facilitate AI-chip exports once those requirements are in place (para. 5). It also records an intention to "substantially increase procurement of military and defense equipment from the United States" (para. 7) and agreement to "strengthen economic security alignment," including cooperation on investment reviews and export controls (para. 19). The 13 November 2025 US-Korea Joint Fact Sheet sets out, side by side, a 15% tariff, $150 billion of shipbuilding investment plus $200 billion under an investment MOU, a Korean plan to raise defence spending to 3.5% of GDP "as soon as possible," $25 billion of US arms purchases by 2030, $33 billion of support for US Forces Korea, and US approval for Korea to build nuclear-powered attack submarines. The document presents these as parallel commitments. Reading them as a quid pro quo is our interpretation, but the pattern supports letting the US make multi-dimensional offers in the model.

**Credibility differs by instrument.** Trade threats have proved weak or legally fragile. Trump announced Greenland tariffs on eight countries, the Netherlands among them, on 17 January 2026 (10% from February, 25% from 1 June). He withdrew them on 21 January, before they took effect, after meeting NATO Secretary General Rutte in Davos and citing a "framework of a future deal." Stocks and the dollar had fallen on 20 January, and the European Parliament's trade committee chair had frozen work on the Turnberry tariff legislation. The sources do not establish which of these caused the reversal. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court held, 6-3 in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, that IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. Security threats, by contrast, have been carried out step by step. US brigade combat teams in Europe went from four to three ("2021 levels", 19 May 2026). On 1 May 2026 Hegseth ordered about 5,000 troops, including a brigade combat team, out of Germany after Chancellor Merz criticized the Iran war, and a Pentagon official cited "recent German rhetoric." In September 2026 the Pentagon was weighing a drawdown of 25,000 or more (with a 40,000 option) of roughly 80,000 troops in Europe. Small cuts are cheap for Washington and fit the burden-shifting it wants anyway. Congress constrains them only procedurally. The FY2026 NDAA does not ban going below 76,000 troops; it requires a certification of consultation with allies and independent impact assessments first. Section 1250A of the FY2024 NDAA (22 U.S.C. 1928f) bars withdrawal from the North Atlantic Treaty without two-thirds Senate consent or an Act of Congress. The US also needs European bases, as the Iran war showed: Spain barred US use of joint bases and then closed its airspace to aircraft involved (30 March 2026), Rubio called NATO "a one-way street," and in June Hegseth called allies' conduct "shameful" and ordered a six-month posture review. The US security stick is therefore best modelled as continuous and graduated, cheap at the margin, with a costly but not prohibitive procedural threshold and a much harder legal barrier to treaty exit. BIS export controls, including the foreign direct product rule, rest on the Export Control Reform Act of 2018, not IEEPA, so the tariff ruling does not reach them.

**The literature disagrees on whether abandonment threats produce compliance, and the disagreement maps onto a modelling choice.** Gerzhoy (2015) shows that alliance coercion, meaning conditional threats of abandonment, succeeded with West Germany when the client was militarily dependent and the patron assured it that the threat was conditional on its choices. Miller (2014) shows that once US sanctions threats became credible through legislation in the late 1970s, they deterred states dependent on the US economically and for security from starting nuclear programmes, largely through selection. Lanoszka (2018) argues that allies doubting the patron's commitment hedge toward independent capabilities. Blankenship (2021) finds that US burden-sharing pressure works better when the US can credibly threaten to cut protection and when that would impose serious costs. Allies that are geographically vulnerable contribute more, and allies in locations valuable to the US contribute less. The conditional-assurance mechanism therefore comes from Gerzhoy, not from Blankenship (2021). We use it as the core and treat the effect of the security lever as non-monotonic. Rising credibility of abandonment increases leverage only while Europeans believe compliance changes the outcome. European opinion is near the point where it stops doing so. ECFR's May 2026 poll (15 countries, n = 19,481) finds 11% seeing the US as an ally, down from 16% six months earlier and 22% in November 2024, and majorities in every country think the US would not come to their aid if attacked. Pew (fieldwork February to May 2026) finds majorities calling the US a reliable partner only in Hungary and Poland; in the other eight European countries surveyed the share fell by 28 to 52 points. This is a US commitment problem that mirrors Europe's inability to commit not to sell to China later. We found no report of a US quid pro quo for the 2024 Dutch servicing restrictions in the sources checked. BIS removed the Samsung and SK hynix China fabs (and Intel Dalian) from the validated end-user programme by a rule announced 29 August 2025 and published 2 September 2025, effective 120 days later. The NSS offers favourable treatment only "potentially." The model should centre on a two-sided commitment problem.

**Differences among member states give the US a wedge, but QMV arithmetic limits it.** Frontline states spend the most (NATO estimates of 2026 core spending: Lithuania 5.33%, Estonia 5.1%, Latvia 4.92%, Poland 4.68%, against Germany 2.69% and France 2.22%). In Le Grand Continent's November 2025 poll, Poland was the only one of nine countries where more respondents saw Trump as an ally (24%) than a foe (19%). Trump said in September 2026 that "major progress" was being made toward a new US Army base in Poland, while Germany, Spain and Italy were named as places for deep cuts. Security side payments to frontline states are cheap because the US would keep some forces there anyway. The companion review of Europe 2031 (notes/review/e2031.md) shows, however, that the scenario's named opponents and abstainer (Netherlands, Ireland, Poland, the three Baltic states, Italy) hold about 28% of EU population and only seven states. That blocks neither QMV threshold, so the scenario's failed ACI vote is not an equilibrium as written. A security wedge confined to frontline states cannot defeat an ACI measure. The US must also reach a large member (Germany, France or Spain) or about six more states of any size. The pivot set, not the frontline, is where security linkage has to work.

**History supports conditional optimism for Europe.** On 18 June 1982 Reagan extended the Soviet pipeline sanctions to foreign subsidiaries and licensees of US firms, hitting Dresser-France and Creusot-Loire. Britain, France, West Germany and Italy said they would honour their contracts. Reagan lifted the sanctions on 13 November 1982 in exchange for an imprecise allied understanding on East-West trade, to which France formally declined to be a party. The Toshiba-Kongsberg case ended differently: Executive Order 12661 (27 December 1988) imposed a three-year ban on US government procurement from Toshiba Machine, Toshiba Corporation, Kongsberg Trading and Kongsberg Vaapenfabrikk, and a three-year import ban on Toshiba Machine and Kongsberg Trading products. We read the contrast as turning on European unity and on how much the US values alliance cohesion. The NSS's call for "cultivating resistance" inside European nations suggests that the second has fallen. This reading is interpretive and should be argued, not assumed.

**Two further points.** First, a European turn to China is not politically credible. In the nine-country Le Grand Continent survey, 55% want the same distance from the US and China, 4% prefer alignment with China and about 20% with the US, and 44% name China as Europe's main technological adversary against 9% for the US. Europe's realistic threat is to withhold or stay neutral, not to switch sides. Second, US leverage through security dependence also wears down. If allies meet the Hague commitment of at least 3.5% of GDP on core defence by 2035 (5% in total, reviewed in 2029), the security lever weakens over roughly the same period in which ASML's monopoly erodes. The model should make the timing race between these two wasting assets explicit.

## Modeling implications

- Model US security provision as a continuous choice s in [s_min, s_max]. Cuts below the FY2026 NDAA level of 76,000 troops are not prohibited; they trigger a certification and assessment requirement, so treat that level as a kink with a fixed political cost, not a hard floor. Treaty exit is separately constrained by Section 1250A (two-thirds of the Senate or an Act of Congress), so the model should separate a cheap posture margin from a costly commitment margin. Evidence of the cheap margin: four to three brigade combat teams (May 2026), 5,000 out of Germany (May 2026), and the 25,000 to 40,000 options (September 2026).
- Give the US an instrument menu with different credibility. IEEPA tariffs are legally gone (20 February 2026), and the Greenland threat was withdrawn before it took effect. Security posture and ECRA-based export controls (including the FDPR) are unaffected. Predict that the US links ASML to security and to AI-compute access rather than to tariffs, as Europe 2031 assumes.
- Build in a two-sided commitment problem. Europe cannot commit not to sell to China later, and the US cannot commit to reward compliance with lasting protection or technology access (the NSS offers favourable treatment only "potentially"; Korean fabs lost VEU status). Model the US reward as subject to reneging with probability r, so Europe's compliance incentive is (1 - r) times the protection gained.
- Make Europe's compliance non-monotonic in the credibility of the US abandonment threat, building on Gerzhoy's (2015) condition that coercion works only when the threat is conditional on the ally's choice, plus Lanoszka's (2018) hedging response. Let p be Europe's belief that the US defends regardless and q its belief that the US defends after compliance; compliance depends on q - p. Collapsing trust (ECFR 11% ally; majorities in every polled country doubt US help) can shrink q - p even as threats harden. Do not attribute the non-monotonicity to Blankenship (2021), whose evidence is that leverage rises with credible abandonment threats and falls with the ally's strategic value to the US.
- Use Blankenship's (2021) strategic-value finding as a reverse-dependence term. Allies in locations valuable to the US contribute less under pressure. Spain's denial of bases and airspace in the Iran war, and the Rubio and Hegseth reactions, show Washington needs European bases. That raises the US cost of large cuts and gives Europe a counter-lever that could be linked to ASML.
- Model member states as differing in security dependence theta_i under real QMV weights. Frontline states (Poland, the Baltics) have the highest theta and spending (4.7 to 5.3% core in 2026) and receive cheap targeted inducements (the planned Poland base; the NSS line on "healthy nations of Central, Eastern, and Southern Europe"). They cannot block on their own: with the Netherlands, Ireland and Italy they hold about 28% of population and seven states. The US wedge has to reach a large member or about six more states, so the pivotal member, not the frontline, prices the side payment.
- Treat the Netherlands as an agent with both asset control and high security exposure (F-35-only fighter fleet dependent on US-run software and mission data; nuclear-sharing host). Delegating ASML control upward (an EU golden share, or ACI triggers written into law) is a hand-tying device (Fearon 1997) that replaces a more exposed agent with a less exposed one; make this a comparative static.
- Make the timing race between two wasting assets explicit. Europe's security dependence falls as it rearms (Hague target of 3.5% core by 2035, review in 2029; Kiel and IISS estimates of about a decade and EUR 250bn a year), while ASML's leverage falls as China indigenises and the US stockpiles tools and know-how. Derive when Europe should hold out versus settle now, with rearmament as an endogenous investment in bargaining power.
- Do not give Europe a credible "align with China" option: only 4% prefer alignment with China, 55% want equal distance, and 44% see China as the main technological adversary. Europe's feasible defection is withholding or neutrality.
- Add a parameter for the value the US places on alliance cohesion. The 1982 pipeline sanctions were lifted after five months when the large Europeans held together; the Toshiba-Kongsberg sanctions stuck against two firms from two separately exposed allies. The NSS's "cultivating resistance" language suggests the cohesion weight is low now.
- Allow multi-dimensional take-it-or-leave-it offers that bundle tariffs, compute-access tiers, security posture and investment, as in the Korean (13 November 2025) and Japanese (4 September 2025) packages and the Joint Statement's export-control alignment clause (para. 19). Europe's counter-linkage tool, the ACI, faces QMV and has never been used.
- Fuhrmann and Sechser (2014) find that formal alliance commitments deter but stationing nuclear weapons on an ally's territory adds no measurable deterrence. If European valuation of the guarantee rests mainly on the treaty commitment (protected by Section 1250A), conventional posture cuts work mainly as signals of intent (Lanoszka) rather than as direct losses of protection. The model should let posture cuts move beliefs (q, p) rather than capability.
