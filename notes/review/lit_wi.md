## Synthesis (edited)

**Position 1: anchor the model on Wagner, not Hirschman.** Hirschman (1945) and Keohane and Nye (1977) treat asymmetric dependence, especially vulnerability (the cost of a cutoff after adjustment), as a direct source of power. Wagner (1988) shows with bargaining theory that asymmetric interdependence does not imply that the less dependent state gains political influence. Influence requires that exchanging economic resources for political concessions leave both sides better off than bargaining over the gains from the economic relationship alone, whatever the degree or direction of asymmetry. For ASML, then, the question is not how much the US needs EUV tools. It is whether the Netherlands and the EU can shift the US disagreement payoff on an issue Washington values more than the commercial surplus from lithography. Keohane and Nye's vulnerability concept survives as a parameter: it sets how fast the counterparty's outside option recovers through stockpiles, alternative servicing and Chinese indigenization. That recovery is what makes ASML a wasting asset.

**Position 2: ASML is a nested chokepoint, and control over it is a double key.** Weaponized interdependence (Farrell and Newman 2019, 2023; Drezner, Farrell and Newman 2021) explains why a central node gives coercive power to the state with effective jurisdiction over it. The ASML case breaks the assumption that the state where the node sits is the state that can weaponize it. The Hague licenses ASML, but Washington reaches the firm through US-origin content, its centrality in the design network (Beaumier and Cartwright 2024) and the foreign direct product rules (Malkin and He 2024; Xu 2026). The December 2024 BIS rule (89 FR 96790) excludes 33 countries listed in a new Supplement No. 4 to Part 742 from its new SME foreign direct product rule. The list includes the Netherlands, Japan and 25 of the 27 EU members (all except Cyprus and Malta); South Korea and Taiwan are not on it. BIS justifies the carve-out on the ground that these governments belong to multilateral export control regimes and "have the authority to control key SME items of concern." A separate clause exempts reexports by entities in other countries that have implemented "equivalent controls", defined as listing the item and applying "the same license review policy." Our reading, which the rule does not state, is that both carve-outs are a menu: the host state is spared extraterritorial reach because it can and does control the items itself, and BIS can revise the list. Firms hold part of the key too. Hynek (2026) argues that ASML's control of tools and the service regime, together with TSMC's control of qualification and capacity, forms a "corporate chokehold" that states do not fully control, and firms push back on their own and allied governments (Bapat and Kwon 2015; Chen and Evers 2023; Cho 2026). The model should make the US override available but costly. History suggests the cost is high: in three cases after the 1982 pipeline sanctions (Nicaragua, Libya, South Africa), Washington was unwilling to incur alliance costs by applying extraterritorial controls and instead shaped corporate decisions by raising firms' perceived risk (Rodman 1995). The CoCom record shows recurrent US-allied conflict over how broadly to restrict East-West trade (Mastanduno 1992).

**Position 3: credibility comes from payoffs and timing more than from private resolve.** The formal sanctions literature agrees that leverage shows up at the threat stage, and that sanctions actually imposed are a selected sample in which success was unlikely (Smith 1995; Lacy and Niou 2004; Drezner 2003; Hovi, Huseby and Sprinz 2005). The 2028 Dutch capitulation in Europe 2031 is therefore compatible with real Dutch leverage that Washington priced into a calibrated demand (Krustev 2010). Whang, McLean and Kuberski (2013) find no support for the informational (resolve) mechanism and robust support for the coercive mechanism, which argues for a model built on who bears which costs over a pure signaling model.

Eaton and Engers (1992) supply the primitive: what a sender extracts depends on relative toughness, which combines patience and suffering. A wasting asset is impatience by another name. If leverage depreciates with Chinese self-reliance (Tan, Dallas, Farrell and Newman 2025) and with each use of the chokepoint (Rajan 2026), Europe's bargaining power is front-loaded and the US gains from delay. That is what the scenario's March 2031 claim asserts: through its Taiwanese partners the US "has amassed enough EUV machines and maintenance know-how to forego ASML access for longer than Europe can forego access to frontier AI." This is the logic of Powell's (2006) commitment problem carried into economic bargaining, and it runs both ways: Europe cannot commit not to sell to China later, and the US cannot commit to reward compliance. Pauly (2024) calls the second half the assurance dilemma: actions that make a threat credible undermine the assurance that punishment is conditional.

The scenario's sequence is more subtle than a pure assurance failure. In May 2028 the Dutch cave and the protagonist complains that "we didn't even manage to negotiate a single thing in return." In April 2029, however, the FISR places the Netherlands in Tier 1 with the Five Eyes, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, while most of Europe, including France and Germany, lands in Tier 2 and Paris and Berlin's request for Tier 1 is refused. The complying host state is rewarded after the fact; the collective that backed it is not. That is an assurance failure at the EU level and a selective reward at the national level, and it is consistent with the Netherlands voting against the ACI measure aimed at ASML servicing in Arizona. A well-specified model should generate both effects: uncompensated collective compliance should raise the EU's willingness to resist in 2031, while targeted rewards to the host state lower the chance that resistance can be organized.

**Position 4: the EU is a collective sender, and the patron can buy its pivot.** The literature disagrees on whether multilateral coalitions help:
- Martin (1993) argues that a leading sender gains cooperation through issue linkage, which is credible when sanctions are costly and run through institutions that raise audience costs.
- Drezner (2000) finds that multilateral sanctions fail through enforcement, not bargaining: without an international organization, cooperating states backslide.
- McLean and Whang (2010) and Early (2015) stress the target's major trading partners and third-party sanctions busters, who are often the sender's own allies.
- Wei (2025) shows that primary senders weigh the cost of adding partners, which rises with preference divergence, against their coercive contribution.

On mechanism we side with Martin and Drezner: the Anti-Coercion Instrument is the device that could turn a non-credible Dutch threat into a credible EU one. But member exposures differ (Mertens and Reisenbichler 2026), and economic dependence shapes political alignment (Kleinman, Liu and Redding 2024), so the patron can discriminate among members. The January 2025 AI Diffusion rule shows what such discrimination looks like: its favored group (Supplement No. 5 to Part 740, paragraph (a)) included ten EU members and left seventeen to country allocations, a wedge in the sense of Crawford (2011). The rule was rescinded on 13 May 2025 before its compliance date and BIS does not enforce it, so it is a precedent for design, not an instrument in force. Kalyanpur and Newman (2019) show that harmonizing jurisdiction raises bargaining power against US extraterritorial pressure, yet export licensing stays national (Pandiyan and Schild 2026; Young and Brown 2026).

**Position 5: the patron's structural advantage is threat pooling.** Clayton, Maggiori and Schreger (2026a) model a hegemon whose power comes from coordinating threats across disparate economic relationships. Clayton, Maggiori and Schreger (2026b) add that integration gains make other relationships poor substitutes, which raises hegemonic power, and that countries' uncoordinated economic security policies fragment the world economy inefficiently. Applied here, the US can link ASML to NATO guarantees, AI access tiers and tariffs, while Europe can threaten only inside the lithography relationship unless it centralizes. Mangini (2024) adds a caution: conditioning market access on compliance across many issues maximizes incentives but makes them brittle, since a target that cannot comply on one issue has no reason to comply on any. That may explain why an all-or-nothing 2031 offer fails.

**Position 6: the security link is where we depart most from the canon, which assumes the sender and target are adversaries.** Here the coercer is a security-dependent junior ally (Morrow 1991; Lake 2009), so the patron can retaliate where it has escalation dominance.
- Gerzhoy (2015) shows that alliance coercion works when the client depends on the patron militarily and the patron assures it that threats of abandonment are conditional on its choices.
- Drezner (1999) predicts that allies use coercion reluctantly but, once they do, extract significant concessions, because conflict expectations are low. Rising conflict expectations between Washington and Brussels should erode that.
- China adds a second coercer and bidder. Yoder and Cohen (2025) model how a prospective ally's bargaining with third parties is shaped by its relationship with a patron, and Cha (2023) shows that targets of Chinese coercion hold counter-leverage (over $46.6 billion of exports to China in goods where China is more than 70 percent import-dependent).

One counterintuitive result is worth deriving. Chinese threats to punish compliance can tie Europe's hands against Washington and raise its leverage, while defense dependence lowers it. In the scenario Beijing's instruments are rare-earth export terms and robot export licences. China's centrality in electrification metals was built through processing investment and industrial policy rather than reserves (Kirk, Passari and Rey 2026), which supports a strong short-run Chinese threat; whether their 13-metal sample includes rare earths should be checked before citing them for rare earths specifically. Markets adjust over longer horizons, as Japan did after the 2010 rare earth embargo (Gholz and Hughes 2021).

Finally, the 2031 holding company is a purchase of residual control rights (Grossman and Hart 1986). In the scenario the US offers a capital injection "enabling a range of new production sites to be built on US soil" and cash transfers to citizens indexed to US AI windfalls, starting at "perhaps €100 per person per year." Once control is sold, the leverage does not come back. Europe should therefore weigh the transfer against the present value of a depreciating asset plus its option value in contingencies such as a Taiwan crisis.

## Modeling implications

1. Core existence condition (Wagner 1988): Europe has political leverage over the US via ASML only if linking ASML access or control to a US political concession creates joint gains relative to bargaining over the lithography surplus alone. Make this the first proposition and avoid any "asymmetric dependence equals power" assumption.
2. Double key. The Netherlands holds formal licensing (Pandiyan and Schild 2026; Young and Brown 2026). The US holds an extraterritorial override (FDP rules) that it can exercise at cost k_F, capturing alliance costs (Rodman 1995; Mastanduno 1992) and erosion of network centrality (Farrell and Newman 2019; Rajan 2026). The equilibrium object is the revocable carve-out: the December 2024 rule spares listed states such as the Netherlands from the SME FDP rule because they control the items themselves. Derive when the US prefers to delist and invoke the override rather than accept partial Dutch compliance.
3. Give ASML a role, either as a strategic player or as a constraint on the Dutch state's enforcement incentives (Bapat and Kwon 2015; Cho 2026; Hynek 2026). Dutch enforcement of US-demanded restrictions is not self-enforcing when China is a large market. Servicing is a flow chokepoint that cannot be stockpiled, except insofar as the US can replicate know-how (the scenario's Taiwanese-partner claim).
4. Wasting asset: let leverage evolve as V_{t+1} = delta(r_t, s) V_t, with depreciation rising in the intensity of past restrictions (Tan, Dallas, Farrell and Newman 2025; Rajan 2026) and in US substitution effort s (stockpiles, Taiwanese servicing). Following Eaton and Engers (1992), this acts as European impatience and shifts bargaining power to the US over time. Predict that Europe front-loads its demands and the US delays.
5. Private information should sit on the depreciation rate. The US knows its own stockpile and servicing substitutes. Model this as screening or cheap talk by the US, and keep resolve uncertainty secondary to cost parameters (Whang, McLean and Kuberski 2013).
6. Two-sided commitment problem (Powell 2006; Pauly 2024), with a national-collective split. The US cannot commit not to issue new demands after compliance, and Europe cannot commit not to sell to China later. Allow the US to reward the complying host state selectively (the Netherlands in Tier 1 in 2029) while withholding rewards from the collective. Show that uncompensated collective compliance raises EU willingness to resist the 2031 ultimatum, while selective rewards to the host state make the host a blocker inside the EU.
7. EU stage as a collective sender under QMV, with member types on two dimensions: security exposure to Russia (dependence on the US guarantee) and economic exposure to China (Mertens and Reisenbichler 2026; Kleinman, Liu and Redding 2024). The US chooses discriminatory treatment as a wedge to break the pivotal coalition (Crawford 2011). Precedent: the January 2025 AI Diffusion rule put 10 EU members in its favored group and 17 outside, but it was rescinded in May 2025 and is not enforced. Derive the minimum US side payment or tier assignment needed to block an ACI measure.
8. Institutional design comparative static: centralizing counter-coercion authority at EU level (stronger ACI, Commission agenda-setting, automaticity) raises hand-tying costs and threat credibility (Martin 1993; Fearon 1997; Kalyanpur and Newman 2019; Mertens 2026) and reduces member backsliding (Drezner 2000).
9. Asymmetric threat pooling (Clayton, Maggiori and Schreger 2026a): the US punishment set is multi-issue (NATO guarantees, AI access tiers, tariffs) and Europe's is single-issue unless it creates new pooled assets. Show how the "indispensability" investment (compute on European soil) expands Europe's pooled threat set, and how uncoordinated national security policies fragment inefficiently (Clayton, Maggiori and Schreger 2026b).
10. Bundling brittleness (Mangini 2024): the all-or-nothing 2031 ultimatum (control rights plus tier status) should, for some parameters, produce rejection where unbundled demands would have been accepted.
11. China as a second coercer and bidder. Chinese threats to punish compliance with the US (rare-earth export terms, robot export licences, legacy chips) raise Europe's cost of complying with Washington and can tie Europe's hands, increasing its leverage against the US, while defense dependence lowers it. Derive conditions under which Europe's leverage over the US rises with its China exposure. Discount Chinese leverage over time via market adjustment (Gholz and Hughes 2021), but treat it as strong in the short run given engineered centrality in critical-metal supply chains (Kirk, Passari and Rey 2026).
12. Property rights stage (Grossman and Hart 1986): model the 2031 holding-company offer as a one-time transfer T (capital injection for US production sites, citizen cash transfers) for residual control rights. Europe accepts if T exceeds the present value of decaying leverage plus option value in contingencies such as a Taiwan crisis. Because leverage depreciates, there is a region where selling at the peak is rational. Characterize it, and show that option value and assurance failures shrink it.
13. Patron payoffs must include its own dependence on allies for access (Carson, Metz and Poast 2025) and the value of slowing China's rise (McCormack and Pascoe 2017), which together bound the credibility of abandonment and FDP threats.
14. Empirical implications: because of threat-stage selection (Smith 1995; Lacy and Niou 2004; Drezner 2003; Hovi, Huseby and Sprinz 2005), European leverage should appear as US concessions or carve-outs at the threat stage (for example the Supplement No. 4 list), not as imposed EU counter-measures. Dutch caves should not be read as zero leverage.

## Open questions

1. No keyword search was possible this session, so the claimed gap (no formal IR model of export-control coalitions among allies under an extraterritorial hegemon) remains unverified. Run a targeted search before claiming it in the paper.
2. Mastanduno (1988): only the opening extract was retrievable, and it states a "dual conventional wisdom" (domestically weak, internationally strong American state) as the backdrop. Read the full article before citing it for anything beyond the early postwar export-control baseline.
3. Morrow (1991): Crossref and Semantic Scholar list only the start page (904). The end page 933 is from prior knowledge; confirm on JSTOR.
4. Rajan (2026): Crossref gives online publication 14 October 2026; OpenAlex gives 19 August 2026. Check the journal page.
5. Kirk, Passari and Rey (2026): confirm whether rare earths are among their 13 electrification metals before citing them for rare-earth leverage.
6. Anti-Coercion Instrument procedure (Regulation (EU) 2023/2675): EUR-Lex was not fetched. Confirm whether the Council determines coercion by QMV and whether response measures are Commission implementing acts under the examination procedure, since the scenario has the ASML measure fail to reach QMV. This is shared with the EU institutions strand.
7. AI Diffusion replacement: BIS promised a replacement rule in May 2025. A Federal Register lookup found no replacement through the 14 July 2026 UAE rule. Check for anything issued between July and October 2026.
8. Thoenig (2024), "Trade policy in the shadow of war", remains unverified and uncited.
9. Depreciation: decide whether it is exogenous (time) or endogenous (use and the intensity of denial). Rajan (2026) and Tan, Dallas, Farrell and Newman (2025) supply mechanisms for the endogenous version, and the choice drives the use-it-or-lose-it results.
10. Japan and Korea declining to coordinate: model it as their own security dependence on the US (a parallel junior-ally problem, as the scenario states) or as firm autonomy (Cho 2026)? This determines whether the epilogue's middle-power coalition is feasible in equilibrium.
11. Selective rewards: the scenario's Tier 1 placement of the Netherlands is not explained in the text. Decide whether to model it as a reward for 2028 compliance, as a wedge against the ACI vote, or as exogenous.
