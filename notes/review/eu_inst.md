# Review: who in Europe decides whether to use ASML as leverage (strand: EU and Dutch decision rules and instruments)

Fact-check completed 28 October 2026. The shared WebSearch budget for this workflow turn was used up before this strand started, so no new web searches were run. Every item was checked instead against primary documents fetched directly: EU legal texts through the Publications Office (Cellar) by CELEX number, including the consolidated dual-use regulation of 21 September 2026; Cellar SPARQL metadata queries re-run independently; the Federal Register API and GovInfo; BIS; the White House; the Commission press corner API; government.nl; the EU trade site; SEC EDGAR; the EP Legislative Observatory; arXiv; Crossref; and the europe2031.ai pages dated 11 June 2026. Population shares, blocking coalitions and power indices were recomputed from scratch (exact dynamic programming, checked by a one-million-draw Monte Carlo). Nothing in the memo turned out to be fabricated. Several numbers and two institutional claims needed correction, and three findings were added (marked below).

## Synthesis (edited)

"Europe" is not a single actor in the ASML game. Who decides depends on which way the leverage runs. EU law gives the Netherlands close to unilateral control over restricting ASML sales to China. It makes most uses of ASML against the United States a collective EU decision, and the Council pivot in that decision is a large member state. The model should treat the European side as a gatekeeper (The Hague) inside a weighted voting body (the Council), with the Commission as agenda setter and, on ownership questions, as a formal reviewer in its own right. It should not treat Europe as one bargainer.

**China-facing decisions.** Dual-use export control is common commercial policy (Werner and Leifer, 1995; Art. 207 TFEU). Even so, Regulation 2021/821 leaves the licensing decision with the competent authority of the member state where the exporter is established (Art. 12(2)). Other member states can only ask for consultations that are "of a non-binding nature" (Art. 14(2)) or flag denials for "essentially identical" transactions (Art. 16(5)), after which the licensing state may still grant. Commission Delegated Regulation 2025/2003 (adopted 8 September 2025, in force 15 November 2025) moved the licence requirement for the most advanced DUV immersion tools to EU level (new entry 3B501.f.1.b: wavelength of 193 nm or more, resolution of 45 nm or less, dedicated chuck overlay of 1.50 nm or less). Older immersion tools (overlay above 1.50 nm and up to 2.40 nm) stay on the Dutch national list as 3B901f6. ASML's own 2025 annual report says the EU listing "did not mean a change" for the firm, because the products already needed a Dutch licence. Neither change moved the grant-or-deny decision away from The Hague. Washington is therefore rational to target the Dutch government, as Europe 2031 depicts for 2028. The scenario's line that "the Commission backs the Dutch" describes cheap talk: the Commission has no licensing power and no veto. Solidarity can enter the Dutch payoff only through linkage, such as compensation, coordinated retaliation or cover against Chinese counter-coercion. Any EU-wide tightening beyond the dual-use list is a unanimity game: CFSP decisions need unanimity (Art. 31 TEU) before Art. 215 TFEU measures follow by QMV, and the 2025 Annex I expansion rested on commitments that member states told the Commission on 23 May 2025 "they have all accepted."

**US-facing decisions.** Using ASML against Washington is institutionally harder. Union General Export Authorisation EU001 authorises exports to the United States of every Annex I item except those in Annex II Section I. In the consolidated text of 21 September 2026, Section I contains no Category 3 entry, so EUV and advanced DUVi scanners ship to US fabs without an individual licence. EU001 contains no national-security opt-out for the exporting state; that clause appears in EU008. Art. 12(7) lets a member state bar an exporter from using a UGEA only for "reasonable suspicion about the exporter's ability to comply." On this reading the Netherlands alone cannot lawfully embargo EUV or advanced DUVi deliveries to Arizona. **(Added nuance.)** Dutch national controls, by contrast, apply "to all destinations outside the EU", so The Hague keeps a unilateral licence over US-bound shipments of the older 3B901f6-class tools. An inference that needs confirmation: by moving advanced DUVi from the Dutch list to Annex I, the November 2025 Europeanisation removed a unilateral Dutch lever toward the United States while making the China-facing control harder to reverse. Two EU routes exist for the listed tools.

- **Delegated act.** The Commission can remove the United States from EU001 by delegated act (Art. 17(2)), and for removing destinations it may use the urgency procedure of Art. 19, under which the act enters into force at once. **(Corrected.)** The act stands unless Parliament objects by a majority of its component members or the Council objects by *reinforced* qualified majority. Because the Council is not acting on a Commission proposal, Art. 238(2) TFEU applies: 72% of members (20 of 27) representing 65% of the population. The Commission stated exactly this threshold for objections to the 2022 taxonomy delegated act. A coalition of only 8 members, or of states holding more than 35% of the population, can therefore keep the act in force. The default strongly favours the Commission. The Netherlands would then still have to deny the individual licences, so it stays pivotal.
- **Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI).** The Commission first examines the case ("normally" up to four months). The Council must then adopt, by ordinary qualified majority on the Commission's proposal, an implementing act finding that coercion exists. It "shall act within 8 weeks", may run longer if it informs the Commission, and "normally" within ten weeks in total. The Commission then picks measures from Annex I through the examination procedure. Annex I point 2 explicitly covers restrictions on exports "including, where appropriate, on goods subject to export control", and point 5 covers services. The ACI's no-opinion clause means a measure first needs a positive committee opinion. In the appeal committee, however, the Commission "may adopt" the act unless a qualified majority votes against it. **(Corrected: this is discretionary, not automatic, and recital 85 asks the Commission to avoid "going against any predominant position amongst Member States" even there.)**

The ACI veto is therefore front-loaded. Doves who expect an ASML measure must block at the determination stage. Hawks cannot commit the Commission in advance to a specific measure, and a measure aimed at a European champion is legally fragile. Article 11(1) requires proportionality, Article 11(2)(b) requires avoiding or minimising harm to Union actors, and the Union-interest test in Article 9 weighs "upstream and downstream industries." Summing the normal maxima (4 months of examination, about 2.5 months in Council, an indicative assessment period of up to 6 months, and deferred application of up to 3 months, which Art. 8(6) allows to be longer "in light of specific circumstances") gives roughly 15 to 16 months plus committee time. Our estimate of 9 to 16 months from trigger to effect is a judgment, not a legal rule. Two provisions cut the other way. Art. 8(11) lets measures adopted against a *threatened* measure apply from the day the threat is carried out, and Art. 8(10) allows application without first notifying the third country in duly justified cases. US instruments such as the foreign direct product rule (FDPR) act at once. This gap in timing makes the wasting-asset problem worse.

**Arithmetic and the pivot.** Take the 2029 vote in Europe 2031: the Netherlands, Ireland, Poland and the Baltics oppose, and Italy abstains (abstention counts as no under QMV). Under 2026 population weights (Council Decision 2025/2427) this coalition holds 28.06% of EU population and seven members. That meets neither blocking threshold: more than 35% of population with at least four members, or at least 13 members, which pushes the yes side below 15. The scenario's outcome needs Germany, France or Spain (any one is enough) to withhold support as well; Romania alone (32.27%) is not enough. We push against the scenario here and draw the opposite lesson. The pivot for any anti-US use of ASML is a large member, most plausibly Germany. **(Corrected values.)** Germany's Shapley-Shubik index is 17.7% against 3.65% for the Netherlands; the normalised Banzhaf indices are 12.0% and 3.8%.

Two cheap blocking coalitions matter for an outside power trying to buy votes:

- Germany and France (33.65% together), plus any two members adding more than about 1.35% (Ireland and Luxembourg reach 35.0003%);
- the 13 smallest members, who together hold only 9.58% of population.

The second is a natural target for side payments in the Groseclose-Snyder sense, and Dal Bó (2007) shows that offers conditional on being pivotal can buy a committee outcome at almost no cost. US tiering maps onto these cleavages. The AI Diffusion Rule put 10 member states in Tier 1. **(Corrected values.)** They hold 70.46% of EU population but are fewer than 15 members. The other 17 fell into the middle tier with 29.54% of population, but enough members to block by count. Tiering member states separately is therefore a ready tool for building blocking minorities.

**Revealed type.** An independent re-run of the Cellar metadata query found no ACI determination or response act through October 2026; the only post-2023 hit is a 2024 corrigendum. The EU's actual record:

- It adopted rebalancing measures under the Enforcement Regulation (Implementing Regulation 2025/1564), including a ban from 7 September 2025 on exports of ferrous and aluminium scrap (CN 7204, 7602) to the United States. It suspended them three times (adopted 5 August 2025, 4 February 2026 and 30 July 2026).
- It enacted the Turnberry tariff concessions in Regulation 2026/1455, keeping a suspension trigger the Commission controls through examination-procedure implementing acts in the Trade Barriers Committee.
- It answered the January 2026 Greenland tariff threat with statements. The 17 January joint statement warned of "a dangerous downward spiral", and von der Leyen promised a response "unflinching, united and proportional" at Davos on 20 January. Neither text mentions the ACI.

Washington, for its part, has made access conditional. In the Joint Statement of 21 August 2025 the EU "plans to work with the United States to adopt and maintain technology security requirements in line with those of the United States", and the US "will endeavour to facilitate such exports once such requirements are in place." The US AI Action Plan names the FDPR and secondary tariffs as tools against allies that backfill. The December 2024 SME exclusion list (Supplement No. 4 to Part 742) covers 25 EU states (all except Cyprus and Malta), on the stated ground that their governments "have the authority to control key SME items of concern", and BIS can amend it by rule. **(Added.)** The AI Diffusion tiers are not in force. BIS announced their rescission on 13 May 2025 and has published no formal rescission or replacement rule. Its 31 May 2026 guidance and the July 2026 UAE rule state that BIS enforces the worldwide licence requirement for advanced AI chips only for destinations in Country Groups D:1, D:4 or D:5 (excluding those also in A:5 or A:6) and for entities headquartered in D:5 or Macau. Country tiering is therefore a latent threat that the US could revive, not a standing constraint. Priors on EU resolve should start low. The cost of the US threat should be modelled as losing the SME exclusion and losing chip access.

**Ownership and compute.** From 17 January 2028, Regulation 2026/1386 makes FDI screening mandatory in every member state and puts semiconductor manufacturing equipment "including ... lithography" and certain AI technologies in its minimum scope. It is "without prejudice to the sole responsibility of each Member State for its national security", so the screening decision stays national. **(Corrected: the ownership game is not purely bilateral.)** The Foreign Subsidies Regulation (Reg. 2022/2560) treats a third-country "capital injection" as a financial contribution. It requires notification of any concentration in which the target is established in the Union with EU turnover of at least EUR 500 million and the parties received more than EUR 50 million from third countries over three years. After an in-depth investigation the Commission may prohibit the concentration. A US-funded holding company taking control of ASML would very likely be notifiable, though that is our inference. The 2031 ultimatum is therefore a US-Dutch decision on screening and a Commission decision on foreign subsidies (and possibly on merger control, which we have not checked). German and French leverage remains political: neither holds a formal veto, but they can lean on the Commission's FSR review. The scenario's line that no Dutch government could agree "without [Germany's and France's] acquiescence" is political, not legal.

On compute, the EU's outside option is thin:

- about 50,000 H100-equivalents in March 2025, against 850,000 in the United States and 110,000 in China (Epoch AI dataset, Pilz et al. 2025);
- three non-EU hyperscalers control more than 70% of the EU cloud market, and the EU providers' share fell from 29% (2017) to 15% (2022) (COM(2026) 502);
- Europe 2031 puts Europe's share of global AI compute at about 5% today, rising briefly to 8% in 2028 and back to just above 5% in 2031 by default, against an ambitious target of 15% in the epilogue;
- the planned gigafactories (about 100,000 chips each) will run largely on US chips. Reg. 2026/150 requires their coordinators to be headquartered in the Union and controlled by persons established there.

Compute on European soil raises Europe's hostage value only as far as EU law can condition how it is used.

## Modeling implications

- **Two linked games, by direction of leverage.** (i) China-facing: the Netherlands is a unilateral gatekeeper under Art. 12(2), and other states have only non-binding consultation rights (Arts 14(2), 16(5)). (ii) US-facing: action against EUV and advanced DUVi requires an EU collective decision, because EU001 already authorises those exports to the US and gives the exporting state no national-security opt-out. The Netherlands keeps a unilateral licence only over US-bound national-list tools (3B901f6 class).
- **Council as a dual-threshold weighted voting game.** Yes needs at least 15 members and at least 65% of population, and abstention counts as no. Use 2026 weights and allow small drift to 2029-2031. The Europe 2031 blocking coalition (28.06%, 7 members) is insufficient; DE, FR or ES must also defect.
- **The pivot is a large member.** Germany's a priori power (Shapley-Shubik 17.7%, Banzhaf 12.0%) is about five times the Netherlands' (3.65% and 3.8%). Dutch leverage comes from gatekeeping, not voting weight. A natural result: the US can neutralise EU-level counter-coercion by securing one large state, or by buying the 13 smallest members (9.58% of population), who block by count. Dal Bó (2007) implies pivot-contingent offers make this cheap.
- **ACI as a two-stage game with a front-loaded veto.** Stage 1: Council determination by positive ordinary QMV within about 8 to 10 weeks after an examination of normally up to 4 months. Stage 2: Commission-designed measures that need a positive committee opinion first, but which the Commission may adopt in the appeal committee unless a qualified majority votes against. Doves who expect an ASML measure block at stage 1. Hawks cannot commit to a specific measure ex ante. Model the appeal-committee step as Commission discretion constrained by a norm against overriding a predominant position, not as automatic adoption.
- **The delegated-act route has a reversed and much stronger default.** Removing the US from EU001 (Art. 17(2), urgency under Art. 19) stays in force unless 20 member states with 65% of population, or an absolute EP majority, object. Eight members or more than 35% of population suffice to protect it. The Dutch must still deny the licences. Letting the Commission choose between the ACI (NL can be outvoted, but a 35% blocking minority can stop stage 1) and delegated act plus Dutch denial (NL pivotal, objection nearly impossible) gives comparative statics on Dutch and Commission preferences.
- **Europeanisation is a commitment device with a cost.** EU listing of advanced DUVi makes the China-facing requirement sticky, since reversal needs a delegated act consistent with commitments every member state accepted. If our inference holds, it also moved US-bound advanced DUVi under EU001 and removed a unilateral Dutch lever toward Washington. Model listing as a choice that trades Dutch flexibility toward the US for credibility toward the US on China.
- **Discount ASML leverage by procedural delay.** The normal ACI maxima sum to about 15 to 16 months plus committee time, while the FDPR and tier changes act immediately. Combined with US stockpiling, this makes ASML a wasting asset: lower Europe's effective discount factor for the counter-threat. Art. 8(11) allows pre-committing measures against a threatened US measure, which partly offsets the lag if the EU moves before the threat is executed.
- **Constrain the measure set by proportionality and the Union interest.** Arts 9 and 11(2)(b) make an ASML-targeting measure legally exposed, so the probability that the Commission selects or sustains it should be lower than for tariffs, IP or FDI measures. This lowers the credibility of the most painful threat.
- **Low prior on EU resolve.** Through October 2026 no ACI act was adopted, the US rebalancing package was suspended three times, the Turnberry concessions were enacted with only a Commission-held suspension trigger, and the Greenland threat drew statements, not the ACI.
- **US instruments as conditional, member-specific market access.** Revocation of the SME exclusion (Supplement No. 4, covering 25 EU states); revival of country tiering for chips and inference (currently not enforced for non-D destinations, so treat it as a latent threat with a revival cost); the August 2025 link between chip exports and EU "technology security requirements"; FDPR and secondary-tariff threats against backfilling allies. Tiering is a wedge or vote-buying tool: in 2025 the 10 Tier 1 states held 70.46% of population but could not pass anything alone, and the 17 others (29.54%) could block by count.
- **Weak commitment toward China.** Licences are case-by-case, valid up to two years unless set otherwise, and revocable (Art. 16(1)). Europe cannot credibly promise China future access, and cannot credibly promise the US permanent denial beyond the Dutch government's horizon.
- **Ownership subgame (2031 holding company) has three formal players, not two.** The Netherlands decides on screening under Reg. 2026/1386 and the Dutch mechanism. The Commission can review and prohibit a third-country-subsidised concentration under the Foreign Subsidies Regulation. DE and FR act as linkage players who can lean on the Commission's review, without holding a formal veto. This partly vindicates the scenario's intuition that the Netherlands cannot settle the matter alone, though through Brussels rather than through Berlin and Paris.
- **Thin and US-dependent AI outside option.** About 50,000 H100e in the EU against 850,000 in the US (March 2025); about 5% of global AI compute by Europe 2031's estimate; more than 70% of EU cloud held by three non-EU hyperscalers; gigafactories of about 100,000 chips each, with EU-controlled coordinators, that depend on US chip exports. Compute on EU soil raises hostage value only to the extent EU law can condition US-owned capacity.
- **Unanimity for China-facing EU-wide tightening, QMV for US-facing counter-coercion.** This asymmetry should be built into the game form.

## Open questions

- Search limitation: the WebSearch budget for this turn was exhausted, so news-only items could not be checked. These need a search pass: which governments publicly called for activating the ACI over Greenland in January 2026; whether and when the US withdrew the Greenland tariff threat; whether the EP paused work on the Turnberry regulation.
- Merger control: would a US government-backed holding company acquiring joint control of ASML be a concentration with a Union dimension under Reg. 139/2004, and could the Netherlands invoke Art. 21(4) EUMR (legitimate interests, public security)? Not checked here. FSR applicability is our inference from Arts 3, 20 and 25 and should be tested against Commission FSR practice on state-backed acquirers.
- EU001 and lithography in practice: confirm with Dutch licensing data or ASML filings that EUV and 3B501.f.1.b tools ship to the US under EU001, and whether US-bound 3B901f6 tools move under a Dutch national general authorisation. Check whether the Netherlands could delay US-bound shipments under Art. 21(3) of Reg. 2021/821 (temporary suspension) or Art. 347 TFEU.
- Netherlands: the current minister responsible for export licensing after the 2025 election and government formation; whether servicing (spare parts, software updates, technical assistance) to China needs separate Dutch authorisation; the role of the Central Import and Export Service (CDIU).
- Nexperia and the Goods Availability Act: dates of the autumn 2025 order and its suspension were not checked here. Chips Act 2.0 confirms only that "the Nexperia case" is cited as a supply shock.
- Dutch screening (Vifo) Act: entry into force, the competent authority (BTI), and whether the delayed decree adding AI and other technologies has been adopted since SWD(2026) 290.
- US: any formal rescission or replacement of the AI Diffusion Rule; whether any EU member state sits in Country Groups D:1, D:4 or D:5 for the purpose of the current enforcement policy (Cyprus was not checked); whether the American AI Exports Program has designated packages for EU states.
- Council follow-up to the 2024 White Paper (has the senior-level forum met?) and the timeline for the dual-use evaluation and any 2027 proposal.
- Effective voting threshold: given consensus norms (Heisenberg 2005; Häge 2013; Novak 2013), would an ACI determination go to a vote, or would the Commission withdraw once a blocking minority is visible? This decides whether to model formal QMV or a supermajority norm.
- Eurostat projections for 2028-2031 to check that the large-state pivot result holds under scenario-year weights.
