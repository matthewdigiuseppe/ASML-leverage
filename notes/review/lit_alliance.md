## Synthesis (edited)

**Asymmetric alliance theory, not chokepoint theory, as the backbone.** We read the ASML case as a repricing of Morrow's (1991) autonomy-for-security trade inside an alignment auction. Morrow models alliances in which one partner gains security and the other gains autonomy. He shows that such asymmetric alliances form more easily and last longer than symmetric ones, and that large changes in members' capabilities make any alliance more likely to break. Dutch export licensing and ASML servicing decisions are a form of the autonomy Europe supplies. The 2028 to 2031 sequence in Europe 2031 is Washington demanding more autonomy for an unchanged or degraded security good.

Wagner (1988) supplies the key discipline. Asymmetric economic interdependence yields political influence only when exchanging economic resources for political concessions leaves both parties better off than bargaining over the economic gains alone. Whether that holds "is independent of the degree of asymmetry in the economic relationship, or its direction." Our Proposition 1 carries Wagner's insight into an auction with a third bidder: Europe's rent equals its best exit premium and does not depend on how much the US needs ASML. Its formal ancestor is the menu auction of Bernheim and Whinston (1986). There, principals bid contingent payment schedules for an agent's action, and refined (truthful) equilibria implement the efficient action. In a two-bidder, binary-action case the winner's payment is pinned down by the loser's willingness to pay.

This pushes against the Europe 2031 epilogue, which equates indispensability with leverage. Indispensability raises Washington's willingness to pay; it does not set the price. In our model the price is set by China's net bid after the US has taxed every European exit option with security, jurisdictional and AI-access penalties. Gowa and Mansfield (1993) explain why a patron taxes exit: trade has security externalities, so free trade is more likely within alliances than across them.

**Security dependence through the conditionality of protection, not its level.** Snyder (1984) frames the ally's problem as a choice between support and nonsupport under competing fears of abandonment and entrapment. That choice interacts with the adversary game of firmness versus conciliation. Gerzhoy (2015) shows that alliance coercion, meaning conditional threats of military abandonment, restrained West Germany's nuclear ambitions. It worked because Bonn was militarily dependent and Washington assured it that abandonment was conditional on its nuclear choices. Blankenship (2021) finds that US burden-sharing pressure works better when Washington can credibly threaten to reduce protection and doing so would be costly to the ally. Geographically vulnerable allies contribute more; allies in locations of high strategic value to the United States contribute less. Lanoszka (2018) is more skeptical. Allies judge US guarantees by in-theater conventional deployments, and alliance coercion is much less useful for curbing an actual nuclear program than for preventing a potential one. Benson (2012) shows that a patron may rationally prefer ambiguous commitments, which transfer risk to the ally and curb its aggressiveness.

These claims can be reconciled once protection is written as the difference between the guarantee to a compliant ally and the guarantee to a defector. A patron that makes its guarantee uncertain for everyone reduces its own leverage over ASML; leverage requires targeted unreliability. Europe 2031 assumes that US unreliability and US leverage rise together. Our model should show that they trade off unless Washington can make abandonment selective. Beckley (2015) shows that the United States rarely gets entangled because it exploits loopholes, sidesteps commitments that imperil its interests and plays allies' demands off one another. Kim (2011) shows that leaders anticipate entrapment and design alliance agreements to limit it. Our inference, not theirs, is that the same escape options discount the guarantee compliant allies can count on. Blankenship's finding on strategically valuable allies points the same way: an ally that holds something the patron needs is harder to pressure.

**Issue linkage cuts both ways.** Poast (2012) finds that offers of trade linkage raised the probability that European alliance negotiations between 1860 and 1945 reached agreement. Poast (2013) finds that buffer states in alliances with trade provisions suffered fewer opportunistic violations and were invaded or occupied less often. Long and Leeds (2006) find that pre-1945 European allies traded more than non-allies only when their treaties specified economic cooperation. Davis (2004) shows that institutionalized linkage between agricultural and industrial issues helped overcome domestic resistance to liberalization, so institutions make linkage more credible. Davis (2009) documents linkage in both directions in the Anglo-Japanese alliance: economic side payments to reinforce security cooperation, and the alliance used as leverage in tariff bargaining.

Lohmann (1997) supplies the dark side: once punishment strategies are linked, a defection in one game can trigger breakdown in another. Our inference is that once Washington links ASML to NATO, Europe cannot threaten with ASML without putting its security at risk, so US linkage disarms the European asset. Carnegie (2014) shows that states forgo productive exchange when it would let a politically dissimilar partner hold them up for political concessions. The US worry about depending on an asset held by a partner whose alignment may drift is a hold-up problem of this kind.

Lake (1996) gives the remedy that powerful states reach for: relational hierarchy, a transfer of residual rights of control. Hierarchy is chosen when the expected costs of opportunism, which fall as hierarchy rises, outweigh the governance costs, which rise with it. Lake (2009) develops hierarchy as relational authority grounded in a social contract that binds both dominant and subordinate states. The 2031 joint holding company is hierarchy made explicit. Powell's (2006) account of commitment problems arising from large, rapid shifts in power suggests why the demand comes when it does. Norrlof (2010) explains why Washington can bundle: its advantages in trade, money and security reinforce one another.

The scenario's line that the Pentagon treats losing control of ASML as comparable to nuclear proliferation makes Gerzhoy and Lanoszka the closest empirical analogs, with one difference. In nonproliferation the client seeks a capability; here the client already holds it and the patron seeks to strip its discretion. Lanoszka's finding that alliance coercion struggles against programs that already exist suggests why a patron would escalate from conditional threats to a demand for direct control.

**Collective action is where the formal literature helps most, and where our current Proposition 3 is too generous to Europe.** Crawford (2008, 2011) develops wedge strategies. Selective accommodation, meaning concessions, compensations and other inducements, detaches potential members of a hostile coalition. Expansionists use it to prevent or break up blocking coalitions. Olson and Zeckhauser (1966) show that when alliance goods are public, they are underprovided and larger members bear a disproportionate share. Sandler and Hartley (2001) survey the later joint-product literature, in which alliance activities also yield ally-specific private benefits. Tier 1 status for the Netherlands is an excludable benefit that turns a public good into a side payment. Henke (2019) shows that pivotal states systematically make such payments, in cash or through political side deals, and that states seen as critical contributors are among the likeliest recipients.

Vote-buying models go further. Dal Bó (2007) shows that an outside party can manipulate a committee's decisions at no cost if it can reward decisive votes differently. He also analyses when secret voting and other designs insulate committees. Groseclose and Snyder (1996) show that when vote buyers move sequentially and the loser gets a last chance to counterattack, equilibrium coalitions are generally larger than minimal winning. Meunier (2000) argues that EU voting rules and negotiating competence shape external outcomes differently in two contexts. In conservative contexts, where the EU defends the status quo, restrictive rules strengthen its hand; in reformist contexts, where it seeks change, they weaken it.

The scenario's 2029 Anti-Coercion Instrument vote is the worst case on all three counts. It is a reformist measure, national positions in the vote are reported, and the patron can offer outcome-contingent rewards to security-dependent members. The model should therefore let Europe choose institutions. Putting any decision that concedes ASML control under a rule that requires broad consent is a form of self-binding that raises the US price.

**Pivotal-state and hedging theories fit Europe poorly, and the model should say why.** Crawford's (2003) pivotal deterrence requires that the pivot can credibly align with either side. Lim and Cooper (2015) redefine hedging as signaling that creates ambiguity about how far a secondary state shares great powers' security interests. They argue that existing treaty alliances with the United States create path dependencies that favour balancing over hedging. Tessman (2012) offers a different concept: strategic hedging by second-tier states, including France in 2003, when a preponderant leader is in relative decline. One purpose is insurance against losing public goods the leader now provides. Europe's rearmament and sovereign-compute investments fit Tessman's concept. Its commercial hedge toward China (DUV sales and servicing) is what Lim and Cooper's logic predicts a US treaty ally facing Russia will struggle to sustain.

China cannot bid security because it is aligned with Russia. The auction is therefore asymmetric: China bids with markets and inputs, the US with protection and AI access. Crawford (2011) holds that accommodation is the wedge that works. Izumikawa (2013) explains why dividers nonetheless turn to coercion: they do so when they see a grave threat in the target's strong alignment with their primary enemy and lack enough reward power to split it. That matches the scenario's 2031 Chinese turn closely. Beijing concludes "that its charm offensive is not enough" and ties threats to the Dutch signing with Washington. China's coercion is therefore coherent on Izumikawa's terms, whether or not Beijing's preferred outcome is that Europe chooses neither side. In our model, Chinese coercion against compliance raises the US payment one for one but still lowers Europe's welfare. Whether it deters the transfer of control is an equilibrium question, not a contradiction in the scenario.

**Europe's record against US extraterritoriality is poor.** The United States withdrew from the JCPOA on 8 May 2018. The Commission then updated the 1996 Blocking Statute, which took effect on 7 August 2018, and France, Germany and the UK created INSTEX in January 2019. INSTEX concluded its first transaction, a medical export to Iran, only on 31 March 2020, and its shareholders decided to dissolve it in March 2023. Ruys and Ryngaert (2020) argue three things: US secondary sanctions force foreign firms to choose between the target and the US market; the international lawfulness of some of them is doubtful; and the effectiveness of European responses such as blocking statutes and special purpose vehicles is uncertain. Drezner (1999) adds that senders are reluctant to coerce allies but extract significant concessions when they do, because allies expect continued interaction. Rising transatlantic conflict expectations should therefore make Washington readier to coerce and Europe less ready to concede. ASML differs from the Iran case because the state controls the asset through export licensing. But ASML's own US exposure means that a European threat carried out through the firm rather than the state lacks credibility.

**Middle-power coalitions.** Keohane (1971) shows how small allies can gain disproportionate influence over a superpower patron. Carr (2014) proposes a "systemic impact" definition of middle powers. Carney's January 2026 Davos speech argues that middle powers must act together, because "when we only negotiate bilaterally with a hegemon, we negotiate from weakness." Coalitions of this kind help only if members' assets are complements and the coalition can pay its own weakest members. Otherwise Washington can deal with them one at a time. That is the logic of Cha's (2010) "powerplay", bilateral asymmetric alliances designed to give the patron maximum control over the smaller ally, and it is how the scenario's Japan and Korea are kept apart from the Dutch.

## Modeling implications

1. **Proposition 1.** Keep the sequential alignment auction (US offers first, China second, Europe chooses). Present Proposition 1 as an extension of Wagner (1988) to a three-party auction, with Bernheim and Whinston (1986) as the formal ancestor. Europe's rent equals its best exit premium and does not depend on the US valuation V. State it explicitly as a rebuttal of the epilogue's claim that indispensability equals leverage.
2. **Conditional protection.** Write security dependence as conditional protection, kappa = pi_A - pi_a, not as the level S. Add an extension in which the US first chooses between general ambiguity (Benson 2012; pi_A falls for everyone) and targeted conditionality (Gerzhoy 2015; only pi_a falls). Ambiguity that limits entrapment lowers US leverage over ASML.
3. **Valuable allies resist more.** Blankenship (2021) finds that allies in locations of high strategic value to the US contribute less under pressure. This is a direct empirical analog to an ally holding a monopoly asset. Use it to motivate a term in which the asset's value to the patron raises the ally's resistance, not only the patron's willingness to pay.
4. **Coercion versus control.** Lanoszka (2018) finds that alliance coercion works much less well against an existing capability than against a potential one. Use this, together with Lake (1996) on residual rights of control, Carnegie (2014) on political hold-up and Powell (2006) on commitment problems from rapid shifts, to motivate the preventive-control proposition: once Europe holds the asset, conditional threats are weak, and the patron moves to demand control itself. Stress the inversion relative to nonproliferation: the client already holds the capability.
5. **Vote buying in the QMV result (Proposition 3c).** With national positions reported and US offers that depend on the outcome (Tier 1 status, basing), blocking can cost the US almost nothing (Dal Bó 2007). If the pro-ACI side can counter-bid last, the US buys more than a minimal blocking minority (Groseclose and Snyder 1996). Report the cheapest-blocking-minority cost as an upper bound and derive insulation conditions (secret or delegated decisions).
6. **Institutional choice.** Add an institutional-choice stage before US offers, following Meunier (2000). Europe can place a decision that concedes ASML control under a rule requiring broad consent (a conservative context), which raises the US price. Contrast this with the 2029 ACI vote, a reformist measure that needs QMV to act.
7. **Excludable transfers.** Model US transfers to member states as excludable private goods (Tier 1 status, bases, deals), following Olson and Zeckhauser (1966) and the joint-product literature surveyed by Sandler and Hartley (2001). Henke (2019) supports targeting such payments at states the payer sees as critical.
8. **Asymmetric bidding and China's coercion.** China cannot bid security, so its instruments are market access, inputs (rare earths, legacy chips) and coercion r. Following Izumikawa (2013), make China's switch from rewards to coercion endogenous: China coerces when the threatened US alignment is severe (transfer of control) and its reward power is insufficient. Keep the model result that coercion against compliance raises the US payment one for one but lowers Europe's welfare. Show that coercion aimed at blocking a transfer of control (r^c) is the only Chinese move that protects European ownership.
9. **Hedging region.** Make the hedge (H) feasible only when the security cost is small, with S increasing in the Russia threat, following Lim and Cooper's (2015) argument that US treaty alliances favour balancing over hedging. Treat rearmament and sovereign compute separately, as Tessman-style (2012) insurance against losing US public goods. Rearmament lowers S over time, one of the "two clocks".
10. **Middle-power coalition.** Model the coalition as complementary assets with internal transfers. The joint exit premium must be supermodular, and the coalition must fund its weakest members; otherwise the US buys the cheapest member bilaterally (Cha 2010; Carney's January 2026 point about bilateral negotiation from weakness).
11. **State versus firm execution.** Separate European threats carried out by the state from those carried out by firms. Ruys and Ryngaert (2020) judge the effectiveness of blocking statutes and special purpose vehicles uncertain, and INSTEX completed one transaction in over four years. An ACI threat against US fabs is credible only if executed through state licensing and if the state covers ASML's FDPR exposure (f_a). Add a parameter for the share of a countermeasure that relies on firm compliance.
12. **Endogenous linkage.** Following Lohmann (1997), a European threat to use ASML against the US can trigger a security response (a fall in pi_A), so linkage disarms the asset. Following Davis (2004) and Poast (2013), Europe's self-binding (Proposition 6) can be institutionalized as a linked treaty: an allied export-control compact tied to security guarantees and AI-access commitments.
13. **Relationship discount.** Add a discount on the future US-EU relationship in the spirit of Drezner (1999). As conflict expectations rise, the US becomes readier to coerce and Europe concedes less readily, which raises the probability of breakdown in the incomplete-information proposition.
14. **Go-it-alone power.** Treat FISR tiering as Gruber-style (2000) go-it-alone power. The US can lower Europe's disagreement payoff (via alpha) before the 2031 bargaining, so the comparison Europe faces is rationed access versus Tier 3, not the old status quo.

## Open questions

1. **Search limit.** No keyword web searches were possible in this check, and the memo's original research also had none. A session with search access should confirm the items below.
2. **Crawford (2003), Pivotal Deterrence.** Title, author and year are confirmed through the Crossref reference list of Fang, Johnson and Leeds (2014). The publisher (Cornell University Press) and the book's exact conditions for pivotal deterrence come from our own knowledge. Confirm both before relying on the "credible alignment with either side" condition.
3. **INSTEX joiners.** Was Norway the sixth state in the 29 November 2019 announcement? Retrieve the primary joint statement, for example from a member government's site.
4. **INSTEX dissolution.** The AP article of 9 March 2023 and the claim that only one transaction was ever completed rest on Wikipedia's citation. Confirm from AP or an E3 statement.
5. **Characterizations from memory where no abstract was available.** Check against the texts: Bernheim and Whinston (1986) on how principals' payoffs relate to their marginal contributions; the end pages of Morrow (1991), Bernheim and Whinston (1986) and Keohane (1971); Keohane's (1971) mechanism; the joint-product discussion in Sandler and Hartley (2001); Gruber (2000); Carr's (2014) definitional criteria; Crawford's (2008) account of British and US economic inducements to Spain.
6. **Bank Melli Iran v Telekom Deutschland (CJEU, C-124/20).** This is the leading Blocking Statute case. EUR-Lex and Curia did not return content to automated retrieval, so the judgment date (believed to be 21 December 2021, Grand Chamber) and the holding remain unconfirmed. Not cited.
7. **Not verified, so not cited.** Kim (2016), The Supply Side of Security; Cooley and Spruyt (2009), Contracting States, directly relevant to the joint holding company; Snyder (1997), Alliance Politics; Cooper, Higgott and Nossal (1993) on middle powers.
8. **Novelty.** Is there a formal IR model of a small state auctioned between two great powers, beyond Bueno de Mesquita and Smith (2007) and the menu-auction and vote-buying literature? A targeted search (for example "competition for clients", "alignment auction", "bidding for allies") is needed to position the contribution.
9. **Observability of ACI votes.** The Council determines coercion by QMV, and response measures are adopted as implementing acts through comitology. Are national positions observable at each stage? The answer decides whether outcome-contingent US offers are feasible and whether insulation by secrecy is available.
10. **Real-world side payments.** Did any EU member state receive explicit, excludable US side payments tied to export-control alignment, for example in the January 2023 US-Japan-Netherlands understanding? That would calibrate the private-benefit wedge.
