## Synthesis (edited)

Europe 2031 (Juijn et al. 2026) reads best as an informal extensive-form game. Its coercive moves come in a clear order, and its bargaining logic is uneven, which makes it a useful motivating case. The scenario was published on 11 June 2026 by eight authors writing in a personal capacity, with copywriting and editing by Tom Chivers (who is credited but is not an author). It stages four rounds.

In "May 2028: The compute crunch", ASML's EUV output has risen "from sixty to eighty-five machines a year". Washington demands that The Hague end ASML's remaining DUVi sales and servicing in China. The Dutch resist because they do not want to "get dragged into a great-power conflict". The Commission backs them, but "the European position fragments before it has properly formed". Japan and South Korea are "politely unavailable" because both "remain heavily dependent on American military support". The US then threatens the Foreign Direct Product Rule, although the text concedes that "such penalties would also harm the US". "The Dutch cave", and the character Caroline notes that "we didn't even manage to negotiate a single thing in return".

In "April 2029: Access all areas", the Frontier Inference Services Rule places the Five Eyes, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and the Netherlands in Tier 1 and most of Europe in Tier 2, with Tier 2 capped at 25 per cent of any provider's frontier inference. Because European customers already take "nearly a quarter" of US frontier inference, the text itself says this means "roughly halving European allocations". In "May 2029: Tilt", the Commission opens the first-ever examination under the Anti-Coercion Instrument and finds coercion after four months, but the measure restricting ASML exports and servicing to US fabs in Arizona "falls short of the qualified majority needed". The Netherlands and Ireland oppose (transatlantic relations), Poland and the Baltics oppose (Russia), and Italy abstains. In August 2030 Chinese state-backed funds extend credit to a Portuguese infrastructure bank, refinance Greek sovereign debt and underwrite a Spanish regional government. In "March 2031: Between giants", the Pentagon treats losing control of ASML as comparable to nuclear proliferation, and the White House moves "now, while the option still exists". The vice president offers the Dutch, German and French leaders a joint Dutch-American holding with a US controlling vote over output, customers and technology transfer, plus a capital injection for production on US soil and cash transfers to European citizens indexed to US AI windfalls (perhaps EUR 100 per person per year at first). The three leaders decline without consulting the other heads of government, partly because they fear the others would push them to accept. The White House then makes the offer public and threatens to drop the whole EU to Tier 3. It claims that through Taiwanese partners it has "amassed enough EUV machines and maintenance know-how to forego ASML access for longer than Europe can forego access to frontier AI". China replies that "rare-earth export terms will be reviewed if the Dutch sign with Washington" and that robot export licences will be reassessed, on a shorter deadline. The epilogue's counterfactual is that "fifteen, maybe twenty per cent" of world compute on European soil, plus a coalition of the Netherlands, Germany, France, Norway, the UK, Canada, Japan and South Korea, would have kept Washington from acting.

Our position is that the scenario gets the structure right: asymmetric interdependence, a wasting asset, collective action under qualified majority voting, and two rival patrons. It mis-specifies where leverage comes from and when it can be used, and several key outcomes are not equilibria of the game it describes. Five points matter for model design.

First, ASML is two instruments, not one. Denial leverage over the US (withholding tools and servicing) and defection leverage (transferring tools or know-how to China) differ in credibility and in how fast they decay. The scenario says both that without ASML the US "could not keep extending its lead in AI" and that the US can outlast Europe through Taiwanese partners. If the second claim holds, the 2031 ultimatum is about denying China, not securing supply, and Europe's remaining card is the threat to defect. Three outside sources point the same way. Co-author Juijn is reported (Implicator, 21 June 2026) as saying "I don't think it actually is a choke point", because the US would not feel an export restriction immediately. Aresu (Startmag, 20 June 2026) argues ASML is not a chokepoint fully in European hands because its production chain is integrated with US partners and suppliers. Goutbeek (Chips & Power, 22 June 2026) finds that an export stop to Arizona would leave already-installed compute running. The scenario also has China mass-producing domestic DUVi about a year after March 2031. The model should separate the two instruments and let their values move with US stockpiles, China's own DUVi production and ASML's US-origin content.

Second, the 2028 capitulation is under-explained. If compliance creates surplus for the US and the US alternative (FDPR penalties) is costly to Washington, the Dutch should be able to extract a price bounded by that cost. The threat also arrives during a compute crunch, when ASML is most valuable to Washington and the threat should be least credible. Zero compensation requires either US proposal power together with a Dutch outside option no better than compliance, or Dutch beliefs that the US is a committed type. The Netherlands appears in Tier 1 in 2029 and then votes against the ACI measure. We read this as a deferred private side payment that splits the asset holder from the bloc. The scenario does not say this; it is our interpretation, and a cleaner mechanism than the "transatlantic relations" the authors give. The real 2026 MATCH Act episode suggests a complementary Dutch strategy of borrowing the EU's weight: Prime Minister Jetten raised the bill at the White House in April, trade minister Sjoerdsma lobbied Commerce Secretary Lutnick and members of Congress in June, and the Netherlands held off signing Pax Silica until the EU was on board.

Third, the collective-action story needs correct arithmetic. Under the 2026 Council population figures, the named opponents and abstainer (Netherlands, Ireland, Poland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Italy) hold 28.06 percent of EU population. Qualified majority requires at least 15 of 27 states and at least 65 percent of population in favour, and a blocking minority needs at least four states holding more than 35 percent. As written, 20 states with about 72 percent of population remain in favour, so the measure passes. This holds whether the ACI response measure needs a positive vote or only the absence of a qualified majority against it in comitology. For the vote to fail, either about 7 more percentage points of population must also vote no or abstain (Germany, France or Spain alone, or a group such as Romania, Belgium and Czechia), or six more states of any size must do so, which would leave only 14 in favour. The pivots therefore include large states with trade and security exposure and cheap small states that can be bought off to defeat the member-state threshold. They do not include the frontline states the text names. The model should use real QMV weights, both thresholds, and a security-linkage parameter.

Fourth, 2031 is a commitment problem under shifting power (Powell 2006). A drifting Europe cannot credibly promise not to sell ASML access to China, so Washington moves while it can. This opens a counterfactual the authors never consider: European commitment devices that bind Europe against the China option without handing control to Washington, such as EU-level entrenched export controls, a golden share, or escrowed servicing. The US carrot has the same problem in reverse. Cash transfers indexed to future windfalls are not credible once control has passed, and capital for production on US soil erodes Europe's future leverage. Europe should discount both heavily.

Fifth, the two-patron endgame is under-specified. China's explicit threat is conditional on the Dutch signing with Washington, yet the text says that signing with neither means Europe would "absorb the full displeasure of both great powers at once" and risk "losing frontier inference and critical manufacturing inputs at the same time". That requires a Chinese punishment for non-alignment that the text never states. A US threat that pushes Europe toward China also undercuts Washington's denial aim unless China's offer is unattractive. "Neither" is likely more robust than the authors allow.

On compute, the reception contains a disagreement a model can settle. The scenario implies that under scarcity European demand is no protection, because the US rations it. Garicano and Grimm (Silicon Continent, 14 June 2026), in an essay that does not itself discuss Europe 2031, argue that becoming major users of American models makes US restrictions less likely. Both can hold: scarcity destroys demand-side leverage but makes installed capacity on European soil valuable. Co-authors state this supply-side mechanism directly. Fox writes that hosted data centres give Europe "physical leverage over the infrastructure on which AI depends", Negele calls owned compute "a very strong bargaining chip", and Juijn told a Leuven audience that in a worst case Europe could cut power to US-owned data centres on its soil. Using that capacity as leverage creates a hold-up problem. The more credibly Europe promises not to expropriate, the less leverage the compute gives, which matches Kaltheuner's objection that "a guarantee that holds only as long as it costs Washington nothing is not a guarantee at all".

Defense dependence is thin in the text. NATO is named once, in the February 2026 Hormuz aside. Security dependence otherwise appears in Japan's and Korea's 2028 refusal, the Polish and Baltic votes in 2029, and their later drift toward the US. The US never puts the security guarantee on the table in 2031. Garicano and Grimm make the same link in their own terms: member states that rely on the American military presence "may diminish total European leverage". The guarantee should be an explicit linkage instrument in the model. That also undercuts the epilogue's coalition, because including Japan and Korea contradicts the scenario's own 2028 mechanism.

## Modeling implications

- Model ASML as two instruments with separate state variables. Denial value to the US, D_t, falls with US stockpiles and the installed base and rises with compute scarcity. Transfer or defection value to China, T_t, falls as China's own DUVi and EUV develop and is capped by ASML's US-origin content, which US de minimis and direct-product rules reach. The 2031 ultimatum targets T_t, not D_t. The scenario's own claim that the US can outlast Europe implies D_t is small by 2031. Real Chinese stockpiling (Q3 2026) shows T_t eroding on the China side as well.
- Model 2028 as sequential bargaining. The US demands. The Netherlands accepts, resists, or seeks EU or Japanese and Korean backing. The US then threatens the FDPR at a self-inflicted cost k_US(D_t). Derive the compensation the Netherlands can extract, bounded by k_US. Show that "nothing in return" requires either US commitment or proposal power with a Dutch outside option no better than compliance, or uncertainty about the US type. The threat comes when D_t, and so k_US, is highest.
- Allow private side payments to the asset holder. Tier 1 status for the Netherlands can be modeled as deferred compensation that creates a wedge stopping the EU from using the asset, so divide-and-rule emerges in equilibrium rather than being attributed to "transatlantic ties".
- Use actual 2026 Council weights and both QMV thresholds (15 of 27 states and 65 percent of population). The named opponents hold 28.06 percent and seven states, so they block neither way. The US can complete a block by adding about 7 points of population (one large state or several mid-sized ones) or six more states of any size. Identify the cheapest pivot set given security linkage and Chinese credit exposure. Model the ACI rule correctly: coercion is determined by Council QMV, while response measures are Commission implementing acts subject to comitology.
- Make the US security guarantee (NATO, the Russia threat) an explicit linkage instrument and an outside option for frontline states and for Japan and Korea. Test the epilogue's coalition for stability once the US can condition protection or tier status on defection. The scenario's own 2028 mechanism predicts the coalition is not stable.
- Treat the 2031 ultimatum as a preventive commitment problem (Powell 2006). Add European commitment devices (entrenched EU export controls, a golden share, escrowed servicing, a third-party custodian) and derive when they pre-empt the ultimatum at lower cost than ceding control.
- Model the US carrot as time-inconsistent. Windfall-indexed transfers and capital for US production are not credible once control has passed, and relocation erodes future European leverage (Coase 1972). Discount the offer by the probability of reneging.
- Model competing conditional threats from two patrons. The US punishes if Europe does not sign; China, as written, punishes only if Europe signs. Check whether "neither" is punished by both, and derive when each threat is credible given that carrying it out pushes Europe toward the rival.
- Model a war of attrition over servicing versus AI access. Europe's per-period cost of losing frontier AI rises with AI dependence; the US cost of losing ASML falls with stockpiles and Taiwanese maintenance capacity. Europe's leverage is front-loaded, which formalizes the scenario's line that the window "closed some time between 2025 and 2027".
- Model compute on European soil as a hostage with hold-up. Under scarcity, demand-side leverage is weak because Washington can ration, but installed capacity Europe could power down is valuable. Hyperscalers anticipate expropriation, so credible promises not to seize raise investment and lower leverage. This reconciles the scenario with Garicano and Grimm and formalizes Kaltheuner's credibility objection.
- Include information asymmetries in both directions. The US has private information about its stockpile and outside option. In the scenario the US also has an AI that knows European principals' cabinet discussions, which lets it make exact take-it-or-leave-it offers, while the European Council sends a delegation with a mandate "ambiguous on purpose". A screening or signaling variant can ask when Europe should call the stockpile claim and whether deliberate ambiguity helps when the other side can read it.
- Add Chinese counter-coercion aimed at the Netherlands: rare earths (truce to 10 November 2026), legacy chips (Nexperia, where The Hague suspended its order within two months), and ASML's China revenue (29.1 percent of 2025 net sales, about 20 percent expected for 2026). This raises the Dutch cost of complying with the US and predicts that the Netherlands will try to Europeanize decisions to spread costs and borrow weight, as it did with Pax Silica.
