## Main corrections to the memo

- **Four citations were mischaracterized.** Bapat and Kwon (2015) find that sanctions succeed when the sender's firms hold a *moderate* share of the target's market, and that senders often have incentives *not* to enforce. Whang, McLean and Kuberski (2013) find *no* support for the informational (resolve-revelation) mechanism; their support is for a coercive mechanism based on the target's dependence. Kalyanpur and Newman (2019) are about internal jurisdictional expansion (EU harmonization) as a shield against a rival's extraterritorial reach, not about large markets projecting jurisdiction abroad. Morrow (1999) shows that the opportunity-cost effect of trade on conflict is indeterminate, and that trade reduces escalation mainly by widening the range of costly signals.
- **The 98% magnet figure has no primary source.** Euronews reports it without attribution, and Neo Performance Materials' CEO uses "up to 98%". It should be treated as an approximate calibration value.
- **The Busan general licences are for US end users.** The White House fact sheet says they are "for the benefit of U.S. end users and their suppliers around the world". European firms benefit only indirectly.
- **The extension to 10 January 2027 is not shown to cover export controls.** MOFCOM's 28 September 2026 statement extends the Kuala Lumpur arrangement but does not mention export controls or rare earths. No amendment of Announcement 70's 10 November 2026 end date was confirmed.
- **The Global Times quote was about ASML, not the Netherlands.** The anonymous source warned that ASML "may never get China's big market back again" if Chinese customers were denied service.
- **Sjoerdsma's objection is misdated and misdescribed.** The Dutch Minister of Foreign Trade objected on 24 June 2026, not 20 August, and his complaint was about coercion. He said the bill "seems to suggest that the United States could then impose measures on us" and that cooperation should be voluntary.
- **The EU's reason for ending DS610 was different.** When it terminated the case on 28 November 2025, the EU said its key objectives had been met and trade had resumed. Reading this as an EU capitulation is an interpretation.
- **Japan was not subject to a blanket dual-use ban.** China's January 2026 ban covers dual-use exports for end uses that could enhance Japan's military capabilities.
- **Several sub-claims could not be confirmed and were removed from the synthesis.** These are: the Spanish pork exporters' sub-10% rates, the pork provisional rates, the count of 34 brandy producers with undertakings, the VW and Mercedes China delivery figures, ASML's "underserved" explanation, the MATCH Act's 2 April 2026 introduction date, and Project Vault.

## Synthesis (edited)

Our model should not treat Europe's dependence on China as a single parameter. China holds two levers with very different time profiles.

**The input lever has a short fuse.** About 98% of the EU's rare-earth permanent magnets come from China. This is a widely reported figure (Euronews, January 2026; Neo Performance Materials' CEO, September 2025) for which we have not located a primary Commission or JRC source. Eurostat's 2025 data, measured by value, put China's share at:

- 92% of EU magnesium imports;
- 77% of gallium imports;
- 68% of ferro-tungsten imports;
- 40% of imports of rare-earth compounds and metals, including scandium and yttrium (REE+). This is a broader category than finished magnets. The top three suppliers of REE+ held 96% by weight.

EU gallium imports fell 56% between 2022 and 2025, which Eurostat attributes to Chinese export restrictions. About 70% of Nexperia's output is reportedly packaged in China.

The lever works within weeks. On 4 June 2025, two months after MOFCOM Announcement 18, CLEPA said that several supplier lines had shut and that only about a quarter of licence requests had been approved. It warned that more plants would be hit within three to four weeks as inventories ran down. In October 2025, China barred exports from Nexperia China. The VDA warned of production losses, and The Guardian reported European carmakers "days away" from stoppages (reported via secondary sources; to be upgraded).

**The market lever is large in levels but shrinking.** EU goods exports to China were EUR 199.6 billion in 2025, down 6.5%, against imports of EUR 559.4 billion and a deficit of EUR 359.8 billion. China took 33% of ASML's 2025 sales. Other indicators show the decline:

- German exports to China fell 9.7% to EUR 81.3 billion in 2025.
- German brands' share of the Chinese car market fell from 26% in 2019 to 16% in 2025 (S&P Global Mobility).
- ASML expects China to be about 20% of 2026 sales. Its own results show China at 14% of net system sales in Q2 2026 and 23% in Q3 2026.

In practice China uses the market lever against sectors that are politically visible but economically small, such as brandy, pork and dairy. It uses the input lever against strategic targets. For the ASML game, the input lever is the one that matters.

**China's coercion can be calibrated, reversed and made conditional, which makes it credible.** The agri-food cases tied to the EV duties show this:

- **Dairy.** Provisional anti-subsidy duties of 21.9% to 42.7% became final duties of 7.4% to 11.7% in February 2026.
- **Pork.** Final anti-dumping duties of 4.9% to 19.8% were set in December 2025.
- **Brandy.** Final dumping margins were 27.7% to 34.9%, but MOFCOM accepted price undertakings from EU industry associations and enterprises. Participating producers are exempt while they sell above minimum prices.

Rare-earth controls also run through instruments that can be adjusted:

- **Licences** that can be granted, delayed or attached to conditions. The April 2025 controls under Announcement 18 were never suspended.
- **General licences for selected exporters** (USGS). At Busan these were promised "for the benefit of U.S. end users and their suppliers around the world", so European firms benefit only indirectly.
- **Suspensions with fixed end dates.** MOFCOM Announcement 70 suspends the October 2025 package until 10 November 2026. MOFCOM's 28 September 2026 statement extends the US-China Kuala Lumpur arrangement to 10 January 2027. That statement does not mention export controls or rare earths, and we have not confirmed a matching amendment to Announcement 70.

The October 2025 package (Announcement 61) is held in reserve. It sets a 0.1% value threshold for some foreign-made items containing Chinese-origin rare earths. It also provides case-by-case review for exports used in the R&D or production of logic chips at 14nm and below or memory with 256 layers and above, and in making production equipment, testing equipment or material inputs for such chips. Practitioners compare it with the US de minimis rule and the foreign direct product rule (FDPR).

A punishment that can be turned up and withdrawn is cheap to threaten and cheap to apply in steps. Europe's ASML lever is the opposite. Cutting off servicing is all-or-nothing, hard to reverse, and loses value over time: China's domestic immersion DUV (deep ultraviolet) programme reportedly targets about five tools in 2026 and about twenty in 2027 (The Information, via TrendForce). The model should build in this difference in reversibility and decay. We expect it to produce a difference in credibility whatever the size of the stakes.

**The Nexperia affair is the closest real case to the Europe 2031 ultimatum and should anchor the model's sequence of moves.** The sequence ran as follows:

1. A US measure came first: the BIS affiliates rule of September 2025, which the White House later agreed to suspend for one year from 10 November 2025.
2. On 30 September 2025 the Dutch government issued an order under the Goods Availability Act (Wbg), its first use since 1952.
3. China did not hit the Netherlands directly. It barred exports from Nexperia China, which put the cost on German and other European carmakers.
4. On 9 November 2025 MOFCOM granted exemptions for civilian uses. It tied the easing to a demand that the EU press the Netherlands to reverse its actions.
5. On 19 November 2025 the Dutch government suspended its order as a "constructive step". It cited "constructive meetings with the Chinese authorities" and said it had acted in close consultation with European and international partners.
6. The White House Busan fact sheet of 1 November 2025 lists, among China's commitments, measures "to ensure the resumption of trade from Nexperia's facilities in China".

Three lessons follow:

1. **China spreads the cost.** Its best response to a Dutch move is to impose costs on other member states and to ask Brussels to discipline The Hague. MOFCOM made that demand explicitly. A bilateral dispute becomes an EU collective-action problem.
2. **Europe got relief, not compensation.** The relief was a by-product of US-China bargaining, which fits the scenario's "caved with nothing in return". MOFCOM called the suspension "the first step in the right direction" with "still some way to go", and blamed the Dutch side for the disruption.
3. **Independent courts partly lock in a position.** The Enterprise Chamber's measures were separate from the Wbg order and survived its suspension. On 11 February 2026 the Chamber ordered a formal investigation and kept the CEO suspended, noting clear signs that he acted with a conflicting interest. Beijing then escalated in its own courts. On 22 May 2026 the Dongguan Intermediate People's Court accepted a Wingtech suit under the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law seeking CNY 8 billion, and in late August 2026 a Chinese court reportedly froze about USD 300 million of Nexperia's Chinese assets. The European and Chinese operations now function almost as two separate companies. The model should let a European concession be incomplete with some exogenous probability, because that changes whether China accepts it.

**China holds back on ASML because it needs ASML, and US pressure removes that reason.** Dutch controls tightened in 2023 and 2024, yet China's responses to ASML-specific measures stayed rhetorical. In March 2023 Ambassador Tan Jian said "This will not be without consequence". In September 2024 MOFCOM expressed "strong dissatisfaction" and an anonymous Global Times source warned that ASML "may never get China's big market back again". No retaliation followed. China reportedly imported about 95 DUV systems in 2025 and depends on ASML to service its installed base.

The relationship is a two-sided version of the political hold-up problem that Carnegie (2014) formalizes. Europe holds servicing over China, and China holds magnets and legacy chips over Europe. The MATCH Act would give allies 150 days to adopt US-equivalent controls. Otherwise Washington could use the FDPR to stop DUV immersion shipments and servicing at SMIC, Huawei, Hua Hong, CXMT and YMTC. The bill was attached to the NDAA, which stalled on 14 July 2026 when Senate Democrats blocked it.

US demands like these ask Europe to give up its hostage, and once servicing stops, retaliation becomes cheaper for China. Three 2026 episodes show that China punishes US allies that side with Washington on security:

- China cut supplies of several rare earths and gallium to Japan to near zero after Prime Minister Takaichi's Taiwan remarks.
- In January 2026 China banned dual-use exports to Japan for end uses that could enhance Japan's military capabilities.
- In July 2026 China put Rheinmetall and thirteen other European entities on its export-control list. They include a Dutch shipbuilding company, a research lab and a technical university.

Complying with US demands therefore costs Europe the lost ASML revenue plus the Chinese retaliation that compliance unlocks. The US offer has to cover both.

**We push back on two readings.** The first is the view that markets adjust (Gholz and Hughes 2021). Ferguson, Waldron and Lim (2023) show adjustment to Chinese *import* bans, which bears on the market lever, not the input lever. For processed magnets and heavy rare earths, what eased European shortages in 2025 was Chinese licensing relief. China's magnet exports to the EU rose 245% from May to June 2025 but were still 35% below a year earlier. Substitution has not done this work. Europe's first magnet plant (Narva: about 2,000 tonnes a year at first, up to 5,000 planned) compares with roughly 22,000 tonnes of imports. A European rare-earth mine before the late 2030s is unlikely, "if ever" (Teer 2026). Five CRMA strategic projects aim to begin rare-earth production between 2026 and 2028, and Kiruna may be a decade from mining. Over 2028 to 2031, China's input lever is close to fixed.

The second is the scenario's picture of China as an equal third pole. China mostly uses its leverage as a wedge, rewarding those who break ranks and punishing those who lead (Crawford 2011; Izumikawa 2013). Two pieces of evidence point this way. The first is differentiated duty rates and price undertakings. The second is Lithuania, the main European case in MERICS's coercion database. The EU ended its WTO case over Lithuania (DS610) on 28 November 2025, saying its objectives had been met and trade had resumed. Hungary received about a quarter of Chinese FDI into Europe in 2022 to 2024. That its new prime minister, Péter Magyar, opened an investigation into the BYD deal in July 2026 suggests the pattern of China-friendly vetoes may be shifting. In a 2031 ultimatum, China's best move is to target the member states that decide a qualified majority, not to make one offer to Europe as a bloc.

**Finally, the China side is moving against Europe.** Europe's leverage over China (ASML's China share; China's domestic DUV) is decaying faster than Europe's dependence on China (magnets). Over time that makes aligning with China a weaker outside option and strengthens the US hand, unless Europe coordinates with Japan and Korea along the lines of Cha's (2023) collective resilience. The model should also let the US offer supply insurance as a carrot. Examples are stockpiles, or folding European concerns into US-China deals as happened with Nexperia at Busan. The US can partly neutralise China's lever, and that is one more source of US bargaining power over Europe.

## Modeling implications

1. **Two Chinese levers with separate parameters.**
   - The input lever (magnets, heavy rare earths, gallium, legacy chips packaged in China) is fast, large and can be aimed at individual member states.
   - The market lever (purchases of ASML tools, cars, machinery, agri-food) is slow and shrinking. ASML's China share falls from 33% (2025) to about 20% (2026 guidance), with 14% and 23% in Q2 and Q3 2026. German brands' Chinese market share fell from 26% to 16%.
   - Europe's sensitivity to the input lever is much greater.
2. **Opposite technologies of punishment.** China's punishment is adjustable and reversible: licences, provisional-then-final duties (dairy 21.9-42.7% to 7.4-11.7%), price undertakings, and suspensions with end dates. Europe's ASML lever is lumpy, irreversible and wasting. The model should derive the gap in credibility from this difference in reversibility and decay, not just from the size of the stakes.
3. **The ASML relationship as a two-sided hold-up.** Europe holds servicing and DUV supply over China, and China holds magnets and legacy chips over Europe. A US demand to cut servicing forces Europe to give up its hostage, which lowers China's cost of retaliating. Europe's participation constraint against the US must include the expected Chinese retaliation that compliance unlocks, not only lost ASML revenue. The commonly cited servicing figure of USD 1-1.5bn is unattributed and should be treated as a rough range.
4. **China as a strategic third player with a wedge strategy.** China chooses a vector of punishments and rewards across member states to block or build a qualified majority. Calibrate on the differentiation actually observed: undertakings and firm-specific rates, Lithuania, and the EU's November 2025 exit from DS610. Member-state types should have two dimensions, US security dependence and China economic exposure.
5. **The Nexperia sequence as the timing template.** The order of moves is:
   1. US measure;
   2. Dutch action;
   3. Chinese denial imposing costs on third-party member states;
   4. MOFCOM's explicit demand that the EU discipline the Netherlands;
   5. Dutch partial retreat;
   6. relief through US-China bargaining.

   China's retaliation should be allowed to fall on member states other than the one acting, which creates an externality inside the EU coalition.
6. **A timing advantage for China.** CLEPA warned of further plant stoppages within 3-4 weeks, and ZVEI reported supplies of only weeks or months. The ACI gives the Commission normally four months to examine a case and the Council 8 to 10 weeks to determine coercion, and response measures come after that. China's punishment therefore lands before Europe's collective response, so the EU response should move after China's first-stage cost is realised.
7. **Courts as an exogenous source of incomplete concessions.** Independent domestic institutions (the Enterprise Chamber) make a European concession incomplete with exogenous probability q. This works like tying hands. It can make the concession unacceptable to China and push escalation into Chinese courts (the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law suit and the reported asset freeze), or it can strengthen the Dutch bargaining position.
8. **US insurance against Chinese retaliation as a carrot.** The US can insure Europe by including European concerns in US-China deals, as at Busan with Nexperia, or through stockpiles. The model should treat this as a further source of US leverage. Note that Busan's general licences cover US end users and their suppliers, so European firms benefit only indirectly unless the US bargains for them explicitly.
9. **Two different decay rates.** One is Europe's leverage over China: ASML's China share, and Chinese domestic DUV reportedly at about 5 tools in 2026 and 20 in 2027. The other is Europe's dependence on China: Narva at 2,000 to 5,000 t against about 22,000 t of imports, and no EU mine before the late 2030s. If leverage decays faster, the value of "align with China" falls over time and the equilibrium shifts toward the US-protectorate branch.
10. **China's own FDPR held in reserve.** China's 0.1% extraterritorial rule and its end-use review covering equipment for advanced chips are its counterpart to the US FDPR. Announcement 61 is suspended to 10 November 2026, and an extension is not confirmed. In the "sign with the US" branch, China's best response may be to target ASML's own inputs. That lowers the value to the US of controlling ASML, and with it the US's willingness to pay Europe.
11. **Calibration anchors.**
    - Magnet dependence of about 0.98 (approximate, no primary source).
    - REE+ compounds 0.40 by value.
    - Magnesium 0.92, gallium 0.77, ferro-tungsten 0.68.
    - EU 2025 deficit with China EUR 359.8bn.
    - ASML about 2.2% of Dutch value added (ING via secondary source).
    - IEA estimate of USD 3.5tn a year cost to the US and Europe from a full magnet block.

## Open questions

- Does ASML's own hardware (for example magnetic-levitation wafer stages) depend on Chinese-origin rare-earth magnets? Would Announcement 61's end-use review for equipment used to make chips at 14nm and below reach ASML directly if the suspension lapses?
- Will Announcement 70 (ending 10 November 2026) be formally extended to match the 28 September 2026 MOFCOM statement on the Kuala Lumpur arrangement? Will the suspension of the gallium, germanium and antimony ban (ending 27 November 2026) be renewed? The MOFCOM announcement number for that suspension, and whether the military end-user ban and licence requirement remain, need a primary source.
- Does the MATCH Act survive in the final FY2027 NDAA, with or without the servicing ban? Its bill number and introduction date need confirmation from congress.gov. Has China issued an explicit threat at the Netherlands or ASML over it?
- Which Dutch shipbuilding entity did China list in July 2026, and has China taken any formal measure against ASML or other Dutch semiconductor firms?
- How much European auto production did the Nexperia disruption cost in October and November 2025 (units or EUR)? This needs ACEA or company statements.
- What is the primary Commission or JRC source for the 98% magnet figure? Eurostat's 40% covers REE+ compounds, not finished magnets (HS 8505.11), so the magnet-specific import share needs its own source.
- The pork provisional rates (reported as 15.6% to 62.4%), the claim that Spanish-owned exporters received rates under 10%, the count of 34 brandy producers with undertakings, and the dairy provisional collection date (23 December 2025) all need MOFCOM primary texts.
- What date did the Enterprise Chamber rule in October 2025 (reported as 7 October)? The ECLI number should be confirmed. The asset freeze and the Tata partnership should be upgraded from Wikipedia to Reuters or SCMP.
- What were China's shares of ASML system sales in Q1 2026 (19%) and Q3 2025 (reported 42%)? These should be confirmed from ASML 6-K presentations.
- Project Vault (the US critical-minerals stockpile the memo proposes as a carrot) was not verified in this session and was removed from the synthesis.
- Was the ACI used before October 2026? This needs confirmation, and Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2023/2675 should be read for the voting rule.
- For the mutual-hostage framing, a classic source such as Williamson's work on hostages in credible commitments should be confirmed before citing. Carnegie (2014) is about forgone investment under one-sided hold-up, not mutual hostages.
- Hirschman (1945) and Wagner (1988) were not verified here and should be confirmed before citing.
- How did member states vote on the 2024 EV duties? Did Hungary's 2026 change of government alter the pattern of China-friendly vetoes in the Council? The date Magyar took office needs a primary source.

- **Mischaracterized (the work exists; use the accurate claim):**
  - **bapat2015sanctions.** Senders often lack incentives to enforce. Sanctions are more likely to succeed when the sender's firms hold a moderate share of the target's market, and they tend to be imposed when conditions do not favour success.
  - **whang2013coercion.** No support for the informational hypothesis. There is robust support for a coercive mechanism based on the target's dependence and the sender's ability to exploit it.
  - **kalyanpur2019mobilizing.** Market power depends on the institutions that govern markets. EU harmonization (jurisdictional expansion inside the EU) curtailed US extraterritorial pressure after Sarbanes-Oxley.
  - **morrow1999trade.** The effect of trade on conflict through opportunity costs is indeterminate. Trade may reduce escalation by widening the range of costly signals.
- **Unverifiable:** none.
