\section{Numerical Verification}\label{app:numerics}

We checked every result in \cref{app:proofs} against solutions computed by brute force from the primitives of the model. The scripts are in the folder \texttt{code/checks} and run with \texttt{python3 -I} (some need numpy or scipy); each finishes in under thirty seconds. No solver uses the formula it checks. Equilibria are found by enumeration, backward induction or direct optimization, and the closed forms are computed separately and compared with the result. Where a statement depends on a tie-breaking rule, the script either solves the game under that rule or computes every equilibrium outcome with no tie-breaking at all. Comments inside the scripts number the results as in our verification notes; the file \texttt{code/checks/README.md} maps them to the numbering used here.

\paragraph{\Cref{lem:bidding}.} \texttt{v2\_m1\_lemma1.py} works in exact rational arithmetic and solves three versions of the auction. In the clock auction, 300 random draws on an integer lattice ($\lambda_C\in\{1,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac34,\tfrac13,\tfrac56\}$, caps binding or not, $Q$ negative, zero or positive) enumerate all pairs of drop levels on a grid of mesh $1/12$. Dropping at one's maximal credible bid is weakly dominant in every draw, and Europe's payoff matches $\pos{\min\{Q,V,\bar m_U\}}$ exactly (draws with $Q=V$ excluded). In the alternating-raise auction, 800 games (400 draws, each with either bidder moving first) are solved for the full set of pure subgame-perfect outcomes by set-valued backward induction, with no tie-breaking. The winner matches the lemma in all 580 games with $Q$ more than one grid step below $\bar z_U$ and in all 44 with $Q$ more than one step above it; the largest payoff to Europe across equilibria is within one grid step of $\pos{\min\{Q,V,\bar m_U\}}$ in all 800; and in 108 games the smallest is lower by more than a grid step (for example $Q=\tfrac72$, with equilibrium payoffs $0,1,2,3,4$), which is the price indeterminacy of the claim in \cref{app:proofs}. Single-valued backward induction on larger grids under four tie-breaking rules (70 draws each) finds no failure of the winner, and when the losing bidder raises whenever indifferent the price is within one grid step of $\pos{Q}$. Edge cases: $Q<0$, $Q=0$, $Q=\bar m_U<V$, $Q=V$, $\bar m_U<Q<V$ (exit in every equilibrium, and every $\tau\in[Q,V)$ makes both Europe and Washington better off), $Q>V$, a binding cap $\bar m_C<W_C$, and $\lambda_C=\tfrac12$.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:master} and \cref{cor:master}.} \texttt{v2\_m1\_prop1.py} runs finite-horizon backward induction in which each proposer compares every offer on a 601-point grid, together with the responder's point of indifference, with the payoff of an unacceptable offer; Europe compares exit with continuation; and Washington's value is tracked separately rather than set to minus Europe's. Over 500 runs (250 draws with two random terminal values each, $\Delta\in\{1,0.5,0.2,0.1\}$, $\beta=0$ and $\lambda=0$ included), the largest gap between Europe's value and $x^*(\Delta)$ is $1.7\times10^{-11}$, the largest sum of the two values is $2.9\times10^{-11}$, and with $\lambda>0$ no proposer ever weakly prefers delay. For the limit, backward induction at $\Delta=0.004,0.002,0.001$ with Richardson extrapolation on 120 draws ($\beta\le 0.9$) gives a largest error against $P^*$ of $7.5\times10^{-6}$. Uniqueness is checked in two ways: limits of backward induction from six terminal conditions, including infeasible ones, differ by at most $4.5\times10^{-12}$ (60 draws), and iterating the four supremum and infimum maps of Step 4 from the box $[-10V,10V]^2$ converges to $(x^*,x^*,-x^*,-x^*)$ within $4.4\times10^{-12}$ (300 draws). At $\lambda=0$ the stationary profiles with delay pass a one-shot deviation check. Using the numerical limits, parts (a) to (c) of \cref{cor:master} hold in every draw (120 draws; 43 with $\lambda=0$; 59 with $\beta=0$); central finite differences in $V$ match $\beta\psi$ to $6.6\times10^{-10}$ where the second term binds (25 draws); the threshold $\hat Q$ classifies every draw; and the nesting limits are approached as stated. For the alternative timing of the footnote, the stationary value matches its piecewise formula to $1.6\times10^{-14}$ on 300 draws (42 with mixing), the nonstationary profile that gives Europe close to $V$ passes a one-shot deviation check in all 67 draws in which it was built, and at $\Delta=10^{-5}$ the stationary value is within $5\times10^{-5}$ of $P^*$. Near $\beta=1$ (with $Q=1.5$, $V=2$, $\lambda=r=1$, so $\psi V<Q$), the gap $P^*-x^*(\Delta)$ follows the approximation in \cref{app:proofs} and is still $0.33$ at $\beta=0.999$ and $\Delta=10^{-3}$. Finally, $Q=-1$, $V=1$, $\beta=\tfrac14$, $\lambda=r=1$ give $P^*=-\tfrac14$ both by backward induction and by the formula.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:slack}, \cref{cor:rationing} and \cref{prop:counter}(b).} \texttt{v2\_m3\_prop2.py} draws instances with one to four instruments and one to four demands. Set functions are additive (40 percent of draws) or general monotone, built so that the conditional part is often not monotone; $Q$ is negative, zero or positive; demands are reversible or sunk; execution costs are zero or positive; and integer draws create ties. Payoffs come only from the final-state primitives. The full set of subgame-perfect outcomes of the adaptive protocol is computed by set-valued backward induction over states (instruments not yet withdrawn, demands conceded), with every ultimatum allowed and Europe either accepting when indifferent or, in a separate run, free to respond either way. A separate solver finds the best single ultimatum. The bite $B(J)$ and the loss $\min\{T,\Lambda\}$ match to $8.9\times10^{-16}$ over 3,000 instances, and the acceptance rule of part (b) has no mismatch in 123,300 pairs of threat set and bundle. The bound of part (c) is never violated in 4,000 adaptive instances (1,013 with $Q>0$; largest excess $1.1\times10^{-16}$). Washington's equilibrium payoff never exceeds $V_1$ and falls strictly below it in 325 of 2,000 continuous instances. Where a sufficient condition of part (d) holds (1,593 instances), the adaptive and single-ultimatum values coincide in every case, and the ratchet count $n\bmod(b+1)$ matches in all 300 draws; with $n=1,\dots,6$ identical demands ($\tau=1$, $B(J^c)=1.5$) the adaptive protocol yields $2,0,2,0,2,0$ against $2$ for a single ultimatum. When a refusal can make a new instrument credible, Europe's loss exceeds the bite of the initial credible set by up to $4.9$ but never that of the enlarged set, which is why the proposition fixes $J^c$. For \cref{cor:rationing}, a plain knapsack with capacity $\min\{\Lambda,B(J^c)\}$ misses Washington's optimum in 521 of 1,046 instances with reversible demands only, and the stated program in none. For \cref{prop:counter}(b), the linkage condition has no mismatch in 3,000 draws with the hedge as the only demand. The hand-picked cases include $B(J^c)=b\tau$ exactly, $Q>0$, the example of a deliberately burned instrument and the knapsack counterexample.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:counter}(a).} \texttt{v2\_m3\_prop2e.py} lets China choose counter-threats from a grid (four or five levels on each demand it can threaten) before Washington's single bundled ultimatum. Over 4,000 instances the acceptance rule of (a)(i) has no mismatch in 13,286 checks. China gains in 615 instances, in every one of them by changing Washington's demand set. A counter-threat is executed on the path in 140 instances (151 threats); dropping any one of them would change Washington's demand set, and each crowds out another demand. Washington never gains. Europe's loss rises in 89 instances, falls in 405 and is unchanged in 3,506, and with a divisible sunk demand that China cannot threaten it equals $B(J^c)$ to $8.9\times10^{-16}$ with and without threats. The numerical example of the text is reproduced, and in 300 instances of the adaptive protocol every executed counter-threat also changes the demand set.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:conditionality}.} \texttt{v2\_m3\_prop3.py} draws 1,500 problems with $H$ from six convex families (quadratic with and without a linear term, exponential, power with exponent $1.5$ to $4$, kinked piecewise linear, and linear, which produces ties), $\kappa_0\in[0,0.3]$, $\bar\kappa-\kappa_0\in[0.05,0.75]$, $S_k\in[0.5,10]$, $Q_0$ negative, zero or positive, and $\nu$ equal to $0$, $1$ or uniform on $[0,1]$. Each problem is maximized on a grid of 200,001 points with golden-section polishing, and the thresholds come from bisection on $H'$. The formula for $\kappa^*$ is never beaten (largest payoff shortfall $8.9\times10^{-16}$; largest gap in the maximizer $3.3\times10^{-8}$ when it is unique), and all five regimes occur (the $\kappa_0$ corner 381 times, $\kappa_1<\kappa_z$ 257, $\kappa_\nu>\kappa_z$ 173, $\kappa_z$ 187, the cap $\bar\kappa$ 502). Finite differences in $Q_0$ over strictly convex draws give exactly the derivatives of part (c) in each regime. For part (d), 3,000 draws of $(s_X,s_L,\bar m_U)$ per distribution show $\bar\kappa$ rising with $s_X$ in 1,218 normal, 1,198 logistic, 974 exponential and 973 uniform cases, and the derivative formula matches finite differences to $3\times10^{-11}$.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:control} and \cref{cor:control}(a) to (c).} \texttt{v2\_m4\_prop4.py} solves the two-period game by backward induction from primitives: Europe's investment by comparing its two payoffs, the period rents from the bidding benchmark, Washington's execution decision after a refusal by comparing ex post payoffs that include the forfeited stake, its withdrawal decision after acceptance, its stake in $\{0,\bar m_U\}$ and payment on a grid, Europe's response, and the issue decision by strict comparison with no ultimatum. The draws are nine hand-picked edge cases (both premia zero; $\sigma+r^c=B_1$; $\Gamma=0$; $e=\bar m_U$; the investment tie; $g_1=0$; finite $\Delta_c<Q_2$; an unconditional bite with $Q_1>0$; a purchase without bite), 20,000 draws of small integers and dyadic $\delta$ on a grid of mesh $1/64$, so that all ties are exact, and 10,000 continuous draws on a grid of mesh $10^{-3}$ (319 draws within $3\times10^{-3}$ of $\Gamma=0$ set aside). The issue decision never differs from the proposition. The errors in $\Gamma$, $T^*$ and Europe's payoff are exactly zero on the integer draws and below the grid mesh on the continuous ones. Of 7,715 draws with an ultimatum, none has a refusal on the path. Under the opposite tie rule for investment, the answer changes in 139 knife-edge draws, and the formulas with a weak inequality in $I$ match all of 5,000 further draws. In 2,000 draws the execution decision after a refusal is the same for eight values of $\Delta_c$. On the same draws: no issued ultimatum violates the conditions of \cref{cor:control}(a); the identity of (b) holds to $2.7\times10^{-15}$, and $\Gamma$ with the investment option is never below $\Gamma$ without it; the purchase without bite gives $\Gamma=1$ and $T^*=2.5$; in 3,000 draws solved for $\Delta_c\in\{0,0.5,1,2,3,4,6,9,\infty\}$, $\Gamma$ never falls as $\Delta_c$ rises (part (c)); and the special case after \cref{prop:control} matches in 1,504 draws, exactly on integer draws.

\paragraph{\Cref{cor:control}(d).} \texttt{v2\_m4\_prop4\_china.py} adds a first stage in which China announces a punishment $r$ on a grid (step $\tfrac12$, up to $\bar r\le 6$) and a stake in $\{0,\bar m_C\}$, with $c_C(r)=\gamma r$, $\gamma\in\{\tfrac12,1,2\}$ and $H_C\in\{0,\tfrac14,1\}$, and solves the full game for each announcement. In 5,000 integer draws, every China-optimal announcement satisfies (i) to (iii), and the payment on the path matches $\pos{\Delta R_H-B_1+\sigma}$ (529 draws with a transfer, 394 with deterrence). With $H_C=0$ the outcome is not unique in 183 draws, as (iv) allows. With a self-rewarding punishment ($\gamma=-\tfrac12$), China punishes a transfer on the path in 968 draws.

\paragraph{\Cref{lem:selflimit} and \cref{cor:windows}.} \texttt{v2\_m4\_cuts\_windows.py}, part A, draws 20,000 games with one to five dates, random cut sets, unconditional parts zero or positive, and $\delta$ equal to one or random. Europe's loss and Washington's cost are computed by comparing, date by date, the outcomes of the bidding contest with and without the cut. In the 13,927 draws inside the lemma's scope the largest errors are $3.6\times10^{-15}$ for the bite and $1.8\times10^{-15}$ for the cost, and the identities of part (iii) hold to $1.3\times10^{-15}$. In all 6,073 draws outside the scope the true loss exceeds the formula, and the investment example gives a bite of $0.5$ against the formula's $1$. Part B solves the multi-date game by backward induction in exact rational arithmetic, with execution decided by comparing Washington's ex post payoffs and a stake in $\{0,\bar m_U\}$. It draws 60,000 games with two to six dates, integer premia in $[-6,4]$, $\delta\in\{1,\tfrac12,\tfrac9{10}\}$ and unconditional parts possible in 24,111, each solved with $\iota=0$ and strict issuing and with $\iota=10^{-9}$. The condition with $t'>t$ misclassifies 1,030 draws, all with some $u_t>0$; the condition of \cref{cor:windows} matches every draw for both values of $\iota$. Of the 20,199 games with an ultimatum, 13,923 issue it at the last eligible date and 1,202 at a date that is not itself eligible. No refused ultimatum is ever optimal.

\paragraph{\Cref{rem:bind}.} \texttt{v2\_m4\_selfbind.py} solves 6,000 integer draws with $Q_1<0$, $\Delta_c=\infty$ and no investment, with and without binding, using the solver of \texttt{v2\_m4\_prop4.py} with shifted premia and the bite recomputed from primitives. Part (a) has no mismatch. The payoff comparison of part (b) matches all 6,000 draws, whereas a comparison of $-\sigma^b-r^b$ with Europe's payoff under the ultimatum alone fails in 999, none of them a case in which Washington would issue and binding pre-empts it. Partial binding ($\Delta_b\in\{1,\dots,5\}$) invites an ultimatum that would not otherwise be issued in 210 draws, and the example of part (c) gives no ultimatum and a payoff of $10$ to Europe without binding, and an ultimatum with $T^*=4.5$ and a payoff of $3$ before $\sigma^b$ with $\Delta_b=3$. Washington's choice between full control and a dual key matches part (d) in every draw (dual key chosen in 370).

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:breakdown}.} \texttt{v2\_m4\_prop5.py} maximizes Washington's gain directly over the payment $T\ge 0$ (a grid of 4,001 points, local refinement and an explicit corner at $T=0$), for both the threat-backed ultimatum and the purchase offer, without using the first-order condition. It covers six families of distributions for $\sigma$: uniform, truncated normal, Beta(2,2), triangular, truncated exponential with a rate of either sign, and a bimodal normal mixture that is not log-concave, with 1,500 draws each ($\bar\sigma\in[0.5,8]$, $B_1\in(0,6)$, $r^c$ zero or in $[0,3]$ so that $\tilde b<0$ occurs, $e\in(0,6)$, $v_c$ zero or in $[0,4]$, $\iota$ zero or in $[0,1]$, $R$ zero or in $[0,6]$). For the five log-concave families, after setting aside 40 to 53 draws per family within $10^{-6}$ of the issuance threshold, the issuance condition of part (c) matches in every draw, the largest error in the cutoff is $3.4\times10^{-7}$, and the largest error in $P_b$ is $2.2\times10^{-7}$. The corner $T=0$ binds in 293 to 388 draws per family. The purchase offer strictly beats the threat in 104 to 133 draws per family and never when $v_c\le r^c$. For the bimodal family the first-order characterization fails in 124 draws, as expected. For the comparative statics, each parameter is raised by a random step in $[0.05,1.5]$ in 500 draws per family, keeping comparisons in which Washington issues before and after. For threat-backed ultimatums there is no violation of part (d) in any family, the bimodal one included. With the purchase option, violations occur only in the directions that part (e) says can fail, and the four examples in the proof are reproduced.

\paragraph{\Cref{rem:gatekeeper,rem:firm,rem:council}.} \texttt{v2\_m2\_remarks\_GFE.py} checks the three remarks. For \cref{rem:gatekeeper} it finds by brute force, on a grid of mesh $10^{-3}$, the smallest staked promise that keeps the gatekeeper aligned and the largest reversible concession it accepts, before and after the benefit, in 20,000 draws that include $Q_{NL}=0$, $x_h=0$ and $x_h=|Q_{NL}|$; the largest errors are $4.4\times10^{-16}$ for the payment and $8.9\times10^{-16}$ for the slack, and a benefit paid as a transfer never saves Washington anything. For \cref{rem:firm}, 200,000 integer draws, many of them ties, reproduce the firm's choice and Europe's effective premium in every case. For \cref{rem:council} the script reads the population shares of Council Decision 2025/2427 \citep{council2025population} from \texttt{pop\_check.py}, which must sit in the same folder. The first-stage blocking rule matches the treaty rule, including the four-member clause, on all 403,168 sets of members tested, and the rule for keeping a delegated act in force matches on 241,708 sets. The script computes the coalitions of \cref{tab:council} and the cost $\Phi(0)$ exactly, taking the cheapest thirteen members for the route by number and solving a dynamic program over member count and population for the route by population; the program matches brute force on 30 random fourteen-member councils to $1.8\times10^{-15}$.

\paragraph{Council arithmetic for the scenario.} \texttt{council\_revealed\_blocking.py} uses the exact populations of Council Decision 2025/2427 and the EUROPOP2023 baseline projections for 2029 to 2031, and applies the qualified-majority rule in integer arithmetic. For the coalition named in the \emph{Europe 2031} scenario it reports the population share (28.06 percent in 2026, 27.76 percent in 2031) and the members whose addition would complete a block; for two orderings of members, by revealed positions in 2025 and 2026 and by structural exposure, the first blocking prefix; blocking under informal supermajority norms; and exact Shapley--Shubik and normalized Banzhaf indices, computed by counting swing coalitions and checked against a Monte Carlo estimate from 200,000 random orderings. \Cref{sec:council} and \cref{app:scenario} use these numbers.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:pain}.} \texttt{v2\_m2\_prop6\_holdup.py} solves the discrete game by backward induction on the grid of offer dates, without using the formula. Costs are random piecewise-constant paths with up to three breaks, constant after a random date of at most 4; the exact stationary fixed point of the discrete recursion after that date, exit floor included, is the terminal value. It draws 6,000 parameter sets with $r\in(0.05,1.5)$, $\lambda=0$ in 20 percent and $\beta=0$ in 15 percent of draws, and $Q$ below, above, at and around $\bar Q$, and it extrapolates from $\Delta=0.002$ and $0.001$. Where $Q\le\bar Q$, the largest error against $x^\circ(0)$ is $3.1\times10^{-7}$ ($2.1\times10^{-4}$ at $Q=\bar Q$ exactly), and the test $Q\le\bar Q$ classifies all 6,000 draws as the test $Q\le\inf_t x^\circ(t)$ does. Where exit binds, the backward-induction value exceeds $x^\circ(0)$ in every draw and converges to the stopping value at first order (largest errors from $0.14$ at $\Delta=0.004$ to $0.0097$ at $\Delta=0.00025$, mean errors from $3.6\times10^{-3}$ to $2.3\times10^{-4}$), with the largest errors at $\beta$ near $0.95$, where the exit-floor branch carries a factor $1/(1-\beta)$. The counterexample in the proof is reproduced ($-0.3679$ by backward induction against $-0.7358$ for $x^\circ(0)$), and the edge cases with zero or constant costs return \cref{prop:master}.

\paragraph{\Cref{cor:interrupt}.} \texttt{v2\_m2\_interruption.py} solves four versions of the game by backward induction over the buffer stock, with the exit floor and constant baseline costs: the stock buffer of the corollary and the three variants (a) to (c). It draws 3,000 parameter sets ($\lambda=0$ in 25 percent, $\beta=0$ in 10, $h=0$ in 20, $\ell=0$ in 5, $t_U\in[0,3]$; exit binds without interruption in 977) and extrapolates from $\Delta=0.002$ and $0.001$. For the stock buffer, the largest error against part (ii) is $8.1\times10^{-4}$; the decision agrees with $(1-\psi)D>\pos{Q-x^\circ_0}$ in all 2,834 draws that are not near a tie, and with the condition of part (i) in all 1,862 such draws in which exit does not bind; in 188 draws exit binds and the condition of part (i) holds, yet interruption does not raise $P^*$; and every one of the 1,048 draws that interrupt at the full stock also interrupts at every lower stock. For variant (a), the largest error is $8.1\times10^{-4}$, no draw interrupts before $t_U$, and in 435 draws interruption raises $P^*$ although the condition of part (i) fails. For variant (b), the gain is exactly zero in all 2,852 draws with $\ell>0$, and the Markov equilibrium is unique at every stock. For variant (c), in 1,070 draws in which exit does not bind, the value is never below that of the stock-buffer timing, and the gain is positive in 551 draws in which the condition of part (i) fails. In a hand-picked case ($r=0.2$, $\lambda=0.3$, $\beta=0.5$, $\ell=0.5$, $c_S=2$, $h=0$, so $\bar t=2.773$), the gain is $0.2115$ at $t_U=0.5$ and $0.0472$ at $t_U=2$, as the closed form gives.
