\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A chokepoint holder's exit premium is a floor on what it obtains from its patron. Unless talks can rupture while the holder makes proposals, or the holder can hurt the patron while they bargain, its leverage is the positive part of that premium, and a holder with a negative premium gets nothing for its chokepoint however much its patron needs it. What the patron can extract with conditional threats is bounded by the holder's slack. Under rupture risk Washington's dependence on ASML raises both what Europe obtains in bargaining and Europe's surplus over exit, which its conditional threats can claw back (\cref{rem:rupture}).

The result adds a condition to the theory of weaponized interdependence. \citet{farrell2019weaponized} require jurisdiction over a hub and the domestic institutions to use it. When a hub's holder bargains with a state that depends on it, a third condition applies: the holder's exit premium must be positive, unless it has agenda power under rupture risk (\cref{cor:master}(c)) or can impose hold-up (\cref{prop:pain}). The Netherlands licenses ASML's exports to China, so it has the institutions in that direction; turning the tools against Washington takes a Union decision (\cref{sec:council}). Dutch jurisdiction is partial, because Washington reaches the firm through US-origin inputs and US persons (\cref{sec:background}), and on our reading that shared jurisdiction can keep the premium negative without any security term. Whether the Netherlands meets the third condition then turns on China's credible promises and, if Washington's threat to withhold frontier AI is credible, on the AI instrument, which on our orders of magnitude weighs more than the conditional part of the guarantee (\cref{sec:record}). We think the substitutes for a positive premium are weak. We see no sign that The Hague can make demands whose rejection would risk realignment, and under its current instruments the Union could lift an interruption of servicing after any US offer, a timing under which interruption does not help (\cref{sec:holdup}).

The record is consistent with the jurisdictional source of Europe's slack. In May 2026 The Hague opposed the MATCH Act's extraterritorial reach, arguing that ``every country is responsible for its own export control legislation'' \citep[our translation]{tk2026match}. We read that as a defence of who holds the key. Keeping the key in Europe does not make the premium positive, and deters a demand for control only if Washington values control little for its own sake (\cref{rem:bind}).
