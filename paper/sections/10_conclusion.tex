\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A chokepoint holder's exit premium is a floor on what it obtains from its patron. Unless talks risk rupture while the holder has agenda power, or the holder can impose costs on the patron while the two bargain, its leverage is the positive part of that premium, and a holder with a negative premium gets nothing for its chokepoint however much its patron needs it. The patron's demands are bounded by the holder's slack. Under rupture risk Washington's dependence on ASML raises both what Europe obtains in bargaining and the slack that Washington's conditional threats can claw back (\cref{rem:rupture}).

The result adds a condition to the theory of weaponized interdependence. \citet{farrell2019weaponized} require that a state have jurisdiction over a hub and the domestic institutions to use it. When the hub's holder bargains with a state that depends on the hub, a third condition applies: the holder's exit premium must be positive, unless talks carry a risk of rupture while the holder can make proposals (\cref{cor:master}(c)) or the holder can hurt the dependent state while they bargain (\cref{prop:pain}). The Netherlands licenses ASML's exports, so it has the institutions. It has jurisdiction only in part, because Washington reaches the firm through US-origin inputs and US persons (\cref{sec:background}). Whether it meets the third condition turns first on that shared jurisdiction, which on our reading can keep the premium negative without any security term, then on China's credible promises and, if Washington's threat to withhold frontier AI is credible, on the AI instrument, which on our orders of magnitude weighs more than the conditional part of the guarantee (\cref{sec:record}). We think the substitutes for a positive premium are weak. We see no sign in the record that The Hague can make demands whose rejection would risk realignment, and an interruption of servicing would help only under a rule that could not be suspended after a US offer, which on our reading the Union's current instruments do not supply (\cref{cor:interrupt}).

The record so far is consistent with the jurisdictional half of the argument. The Hague's one benefit, a place on Supplement No.~4, is a status that BIS can revoke by amending a rule \citep{bis2024fdpr}. What The Hague opposed in May 2026 was the extraterritorial reach of the MATCH Act, on the ground that ``every country is responsible for its own export control legislation'' \citep[our translation]{tk2026match}.
