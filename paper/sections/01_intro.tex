\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In \emph{Europe 2031}, a scenario published in June 2026 by eight authors writing in a personal capacity, the story's climax comes in March 2031, when the US vice president offers the leaders of the Netherlands, Germany and France a joint Dutch-American holding company in which Washington holds a controlling vote over ASML's output, customers and technology transfer \citep{juijn2026europe}. ASML, the Dutch firm that alone builds extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines, is what the scenario's summary page calls ``the only card Europe still holds''. Three years earlier in the story, the Dutch end ASML's remaining business in China under US pressure, and one character reflects that ``we didn't even manage to negotiate a single thing in return''. The scenario is fiction (\cref{app:scenario}). Its premise describes the record.

Since 2019 The Hague has aligned its export controls in steps, from 2023 each following a US move, and in November 2025 the EU put the most advanced immersion lithography tools on its control list \citep{eu2025dualuse}. What The Hague received was a status. In December 2024 the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) placed the Netherlands on Supplement No.~4, whose listed countries' exporters are spared new US licence requirements on chipmaking equipment because their own governments can control the items \citep{bis2024fdpr}. We code it as a threat left unexercised, a reward for self-binding and not a transfer (\cref{sec:coding}).

Yet the holders of these chokepoints resist some demands. In April 2026 the MATCH Act, introduced in both chambers, proposed US controls on all immersion lithography and on servicing listed Chinese fabs, to be applied to allies whose own controls were not certified as equivalent within 150 days \citep{hr8170, s4281}. On 12 May the Dutch trade minister told parliament that ``the cabinet is against the extraterritorial effect of the American proposal'' \citep[our translation]{tk2026match}. As of June 2026 both the Netherlands and Japan still let their firms service equipment in Chinese fabs \citep{zakaria2026match}. Tokyo had put 23 types of chipmaking equipment under licence in 2023 while stating that it was ``not aligning itself with the United States' measures'' \citep{allen2023translation, hoganlovells2023japan}.

Holders that Washington needs thus comply for little, and selectively. Theories of asymmetric and weaponized interdependence give power to the less dependent party and to the state with jurisdiction over a hub \citep{hirschman1945national, keohane1977power, farrell2019weaponized}. On that account Washington, which has no substitute for EUV lithography, should be paying The Hague. Accounts of alliance dependence and hierarchy treat compliance as the price of protection already received \citep{morrow1991alliances, lake2009hierarchy}. They are consistent with the compliance and leave the resistance on servicing and extraterritorial reach unexplained. Models of rival bidders hold that a client courted by two patrons is paid what the losing bidder would offer \citep{groseclose1996buying, bueno2016competition}, as Kyrgyzstan was paid for the Manas base in 2009 \citep{cooley2012great}. A bidding account needs a rival offer conditioned on exit. China is ASML's largest market \citep{asml2026annual}, but we have found no Chinese offer conditioned on Dutch exit, and The Hague has not been paid.

We argue that a chokepoint holder's exit premium is a floor on what it obtains from its patron, and that the holder's surplus over exit caps what the patron can extract with conditional threats. The exit premium is the rival's credible offer net of the credible costs of exit, including the value the holder places on alignment. Unless talks risk lasting rupture while the holder can make proposals, or the holder can impose costs on the patron while the two bargain, the holder's leverage is exactly the positive part of its premium (\cref{prop:master}). A holder with a negative premium then gets nothing for its chokepoint, however much its patron needs it. That floor is a known result: the outside-option principle \citep{shaked1984involuntary, sutton1986noncooperative, binmore1989outside} and the one-voter case of sequential vote buying \citep{groseclose1996buying, dekel2008vote}.

Our contribution is what comes after the floor. \citet{clayton2026putting} write that ``threats generate power to the extent that they generate slack in the participation constraint''. We ask what slack buys a patron when each of its threats must be sequentially rational, when the holder's outside option is a rival's bid, and when a threat that is carried out is spent. The first answer is a slack principle. Whatever instruments back them, and whatever bargaining sets the holder's price, the conditional parts of a patron's threats draw on one budget, the holder's surplus over exit (\cref{prop:slack}). Whatever agenda power, hold-up or a rival's bidding wins Europe above its outside option, Washington's credible conditional threats can claw back, down to the premium itself. The second answer is that which demands compete for that budget is the patron's choice. A demand that Washington enforces only through the alliance draws on the budget, because a Europe that realigned would undo it along with every other such demand. A demand that Washington can enforce with a penalty of its own, such as a tariff threat, does not, but it costs Europe more, because realigning no longer undoes it (\cref{prop:linkage}). Whether a concession on trade leaves less to extract on servicing therefore depends on how Washington enforces it, which can be observed. The third answer concerns resistance. Under complete information a demand that the holder would refuse is never made. Refusals need a patron unsure of what a demand costs, and near a zero premium, where conditional threats bite least, the patron can prevent fewer of them (\cref{prop:refusal}). China can raise a demand's cost: on our reading, its 2026 decree on supply-chain security attaches a counter-threat to any order to stop servicing Chinese fabs \citep{statecouncil2026supplychain}.

After the setting and the literature (\cref{sec:context}), \cref{sec:model} sets out the game and derives the floor. A China that values Washington's losses drives the price up to the limit of its own credibility (\cref{rem:spite}), and a rupture that is later repaired weights Washington's dependence by the share of time a relationship would spend broken. \Cref{sec:slack} proves the slack principle and \cref{sec:linkref} the results on linkage and refusals. \Cref{sec:extensions} summarizes four extensions developed in the online supplement: conditionality as a costly choice, preventive demands for control, the separate roles of The Hague, ASML and the Council, and Europe's power to interrupt servicing. \Cref{sec:evidence} is a plausibility probe. The record is consistent with a jurisdictional source of Europe's slack: Washington has coerced The Hague mainly with statuses it can grant and withdraw by rule, and its jurisdiction over ASML's inputs and staff can make the premium negative without any security term. On our orders of magnitude, if Washington's threat to withhold frontier AI is credible, the AI instrument is likelier than the conditional part of the guarantee to decide the sign of Europe's premium. \Cref{sec:conclusion} draws implications.

We contribute to three literatures. In geoeconomics, where \citet{clayton2026framework} pool enforcement threats that are never spent and the bidding models cited above have carrots only, we offer a theory of how a patron rations cut-off threats across demands as an ally's outside option improves, and of when issue linkage makes demands compete. In alliance politics, where abandonment threats are known to work when they are conditional and paired with assurance \citep{gerzhoy2015alliance, blankenship2023burden}, we show that what a patron can extract from a dependent ally is capped by the ally's surplus over its alternative, however the ally's price is set. And to the two conditions for weaponizing a hub in \citet{farrell2019weaponized}, jurisdiction and the institutions to use it, we add a third for a holder that bargains with a state that depends on the hub (\cref{sec:conclusion}).
