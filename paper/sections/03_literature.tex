\section{Leverage, Linkage, and Commitment}\label{sec:literature}

Three bodies of work bear on the ASML question. Each supplies part of the model, and each, applied on its own, gives the wrong answer about where Europe stands.

\subsection{Interdependence and chokepoints}

\citet{hirschman1945national} argued that a state gains influence over trading partners who would lose more than it would from ending their exchange. \citet{keohane1977power} refined the idea by distinguishing sensitivity, the costs a cutoff imposes before anyone adjusts, from vulnerability, the costs that remain after adjustment. Work on weaponized interdependence moved the argument from bilateral trade to networks. States with jurisdiction over hubs in hub-and-spoke networks can exploit them to gather information or deny access, provided their domestic institutions let them do so \citep{farrell2019weaponized, farrell2023underground, drezner2021uses}. Applied to the semiconductor supply chain, this logic has been used to explain American power over China \citep{malkin2024geoeconomics, beaumier2024cross} and, by extension, to suggest that the holders of other chokepoints, including the Netherlands, hold cards of their own.

\citet{wagner1988economic} identified the problem with this inference. Asymmetric interdependence implies nothing about political influence unless trading economic for political concessions leaves both sides better off than bargaining over the economic surplus alone. The less dependent state gains influence only through credible threats that change the other side's disagreement payoff. \citet{crescenzi2003economic} makes a related point about exit costs. Our model takes Wagner's position and makes it precise for a three-player setting. The holder of a chokepoint has leverage over its main user only if it has a credible alternative, and the alternative is set by the rival's willingness to pay net of the costs the main user can impose. Recent work also treats chokepoints as depleting assets: activation induces rerouting and indigenization \citep{tan2025driven, rajan2026eroding}. That decay is one reason the timing of leverage matters in \cref{sec:control}.

\subsection{Linkage and alliance coercion}

The formal literature on sanctions treats coercion as bargaining in which outcomes depend on the parties' relative toughness and on what each would suffer \citep{eaton1992sanctions}. Because targets that would rather concede do so before sanctions are imposed, successful coercion is usually unobserved \citep{drezner2003hidden, lacy2004theory}. Coercive cost, more than revealed resolve, appears to drive the success of threats \citep{whang2013coercion}. Coercers who link issues can trade concessions across them \citep{lacy2004theory, poast2012issue}. A hegemon that can coordinate threats across many economic relationships gains power that no single relationship would give it \citep{clayton2026framework}.

These models typically pit a sender against an adversary. In the ASML case the sender would be a junior ally that depends on its target for protection. Alliance theory has long held that asymmetric alliances trade the minor partner's autonomy for security \citep{morrow1991alliances}, that allies fear abandonment and entrapment \citep{snyder1984security}, and that patrons can exercise authority over subordinates in exchange for protection \citep{lake2009hierarchy}. The United States has used that authority before. Conditional threats of abandonment helped stop West German nuclear ambitions \citep{gerzhoy2015alliance}. Toshiba's 1987 sale of milling machines to the Soviet Union led to US sanctions on the firm \citep{mastanduno1992economic}. Coercion of this kind needs an assurance that compliance will be rewarded as well as a threat, and the two can work against each other \citep{pauly2024damned, cebul2021coercion}. The record also contains a counter-example. In 1982 Washington tried to stop European firms from supplying the Soviet gas pipeline through extraterritorial sanctions and backed down within months once Britain, France, Italy, and West Germany stood behind their firms \citep{jentleson1986pipeline, rodman1995sanctions}.

Our model brings these strands together. The same dependence that allows the patron to coerce the holder also lets it neutralize the holder's chokepoint. Whether it does so depends on the conditionality of its commitments, not on their size, and on whether carrying out each threat would hurt the patron. Firm-level penalties that would disrupt the patron's own supply are where \citet{rodman1995sanctions} and the 1982 episode belong. Jurisdiction over flows bound for the rival is a different and much cheaper instrument.

\subsection{Rivals, bidders, and wedges}

The third player in the ASML question is China, which would gain from Europe's realignment and can both reward and punish. The literature on wedge strategies studies how a state detaches members of a hostile coalition by rewards or by coercion \citep{crawford2011preventing, izumikawa2013coerce}. Formal work on third parties in bargaining shows that a prospective ally may signal its alignment by taking a hard line with its patron's rivals \citep{yoder2025fighting}. And when two outside powers compete for a client's policy, the client's price depends on the competition between them \citep{bueno2016competition}. The closest formal structure to our baseline is sequential vote buying, in which a first mover must make every winning coalition too expensive for a second mover \citep{groseclose1996buying}, and the related theory of menu auctions \citep{bernheim1986menu}. In both, the winning bidder pays roughly the loser's valuation. Our model adds what these frameworks lack for the ASML case: one bidder also controls the client's security and the jurisdiction under which the client's firm operates, and the credibility of each instrument is derived rather than assumed.

\subsection{Commitment and control}

The last ingredient is time. \citet{fearon1995rationalist} and \citet{powell2006war} show that anticipated shifts in bargaining power create commitment problems that can make bargaining fail even under complete information. \citet{fearon1996bargaining} considers bargaining over objects that affect future bargaining power, and \citet{debs2014known} study preventive action against latent capabilities. Control of ASML is such an object, and the scenario's Pentagon compares losing it to nuclear proliferation. The theory of the firm offers the relevant concept: ownership confers residual control rights, which determine how future surplus is divided when contracts are incomplete \citep{grossman1986costs}. \Cref{sec:control} combines the two. A patron that anticipates the holder's exit premium will rise can buy or coerce control rights while its threats still bite, and the holder's sovereignty costs and the rival's threats determine the price.
