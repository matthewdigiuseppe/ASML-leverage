\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Interdependence and chokepoints}

\citet{hirschman1945national} located influence in the losses a partner would suffer if trade stopped, and \citet{keohane1977power} separated the sensitivity and vulnerability that \cref{sec:holdup} uses. \citet{farrell2019weaponized} moved the argument to networks and attached two conditions: a state must have ``physical or legal jurisdiction over hub nodes'' (p.~56) and the ``legal and regulatory institutions'' to exploit them (p.~57), and where hubs span jurisdictions the states involved must coordinate (pp.~56, 58). \citet{farrell2023underground} trace how Washington built that capacity. \citet{kalyanpur2019mobilizing} show that jurisdiction is itself a policy choice, since harmonized EU rules let European firms escape the reach of US securities law. In semiconductors the foreign direct product rule forced foreign chipmakers to choose between US tools and Huawei as a customer \citep{bown2020how}, and US firms routed sales through third countries until that rule closed the gap \citep[194--195]{chen2023wars}. Treating a toolmaker as a hub extends Farrell and Newman's argument beyond finance and the internet. ASML meets the network condition, but Washington shares jurisdiction over it (\cref{sec:background}), and the European institutions that could turn it against the United States are collective (\cref{sec:council}).

These works disagree about whether a hub yields lasting power. For Farrell and Newman the power comes from network position and the institutions behind it, and targets' responses come later \citep[76]{farrell2019weaponized}. \citet{gholz2021market} find that Japan absorbed China's 2010 rare-earth embargo through market adjustment and argue that cost measures that ignore market structure overstate a sender's power. Work on chokepoints as depleting assets sides with Gholz and Hughes: activation induces indigenization and rerouting \citep{tan2025driven, rajan2026eroding}. We let the exit premium drift and ask what a patron does while it lasts (\cref{sec:control}). \citet{wagner1988economic} supplies the discipline: asymmetric interdependence yields influence only when trading economic resources for political concessions beats bargaining over the economic gains alone, however asymmetric the relationship.

Whether Europe can act on its side of the hub is also disputed. \citet{meunier2019geopoliticization} and \citet{bauerledanzman2024eu} trace the EU's turn toward geoeconomic instruments, while \citet{kastner2016buying} finds that trade dependence on China buys accommodation on economic questions more reliably than on political ones, which bears on the size of China's offer more than on its threats.

\subsection{Alliance coercion and dependence}

\citet{snyder1984security, snyder1997alliance} distinguishes the fear of abandonment from the fear of entrapment, and a protégé's need for its patron from the patron's own strategic interest in defending it; when that interest is well known, the stronger state ``cannot credibly threaten defection or realignment'' \citep[472--473]{snyder1984security}. \citet{morrow1991alliances} treats asymmetric alliances as trades of autonomy for security, and \citet{lake2009hierarchy} treats the patron's authority as relational, a social contract in which the patron provides order and protection and the subordinate complies. On those accounts compliance needs no fresh threat, a rival reading we take up in \cref{sec:predictions}. \citet{drezner1999sanctions} finds that allies rarely coerce each other but can win significant concessions when they do.

The coercion literature disagrees about which instrument worked in the canonical case. \citet{gerzhoy2015alliance} credits conditional threats of military abandonment, paired with assurance, for stopping West Germany's nuclear ambitions. \citet{lanoszka2018atomic} finds alliance coercion hard and argues that an ally's economic and technological reliance on Washington is the stronger instrument. \citet{pauly2024damned} explains why the pairing is fragile: the steps that make a threat credible undermine the coercer's assurance that compliance will not be punished. His case is an adversary, Iran. His assurance is owed to the target; our cost of conditionality falls mainly on other allies (\cref{sec:conditionality}). \citet{blankenship2021price} finds that allies in locations Washington values bear less of the common burden, consistent with less credible abandonment threats, and \citet[111--119]{blankenship2023burden} shows Iceland extracting compensation and fishing concessions for Keflavik. \citet{wolford2017alliances} show that threats against a politically valuable ally can lack credibility, which is our channel of self-harming sanctions.

Base politics studies a host that holds an asset its patron needs. \citet{cooley2008base} traces base stability to political change in hosts and \citet{calder2007embattled} to compensation, while \citet{cooley2009contracting} treat sovereignty transfers as incomplete contracts open to renegotiation. In 2009 Kyrgyzstan announced the closure of the US base at Manas alongside a Russian aid package, collected the first Russian payment, and then signed a new deal with Washington at a higher rent \citep[ch.~7]{cooley2012great}.

Nonproliferation work studies the reverse problem, a patron restraining an ally's sensitive exports. It splits on motive. \citet{kroenig2009exporting} finds that sensitive nuclear transfers follow suppliers' strategic position and no support for commercial motives. \citet{gray2012commercial} shows Bonn putting commercial rivalry first in its 1975 sale to Brazil, keeping the deal and promising only future caution.

\subsection{Rival bidders and geoeconomics}

That a contested client earns what the losing bidder would pay is old. It is the one-voter case of sequential vote buying \citep{groseclose1996buying}, extended to finite legislatures by \citet{banks2000buying}; \citet[Prop.~2]{dekel2008vote} show that the winner pays the loser's value net of the client's preference, a price pinned down only in undominated strategies. Menu auctions \citep{bernheim1986menu, grossman1994protection} give the simultaneous-offer analogue, and \citet{bueno2016competition} show that a dominant aid donor captures the surplus of an aid-for-policy deal while a rival donor's entry raises what it must pay. The outside-option principle supplies the threshold: a threat to walk away matters only when the alternative beats continued bargaining \citep{shaked1984involuntary, sutton1986noncooperative, binmore1989outside}. In the two-hegemon extension of \citet[Supplement B.2]{clayton2026framework}, hegemons whose threats overlap bid transfers down to zero. \Cref{lem:bidding} restates these results; we claim no novelty for it. \citet{lacy2004theory} model sanctions as coercive linkage of an economic relationship to a political demand under two-sided incomplete information: threats likely to succeed work before sanctions are imposed, so imposed sanctions are a selected sample.

\citet{clayton2026framework} show that a hegemon gains by pooling threats across relationships, bounded by the target's participation constraint, and \citet{clayton2026putting} state the principle we build on: ``Threats generate power to the extent that they generate slack in the participation constraint.'' The term slack is theirs. Their pooled threats are enforcement threats that raise the target's inside option and are never spent; threats to cut off a target that refuses appear only in their Supplement B.3.1, where they are partly self-defeating. In \citet{clayton2026theory} a hegemon gains by committing to limit its own coercion, and \citet{broner2025hegemonic} and \citet{becko2025optimal} model alignment among many small countries facing committed instruments. The effect of a rival is contested. Rival offers come when a protégé's relations with its patron sour \citep{yang2026major}, yet \citet{camboni2025spheres} find that China's growth can lead countries in the US sphere to offer Washington more.

We combine the two strands. Our patron's threats are cut-off threats aimed at an ally for whom a rival bids, each must be sequentially rational, and an executed threat is spent; as a result only conditional parts are capped by slack, conditional bites share one budget, and a patron that cannot commit to stop demanding obtains less (\cref{prop:slack}). Bidders in \citet{bueno2016competition} have carrots only. Ours have sticks and counter-threats, and China executes a counter-threat only to crowd out another demand (\cref{prop:counter}). Once the holder can propose and talks can rupture, the patron's dependence returns through agenda power, which the passive client of \citet{groseclose1996buying} and \citet{dekel2008vote} lacks (\cref{prop:master}). Where the hegemon of \citet{clayton2026theory} gains by binding itself, our patron chooses how conditional to make its guarantee, and it is the holder that may bind itself (\cref{prop:conditionality,rem:bind}).

\subsection{Commitment, control and prevention}

Anticipated shifts in power create commitment problems that can make bargaining fail under complete information \citep{fearon1995rationalist, powell2006war}, and \citet{krainin2017preventive} shows that slow but persistent shifts suffice. \citet{fearon1996bargaining}, an unpublished manuscript, studies bargaining over objects that shape future bargaining power, in which a discontinuous shift can cause breakdown. Control of ASML is such an object. Work on investment in future power divides. \citet{debs2014known} find that uncertainty about a rising state's investment can itself produce preventive war, while in \citet{bas2016dynamic} an observer that monitors imperfectly waits until the shift looks imminent, and \citet{spaniel2019bargaining} shows that potential power can instead be bought off with concessions. \citet{benson2023commitment} find that the conditions that make prevention attractive also make it unlikely to be carried out. \Cref{sec:preventive} sets our preventive demand for control against these models.

Control rights come from the theory of the firm: ownership allocates residual rights over an asset when contracts are incomplete \citep{grossman1986costs, hart1990property}. \citet{lake1996anarchy} orders relations from alliance to empire by these rights, and \citet{frieden1994international} ties colonial control to investments whose rents are easy to seize. In \citet{harstad2016market} leasing beats buying when the seller's consumption value and the buyer's protection cost are large, which bears on how far a transfer binds (\cref{cor:control}). Sold tools are a wasting asset, since a durable-goods seller cannot commit \citep{coase1972durability, bulow1982durable}, while control of maintenance works like leasing and restores commitment \citep{morita2004durable}; servicing is therefore a renewable source of hold-up (\cref{sec:holdup}). Leaders can tie their hands with audience costs and other public stakes \citep{schelling1960strategy, fearon1994domestic, fearon1997signaling}. In our model every threat and promise is staked this way, under one commitment technology (\cref{sec:commitment}).
