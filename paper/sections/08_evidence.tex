\section{The Record as a Plausibility Probe}\label{sec:evidence}

The parameters of the model are not measured, and a negative exit premium can rationalize almost any act of compliance after the fact. Compliance alone also cannot separate coercion from shared interest, because successful coercion ends at the threat stage \citep{drezner2003hidden}. We therefore ask what the model implies that its rivals do not, fix rules for coding what Washington gave and threatened, and read the Dutch record and nine comparison cases with those rules. None of this is a test. \Cref{sec:tests} describes the designs that would be.

\subsection{Observable implications}\label{sec:predictions}

\Cref{tab:implications} sets seven implications of the model beside five rival accounts. Each rival is a reading someone holds about allied compliance. Shared threat perception says the Netherlands aligns because it shares Washington's view of China; in the model this is a large intrinsic cost of exit $\eta$. \citet{lake2009hierarchy} treats compliance as owed to a patron whose authority rests on protection already provided and partly on legitimacy. \citet{morrow1991alliances} treats an asymmetric alliance as a trade of the protégé's autonomy for security, so that protection is itself the payment. \citet{drezner1999sanctions} argues that states that expect future conflict with each other rarely win concessions by coercion, while allies, who expect cooperation, win them when they try. A cost-minimizing coercer polices each demand with the cheapest credible instrument and sizes sanctions so that punishing a small offence does not erode deterrence of a large one \citep{stigler1970optimum, shavell1992note}. The entries for the rivals are our derivations; their authors did not write about these cases.

The rows differ in how much they discriminate. The threshold separates the model from hierarchy and the autonomy--security trade-off only through cases with a positive premium. A holder with a credible rival bid and a weakly conditional guarantee should be paid, as Kyrgyzstan was for Manas when a Russian aid package raised the rent Washington paid \citep{cooley2012great}, while the rivals predict no payment anywhere. The second and third rows are the sharpest. Both turn on the holder's slack: the free part of linkage, and the capacity shared by every demand, shrink as China's credible offer rises, whatever the cost of the instruments. Cost minimization predicts no such change, and none of the alliance accounts lets a rival's offer enter the price of compliance. The fourth and fifth rows concern China and Washington's response to it. Hierarchy predicts a patron that protects its subordinate against third parties. Our model predicts that a Chinese counter-threat changes what Washington asks for, and that Washington answers a stronger Chinese bid by making its guarantee more conditional. The last row discriminates least. The frontline prediction is shared by every account in which security dependence produces deference, and it separates the model only from accounts that treat Europe as one actor.

\subsection{Coding transfers, threats and leverage}\label{sec:coding}

Reading the record through \cref{prop:master} requires a line between a payment and a threat that was never carried out. We draw it at Washington's ex post optimal provision to an aligned ally, the baseline $x_j(A)$ of each instrument (\cref{sec:security}).

A \emph{transfer} is a benefit conditional on alignment that exceeds what Washington would provide to an aligned ally if it optimized ex post. Only transfers enter $P^*$. A \emph{threat left unexercised} is either a baseline benefit that Washington would withdraw after exit or a sanction it could impose and does not. It enters the exit cost $c$ as a conditional part $d_j$, and it is not a transfer, however valuable it is to the holder. This is the rule \cref{rem:gatekeeper} implies: a benefit Washington provides to a loyal Netherlands anyway, and credibly withdraws on exit, lowers the gatekeeper's premium and saves Washington money, while a promise above the baseline is a payment like any other.

The December 2024 exclusion of the Netherlands and the other countries on Supplement No.~4 from the new SME licence requirements (\cref{sec:background}) is the case that matters. BIS conditioned it on the listed governments' own capacity: they ``have the authority to control key SME items of concern'' \citep{bis2024fdpr}. The exclusion lasts as long as The Hague controls the items itself, and BIS can withdraw it by amending the rule. We therefore code it as a threat left unexercised, conditioned on Europe's own capacity to control, or equivalently as a reward for self-binding (\cref{rem:bind}). It is not a transfer. With this coding the exclusion is consistent with a nonpositive premium: it adds to $c$ and lowers $Q$, and under \cref{cor:master} a holder with $Q\le 0$ and little risk of rupture receives no transfer. The same rule codes the National Security Strategy's ``more favorable treatment'' for countries that align their export controls \citep{whitehouse2025nss} as conditionality, as \cref{sec:conditionality} argues.

We code the instrument a US demand invokes in three classes. \emph{Flow jurisdiction} reaches China-bound flows directly (de minimis and foreign direct product rules, entity listings, licences for US-origin items); it leaves $Q$ unchanged and needs no concession from the holder (\cref{prop:counter}(b)). \emph{Conditional linkage} threatens to withdraw from an aligned holder something in another domain that an exited holder would lose anyway, such as posture, AI access or trade terms. An \emph{unconditional} instrument hurts the holder whatever it does with the chokepoint. Chinese responses are coded as counter-threats if announced before the holder concedes and as executed if carried out.

Transfers are a narrow measure of leverage, and on that measure the Netherlands has little to show by construction. Our model also gives meaning to four broader measures: \emph{delay} between a US move and the holder's step; the \emph{scope} of the holder's controls relative to the demand; \emph{retained jurisdiction}, meaning who holds the licensing pen; and \emph{tolerated hedges}, flows Washington could reach but leaves alone. None of them is a payment. On our reading they measure how much of the holder's slack Washington chose to spend. By \cref{cor:rationing}, a patron leaves a hedge alone when its value per unit of the holder's cost is low or when the budget has gone to other demands; retained jurisdiction is self-binding with the key kept in Europe (\cref{rem:bind}).

\subsection{The Dutch record and Nexperia}\label{sec:record}

\paragraph{Alignment without a price.} Since 2019 The Hague has aligned in steps, and from 2023 each step followed a US move (\cref{sec:background}). Under our rules it received no transfer: its one benefit, Supplement No.~4, is a threat left unexercised. If talks carry no risk of rupture, or Washington makes the offers, \cref{cor:master}(b) gives $P^*\le 0$ whenever $Q\le0$, and the record is consistent with a holder whose premium has been negative throughout, even though customers in China took between 26 and 36 percent of ASML's sales from 2023 to 2025 (\cref{sec:background}).

The negative premium needs no security term. On our reading, US flow jurisdiction over China-bound tools blocks most of what China would gain from exit, which makes $\theta$ large and $\omega$ small (\cref{eq:omega}). ASML's US entities and staff are US persons (\cref{sec:background}), so the firm would not ship against a credible US penalty even if The Hague licensed exit, and Europe would then have to compel it (\cref{rem:firm}). With $\kappa=0$ these terms alone can make $Q$ negative. The record therefore supports the jurisdictional channel and leaves the security channel untested: we have found no documented case that links US security commitments to ASML. Cross-domain linkage exists elsewhere in the record (\cref{sec:cases}), but none ties posture or the guarantee to Dutch chip controls. The disputes since 2023 concern legacy sales and servicing, and \cref{prop:counter}(b) says that Washington polices such a hedge with flow jurisdiction whenever that is credible, so they could not reveal $\kappa$ even if it were large.

The security signals of 2025 and 2026 do not settle the question either. An untargeted drawdown lowers the conditional part of posture and should raise Europe's premium, while a withdrawal aimed at an ally's conduct is an executed threat (\cref{sec:conditionality}). The Dutch response to US pressure in 2026 was voice without exit (see below). On our reading, either the premium stayed negative because jurisdiction alone keeps it there, or perceived conditionality rose with the targeted moves. The record cannot tell these apart, and \cref{sec:tests} proposes evidence that could.

\paragraph{Slack spent and unspent.} The broader measures show what The Hague kept. Licensing of the older immersion tools came a year after the advanced ones, The Hague took the licensing pen from Washington in September 2024, and servicing of the installed base in China continued at least into mid-2026 (\cref{sec:background}). We read this as rationing. Washington spent Europe's slack first on the sales controls it valued most and left the servicing hedge, costly to ASML, until 2026. A shared-interest account fits the substance of Dutch alignment, and the Dutch regulations justify the controls by Dutch security and the Netherlands' exceptional position in the semiconductor value chain without mentioning the United States. % TODO(bib): keys stcrt2024duv and stcrt2025metrology (notes/v2/facts_record.md items 1.1 and 1.3) are not in refs.bib.
It fits the timing less well, since a holder aligning for its own reasons has no need to wait for US rules.

\paragraph{Nexperia.} The Nexperia dispute of 2025 is the one episode in which a Chinese counter-threat was executed against a Dutch step taken under US pressure. On 5 June 2025 the Dutch economy ministry told Nexperia that Washington might soon introduce a ``50\% rule'' and that, if the Nijmegen firm sought an exemption, Washington would look at measures to stop transfers of US intellectual property and technology. On 12 June the Dutch Foreign Ministry met the State Department's Bureau of International Security and Nonproliferation. The Dutch minutes record that the US side called it problematic that the chief executive was still the same Chinese owner, and conclude that it was ``almost certain the CEO will have to be replaced to qualify for the exemption from the entity list'' \citep[paras.~2.19--2.20; our translation]{gerechtshof2025nexperia}. The minutes, reported in October 2025 \citep{dutchnews2025nexperia}, are a Dutch record of US views, not a formal US condition. On 30 September, after a US rule threatened Nexperia's operations, the economy minister used the Goods Availability Act to take control of the firm, and the Enterprise Chamber suspended the chief executive in early October \citep{taylorwessing2025nexperia, gerechtshof2025nexperia}. China then blocked exports from Nexperia's Chinese plants, which threatened production at European carmakers \citep{taylorwessing2025nexperia}. At Busan on 30 October, Washington agreed to suspend the Affiliates Rule for one year from 10 November, and China agreed to ``take appropriate measures to ensure the resumption of trade from Nexperia's facilities in China'' \citep{fmprc2025busan, whitehouse2025busan}. On 19 November the Dutch minister suspended his order \citep{taylorwessing2025nexperia}. The Enterprise Chamber's measures were separate from the order and outlasted it. % TODO(bib): source is Kamerstukken 32637 nr. 717 (key kst32637_717 in notes/review/gap_dutch_domestic_politics.md), not in refs.bib.

We read the episode as a US-backed demand on the Netherlands, an entity-list exemption offered in exchange for removing Chinese control of the firm, met by a Chinese counter-threat that was executed. The Dutch suspension came once Washington had suspended the rule that motivated the demand. Under complete information, \cref{prop:counter}(a)(iii) allows execution on the equilibrium path only when the counter-threat crowds out another demand. That reading requires three things: a Chinese threat known in The Hague and Washington before 30 September; a US demand on the Netherlands that Washington shelved because the Nexperia concession had become expensive; and a Dutch government that conceded while expecting to bear the cost. The alternative is that The Hague, and perhaps Washington, misjudged China's resolve. That reading lies outside our complete-information model, and it requires that the Chinese response surprised the decision makers. The quick reversal fits it better. Neither reading is established by the public record, and the minutes that revealed the US demand exist only because a court published them.

Europe bore the cost and was not compensated. By \cref{prop:counter}(a)(v) a counter-threat leaves the holder's loss unchanged when Washington has a divisible sunk demand that China cannot reach, and otherwise the holder's loss can rise. The relief came through a US concession to China at Busan. US--China bargaining is outside the model, so we do not read Busan as a transfer to Europe. The reversal under Chinese pressure is also evidence that compelling a firm is costly when China retaliates against the compulsion itself (\cref{rem:firm}). And the order shows that the Dutch state can override control of a firm under its own law, which on our reading lowers the enforceability of any control right that Washington might hold through a Dutch entity (\cref{cor:control}(c)).

\paragraph{Servicing and the MATCH Act.} Servicing of China's installed base is the hedge in our terms, and Washington tolerated it into 2026. The MATCH Act would police it by flow jurisdiction: allies that do not align within 150 days would face US controls on sales and servicing (\cref{sec:background}). In the model a statute is a stake that makes the threat credible if $e_F\le\bar m_U$, and a credible threat to block the hedged flows leaves $Q$ unchanged and need not be carried out (\cref{prop:counter}(b)). The design also moves the burden. Washington would act on the flows itself if The Hague did not concede, and on our reading an order to stop servicing carries a Chinese counter-threat after Decrees 834 and 835 \citep{statecouncil2026supplychain, reedsmith2026decree}, so the concession has become more expensive for Europe (\cref{prop:counter}(a)(i)). The Dutch answer was voice. Prime Minister Jetten raised the bill with President Trump in April 2026, the trade minister took Dutch objections to the commerce secretary and members of Congress in June, and the cabinet told parliament that it opposed the bill's extraterritorial reach \citep{nltimes2026pushback, nltimes2026irritated, vangerven2026dutch, tk2026match}. The Hague did not threaten to stop aligning. With $Q<0$, voice and the hold-up of \cref{sec:holdup} are all a holder has.

China's share of ASML's system sales rose from 14 percent in the second quarter of 2026 to 23 percent in the third \citep{asml2026q2call, asml2026q3}. The model has no inventories, so a stockpiling reading is not one of its implications. A rise in Dutch licensing of legacy tools, which would be a larger tolerated hedge, is. Licensing decisions and order dates would separate the two.

\paragraph{What the comparison shows.} No holder in the table was paid in cash for aligning on a chokepoint. The currency is status (Supplement No.~4, validated end-user status, Country Group A:5), which BIS grants and withdraws by rule. Statuses that Washington would provide to a loyal holder anyway are exit costs under our coding, so this is the pattern \cref{cor:master} leads us to expect from holders with negative premia. Where money moved, it moved from the client to the patron, as in the offsets and the UAE arrangements, or to a third party. In 1967 Washington placed orders in Britain to close the trilateral deal, and in 1977 it offered Brazil, not Bonn, a fuel guarantee \citep{frus1995westerneurope, frus2015nonproliferation}.

Resistance clusters on the installed base and on deals already signed. Tokyo and The Hague kept servicing Chinese fabs, Bonn kept its contract with Brazil while offering restraint on future transfers, and the Korean fabs kept operating under licence. In the model these are concessions whose cost to the holder is large relative to the bite that remains, and the Japanese case adds a Chinese counter-threat on exactly the concession Washington sought. A cost-minimizing coercer that found enforcement against servicing expensive would also tolerate it, so this row of evidence fits our account without separating it from that rival.

Explicit links between an ally's conduct on one issue and US treatment on another appear in three places: Johnson's link between troop levels and offset payments in 1966, the posture and trade measures against Germany and Spain in 2026, and the BIS rule of July 2026 that cited the UAE's role in Operation Epic Fury. It never appears in a demand on a European holder's chip controls. The security channel of the model is a feasible instrument that the record shows Washington using, but not on ASML.

\paragraph{Where the comparison strains the model.} The offsets fit only partly. Washington linked troop levels to German payments, and the payments were sunk concessions, which escape the slack cap (\cref{prop:slack}(b)). The arrangement was renegotiated from the 1961 memorandum through the informal settlement of 1964 to the trilateral talks of 1966--67 \citep{frus1995foreignecon, frus1999germany, frus1995westerneurope}, which is what \cref{prop:slack}(d) leads us to expect when a patron cannot commit to stop demanding, though FRUS does not show Bonn reasoning that way. The redeployment of up to 35,000 personnel agreed in the 1967 settlement strains the model further. A complete-information patron does not both collect and cut. We see two readings. If the troops were at Washington's ex post optimum, the linkage was a costly conditional threat and the partial cut is unexplained. If they were above it, as balance-of-payments pressure and the Mansfield resolution suggest, keeping them was a transfer to Bonn, the offsets were its price, and Washington cut where cutting saved money \citep{frus1995westerneurope, gavin2004gold, zimmermann2002money}. FRUS does not let us choose. Erhard's fall is outside the model. Contemporaries tied it to the offset dispute, and McCloy recorded Brandt calling the package approach ``a major mistake'' that hastened it \citep{frus1999germany}. Our holder bears a sovereignty cost only when it transfers control, and the case suggests that conceding to the patron on other issues carries a domestic cost too.

The United Kingdom in 1957 shows a secondary demand that Washington chose not to back with linkage. Eisenhower conceded privately that there was ``very little of profit in the matter'', Macmillan answered that ``We live by exports'', and in September the National Security Council decided that no sanctions would be applied against allies for eliminating the special China controls \citep{frus1989economicdefense, mastanduno1992economic}. A demand worth little to Washington and costly to the holder is the first to go under \cref{cor:rationing}. The strain is that the holder did not act alone. Twelve of sixteen members backed abolition \citep{frus1989economicdefense}, so the demand faced a collective exit that our single-holder model does not represent. Washington kept its own embargo, the analogue of retaining flow jurisdiction over its own trade. The Brazil deal adds a different strain. German officials feared for the US security commitment, although in the FRUS record Washington did not tie its pressure to it \citep{frus2014westerneurope, frus2015nonproliferation}. In our complete-information model, conditionality that exists only in the holder's beliefs has no place, and \citet{gray2012commercial} reads Bonn's resistance as commercial rivalry, against the finding of \citet{kroenig2009exporting} that sensitive nuclear transfers follow strategic rather than commercial motives.

\subsection{Designs that could test the model}\label{sec:tests}

\paragraph{An event dataset.} The unit would be a US demand on a holder of a chipmaking-equipment chokepoint, 2019--2026: the Netherlands, Japan, South Korea and Germany. For each demand we would code the instrument threatened in the three classes of \cref{sec:coding}, the holder's response, any compensation, and any Chinese response, from the Federal Register, national gazettes, parliamentary records and firm filings. Proxies for the holder's premium are China's share of the firm's sales, credible Chinese counter-threats and the holder's security exposure. The model implies that conditional linkage against secondary demands appears when a holder has slack and fades as its premium rises toward zero, while the use of flow jurisdiction does not track the premium (\cref{prop:counter}(b)). It also implies that concessions in one issue area crowd out demands in another within the same period (\cref{cor:rationing}). Cost minimization implies neither.

\paragraph{An elite survey experiment.} Because conditionality is a property of US behaviour, the natural respondents are US foreign-policy elites. A vignette would describe an ally that holds a chokepoint and vary whether it complied with US export controls on China, refused, or complied on sales while continuing to service Chinese fabs. Outcomes would be support for defending the ally if attacked and support for keeping US forces and intelligence sharing there. The difference in support between compliant and noncompliant allies estimates the conditionality of the core guarantee and of posture separately. Further arms would vary whether the president had publicly tied protection to compliance, which is the stake of \cref{prop:conditionality}, and whether the ally hosts bases the United States uses, which tests the sign in part (d) of that proposition.

\paragraph{Process tracing.} Requests under the Dutch Open Government Act (Woo) for ministerial records of export-control talks with US counterparts from 2019 to 2026 would show whether security commitments, posture or the F-35 programme were ever raised, which is the evidence the security channel needs. The same records would date Dutch drafting against US threats, which separates coercion from shared interest better than outcomes do, and would show what The Hague expected from China before 30 September 2025, which separates the two readings of Nexperia. Licensing decisions and order dates would distinguish a larger tolerated hedge from stockpiling in the 2026 rebound.

\paragraph{The Busan expiry.} The US suspension of the Affiliates Rule ran for one year from 10 November 2025, and China's suspension of its October 2025 controls ran to 10 November 2026 \citep{whitehouse2025busan, mofcom2025pause}. In September 2026 the two governments agreed to extend the arrangement that paused them to 10 January 2027, but no legal act extending the suspensions themselves had appeared by late October \citep{mofcom2026extension}. If both measures return, the rule that prompted the Nexperia demand and China's controls on rare earths come back together \citep{mayerbrown2025rareearth}. \Cref{prop:counter}(a) then leads us to expect Washington to shift its demands on The Hague away from concessions to which China has attached credible counter-threats, toward demands China cannot reach or toward flow jurisdiction it exercises itself. A second executed Chinese counter-threat against a Dutch step would be hard to square with misjudgment, which learning should correct, and the crowding-out reading would then require us to find the demand it displaced.
