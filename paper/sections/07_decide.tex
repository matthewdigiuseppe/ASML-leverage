\section{Who Decides, and the Power to Hurt}\label{sec:decide}

So far Europe has been one actor. In practice the Dutch licensing authority decides whether ASML may ship to China, the firm decides whether it will, and the European Union decides whether ASML's tools can be turned against the United States (\cref{sec:background}). This section separates the three. It then gives Europe a way to impose costs on Washington while the two bargain, which is where Washington's dependence on ASML does its most direct work.

\subsection{The gatekeeper}\label{sec:gatekeeper}

Exit toward China is a Dutch decision. Under Article 12(2) of the dual-use regulation the licence is granted by the competent authority of the member state where the exporter is established \citep{eu2021dualuse}, and other member states may only request consultations that do not bind. The Commission has no licensing power and no veto. Let $Q_{NL}=\omega_{NL}-c_{NL}$ be the gatekeeper's own exit premium: the value to The Hague of China's credible offer, net of the credible costs of exit that fall on the Netherlands. By \cref{lem:bidding}, Washington keeps the Netherlands aligned at price $\pos{Q_{NL}}$ when $Q_{NL}<V$ and $Q_{NL}\le\bar m_U$.

\begin{remark}[Gatekeeper]\label{rem:gatekeeper}
Let $x_h\ge0$ be the value to the Netherlands of a benefit that the United States provides to a loyal Netherlands in the baseline and credibly withdraws if the Netherlands licenses exit. Then the gatekeeper's premium becomes $Q_{NL}'=Q_{NL}-x_h$. If $Q_{NL}'<V$ and $Q_{NL}'\le\bar m_U$, the United States' payment falls from $\pos{Q_{NL}}$ to $\pos{Q_{NL}-x_h}$, that is, by $\min\{x_h,\pos{Q_{NL}}\}$. The gatekeeper's slack rises from $\pos{-Q_{NL}}$ to $\pos{x_h-Q_{NL}}$, that is, by $\pos{x_h-\pos{Q_{NL}}}$. If $Q_{NL}>\min\{V,\bar m_U\}$, the benefit restores alignment if and only if $Q_{NL}-x_h\le\bar m_U$ (and $Q_{NL}-x_h<V$). A benefit paid as a promised increment above the baseline lowers no payment, since the total outlay is then $\max\{x_h,Q_{NL}\}\ge\pos{Q_{NL}}$.
\end{remark}

The remark is divide and rule. A benefit aimed at the gatekeeper alone, whose withdrawal on exit is credible (\cref{sec:commitment}), buys alignment at a discount, and when the gatekeeper already has slack every unit of it adds to the stock Washington can spend on other demands (\cref{prop:slack}). Only provision that Washington would make to a loyal Netherlands anyway does this work. A side payment promised on top of the baseline is a transfer like any other and saves Washington nothing.

Partners inside Europe affect the gatekeeper's premium only through transfers. Germany, France or the Commission cannot refuse a Dutch licence, so solidarity reaches The Hague only as compensation, coordinated retaliation or cover against Chinese counter-coercion, each of which moves $\omega_{NL}$ or $c_{NL}$. Washington can tier member states directly. The AI Diffusion Rule of January 2025 placed ten EU members, the Netherlands among them, in its favoured group and the other seventeen in the middle tier \citep{bis2025diffusion}. By our computation from the 2026 Council population figures \citep{council2025population}, the ten held 70.46 percent of EU population, too few members to form a qualified majority by themselves, while the seventeen held 29.54 percent and enough members to block by number. The rule was rescinded before it took effect (\cref{sec:background}), so tiering is a latent instrument, but it shows how one rule can reward the gatekeeper and split the Council at once (\cref{sec:council}).

\subsection{The firm between two jurisdictions}\label{sec:firm}

A licence permits a shipment and orders nothing. ASML decides whether to ship, and it answers to two sovereigns besides its own.

\begin{remark}[Firm between jurisdictions]\label{rem:firm}
Suppose the Netherlands licenses exit. The firm earns $y$ from its Chinese business and faces the credible US penalty $f^F=\chi_f f$ if it ships. If it complies with US measures instead, it faces a Chinese penalty $p_C\ge0$ for that compliance and a penalty $p_B\ge0$ under the EU Blocking Statute, which is positive only if the US measures are listed in the statute's annex. The firm ships if and only if $y+p_C+p_B\ge f^F$ (ties ship). If it would not ship, exit requires the state to compel it at cost $\zeta\ge0$, where $\zeta$ excludes the firm's own net loss (already counted in $\omega$ and $c$ through $y$ and $d_f$). Europe's effective exit premium is $Q-\zeta\,\mathbf 1\{y+p_C+p_B<f^F\}$, and Europe compels the firm only if $Q-\zeta>0$.
\end{remark}

Since April 2026, $p_C$ has a legal basis. Decree 835 lets Beijing put those who implement foreign extraterritorial measures on a Malicious Entity List, issue prohibition orders, and expose them to private damages suits and fines \citep{reedsmith2026decree}. ASML runs a repair centre in Beijing and services a large installed base in China (\cref{sec:background}), so its staff in China who implemented a US servicing ban would be exposed to these tools \citep{asml2026annual}. A higher $p_C$ makes a licensed firm more willing to ship, which shrinks the range in which compulsion is needed and weakly raises Europe's effective premium. The decree leaves the credibility of Washington's jurisdiction over China-bound flows unchanged, since that turns on Washington's own ex post cost. What it raises is the penalty the firm must expect before it prefers compliance. For a US ban on servicing in China the same penalty enters \cref{prop:counter}(a) as a counter-threat $r_k$ only if Europe internalizes the firm's loss.

Europe's own instrument for this margin is weak. The Blocking Statute bars EU persons from complying with listed foreign laws, denies recognition to foreign judgments based on them and allows damages claims \citep{eu1996blocking}. Its annex lists US sanctions on Cuba and Iran, updated in 2018 after Washington reimposed Iran sanctions \citep{ec2018blocking}, and no US export-control law. So $p_B=0$ today, and making it positive would take a further delegated act. Even then the penalty would be bounded. In \emph{Bank Melli} the Court of Justice held that the prohibition applies without any US order, but that a national court may annul a termination made to comply only if annulment does not have disproportionate effects, weighing the probability and extent of the firm's economic loss \citep{cjeu2021bankmelli}. Enforcement runs through national courts and is bounded by proportionality toward firms with a large US business. We read this as keeping $p_B$ small for ASML, whose US subsidiaries and staff (\cref{sec:background}) give it such a business.

In the Nexperia case the Dutch state used the Goods Availability Act to compel a firm and suspended the order seven weeks later, after China had blocked exports from Nexperia's Chinese plants; we read the suspension as evidence that $\zeta$ is high when China retaliates against the compulsion itself (\cref{sec:record}).

\subsection{The Council}\label{sec:council}

Turning ASML against Washington is collective, because EUV and the most advanced immersion tools ship to US fabs under the Union general export authorisation EU001 (\cref{sec:background}). Two routes could stop those shipments.

The Anti-Coercion Instrument has two stages \citep{eu2023aci}. The Commission examines the case, normally within four months. The Council then determines by qualified majority, on a Commission proposal, that coercion exists; it acts within eight weeks, and normally within ten. Response measures are Commission implementing acts under the examination procedure, with a clause that bars adoption when the committee delivers no opinion. In the appeal committee, however, the Commission may adopt unless a qualified majority votes against \citep{eu2011comitology}, and the instrument's recital 85 asks it to avoid going against any predominant position among member states. The second route is a delegated act removing the United States from EU001 \citep{eu2021dualuse}. Under the urgency procedure it enters into force at once and stands unless the European Parliament objects by a majority of its component members or the Council objects by a reinforced qualified majority of 20 members representing 65 percent of the population \citep{eu2016tfeu}. Eight members, or members holding more than 35 percent of the population, keep the act in force. The Netherlands would then still have to deny the individual licences, so the delegated-act route hands the decision back to the gatekeeper of \cref{sec:gatekeeper}.

Let $q_i$ be member $i$'s net gain from adopting an anti-US countermeasure. A qualified majority needs at least 15 of 27 members representing at least 65 percent of the population, and abstention counts against.

\begin{remark}[Blocking minorities]\label{rem:council}
Let $\mathcal B$ be the family of coalitions that block the Council's determination at the first stage: at least 13 members, or at least four members with more than 35 percent of the population. With enforceable vote-contingent payments and no rival bidder, the United States defeats the determination at cost $\Phi(0)=\min_{J\in\mathcal B}\sum_{i\in J}\pos{q_i}$. Members with $q_i\le0$ join any blocking coalition at zero cost, so $\Phi(0)$ is the cheapest completion of $F=\{i:q_i\le0\}$ into a blocking coalition, and $\Phi(0)=0$ if and only if $F\in\mathcal B$. The six frontline states (15.09 percent of the population) do not block. Thirteen eastern and Nordic members (26.77 percent) do, by number. Defeating a response measure in the appeal committee, or a delegated act by Council objection, needs a qualified majority against, which is itself in $\mathcal B$, so it costs at least $\Phi(0)$.
\end{remark}

The cost function is the one-voter logic of \cref{lem:bidding} applied to a weighted body, as in \citet{groseclose1996buying}. We assume no one bids for adoption; with a counter-bidder that moves last, Washington would have to buy more than a minimal blocking coalition \citep{groseclose1996buying, banks2000buying}. The remark puts the veto at the front. Every later Council stage of either route requires a coalition at least as large as a stage-one block, so a Washington that wants to stop a countermeasure buys its blocking minority before the determination. \Cref{tab:council} gives the arithmetic for the coalitions that matter.

The table yields a prediction. Washington can make posture and enablers conditional member by member (\cref{sec:security}), so the members whose defence depends most on US forward presence have the lowest $q_i$ and are the natural blockers. They are not enough. The frontline core falls short on both counts, and a population block without Germany, France, Italy and Spain needs at least twelve members. If all thirteen eastern and Nordic members in the fourth row had $q_i\le0$, Washington would defeat a determination for free. Hungary's support is doubtful, but Slovenia or Greece can replace it and the group still has thirteen members. We therefore expect opposition to an anti-US measure to start on the frontline and to block only once it spreads to central Europe and the Nordics, or once one large state joins.

Informal norms and procedural defaults pull the outcome away from the formal arithmetic, in opposite directions. Council practice is consensual. About four in five legislative acts passed with no recorded negative vote or abstention in 1994--2002 \citep{heisenberg2005institution}, and \citet{hage2013coalition} models that consensus as a by-product of negotiators organizing around blocking minorities before any vote is taken. Recorded votes also understate opposition, because a vote would disclose who lost \citep{novak2013silence}. The data cover legislation before the Lisbon voting rules, so we treat them as indicative, but they suggest that a measure large states oppose rarely reaches a vote. Reversed defaults pull the other way. On 4 October 2024 the trade-defence committee voted on countervailing duties on battery electric vehicles from China: ten member states in favour, five against (Germany among them) and twelve abstaining \citep{afp2024bev}. The committee delivered no opinion, no qualified majority opposed, and the Commission adopted the duties on 29 October \citep{ec2024bev, eu2011comitology}. That vote is not evidence that an ACI measure could pass with a minority, since the ACI's no-opinion clause reverses this default at the committee stage. It shows what the Commission does when the procedure puts the burden on opponents, as the ACI's appeal stage and the delegated-act route both do.

The same arithmetic applies to the Council vote in \emph{Europe 2031}. The seven states the scenario names as opposing or abstaining on its 2029 countermeasure (the Netherlands, Ireland, Poland, the three Baltic states and Italy) hold 28.06 percent of EU population \citep{juijn2026europe, council2025population}. They block neither by number nor by population. For the vote to fail, Germany, France or Spain would also have to withhold support, or six more members of any size. \Cref{app:scenario} sets this beside the scenario's other episodes.

\subsection{Hold-up}\label{sec:holdup}

\Cref{prop:master} lets the status quo run unchanged while Europe and Washington bargain. A dispute over control of ASML or over Europe's access to frontier AI would not leave it unchanged. Washington could cut Europe's AI allocation while talks continue, and Europe could interrupt ASML's servicing of US-aligned fabs. Let disagreement carry flow costs $c_E(t)$ to Europe and $c_U(t)$ to the United States, and write $\hat C_i(t)=(r+\lambda)\int_0^\infty e^{-(r+\lambda)s}c_i(t+s)\,ds$ for the average discounted cost from date $t$ on.

\begin{proposition}[Pain and rupture]\label{prop:pain}
Consider the game of \cref{prop:master} with $r>0$ and disagreement flow costs $c_E(t),c_U(t)\ge0$ that are bounded and piecewise uniformly continuous, with finitely many discontinuities in every bounded interval. Let $M=Q+\beta(V-Q)$, $n(t)=\beta c_U(t)-(1-\beta)c_E(t)$, $N(t)=\beta\hat C_U(t)-(1-\beta)\hat C_E(t)$ and
\[
x^\circ(t)=\psi M+(1-\psi)N(t).
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For every $\Delta>0$, Europe's Markov perfect equilibrium payoff is unique. In every round in which $\lambda>0$ or $c_E+c_U>0$, every Markov perfect equilibrium has immediate agreement. If $\lambda=0$ and $c_E(t)+c_U(t)=0$ on an interval, there are also payoff-equivalent equilibria with delay during that interval.
\item As $\Delta\to0$, Europe's equilibrium payoff converges to
\begin{align*}
P^*&=\sup_{\tau\in[0,\infty]}\Big\{\int_0^\tau e^{-(r+\lambda)s}\big(\lambda M+r\,n(s)\big)\,ds+e^{-(r+\lambda)\tau}Q\Big\}\\
&=x^\circ(0)+\pos{\sup_{t\ge0}e^{-(r+\lambda)t}\big(Q-x^\circ(t)\big)}.
\end{align*}
\item $P^*=x^\circ(0)=\psi\big[Q+\beta(V-Q)\big]+(1-\psi)\big[\beta\hat C_U(0)-(1-\beta)\hat C_E(0)\big]$ if and only if
\[
Q\le\inf_{t\ge0}x^\circ(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q\le\bar Q\equiv\inf_{t\ge0}\frac{\lambda\beta V+r\,N(t)}{r+\lambda\beta}.
\]
Under this condition the limiting equilibrium value at every date $t$ is $x^\circ(t)$, so the exit option is never used. The condition $Q\le x^\circ(0)$ alone is not sufficient. With constant costs, $P^*=\max\{Q,x^\circ\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

When exit does not bind, Europe's transfer is a weighted average of two terms. With weight $\psi$ it gets the rupture value $M$ of \cref{prop:master}. With weight $1-\psi$ it gets the hold-up term $N$: Washington's average pain weighted by Europe's agenda power, minus Europe's own pain weighted by Washington's. With zero costs the proposition returns \cref{prop:master}. The hold-up term needs no rupture risk. At $\lambda=0$, \cref{prop:master} gives $\pos{Q}$ whatever $V$ is, while here $P^*=\max\{Q,\beta\hat C_U-(1-\beta)\hat C_E\}$ with constant costs. Hold-up is the fifth channel of \cref{cor:master}(e).

\citet{keohane1977power} distinguished sensitivity, how quickly and how sharply a cutoff hurts before anyone adjusts, from vulnerability, the cost that remains after adjustment. The proposition prices both. A buffer of stockpiled parts and transferred know-how delays the onset of Washington's cost and so shrinks $\hat C_U$; that is sensitivity. The flow cost once the buffer is spent, net of whatever servicing Washington can compel, is vulnerability. The next result makes the split explicit for servicing. Let baseline disagreement flows be $c_E^0$ and $c_U^0$, which may include Washington's own cost of cutting a loyal Europe's AI access from date zero, and let $x^\circ_0$ be $x^\circ(0)$ under these baseline costs.

\begin{corollary}[Interruption]\label{cor:interrupt}
Let Europe choose, at the start of each round and before the proposer is drawn, whether servicing of US-aligned fabs is interrupted. Interruption costs Europe a flow $\ell$, and costs the United States a flow $(1-h)c_S$ once its buffer is exhausted, where $h$ is the share of that servicing Washington can compel. The buffer is a stock of $t_U$ units of interruption time, depleted only in rounds with interruption. Let $D=\beta(1-h)c_Se^{-(r+\lambda)t_U}-(1-\beta)\ell$. In the frequent-offer limit:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If exit never binds without interruption (\cref{prop:pain}(iii) with the baseline costs), allowing interruption raises $P^*$ by $(1-\psi)\pos{D}$. Interruption raises $P^*$ if and only if $\beta(1-h)c_Se^{-(r+\lambda)t_U}>(1-\beta)\ell$, that is, if and only if $t_U<\bar t\equiv\ln[\beta(1-h)c_S/((1-\beta)\ell)]/(r+\lambda)$ (with $\bar t=\infty$ if $\ell=0<\beta(1-h)c_S$).
\item With constant baseline costs and the exit option, $P^*=\max\{Q,\,x^\circ_0,\,x^\circ_0+(1-\psi)D\}$. Interruption raises $P^*$ if and only if $(1-\psi)D>\pos{Q-x^\circ_0}$. If exit binds without interruption, the condition of part (i) is necessary but not sufficient.
\item The decision is time consistent. At stock $b$, Europe interrupts if and only if $\beta(1-h)c_Se^{-(r+\lambda)b}>(1-\beta)\ell$. Along an interruption path $b$ falls, so the condition keeps holding. If it fails at $t=0$, the stock never moves and it fails in every round.
\end{enumerate}
The result depends on the stock buffer and on the timing. (a) If the buffer runs out at calendar date $t_U$ whatever Europe does, Europe never interrupts before $t_U$; interruption raises $P^*$ by $(1-\psi)e^{-(r+\lambda)t_U}\pos{\beta(1-h)c_S-(1-\beta)\ell}$, so it helps if and only if $\beta(1-h)c_S>(1-\beta)\ell$, for any finite $t_U$. (b) If Europe chooses interruption after an offer is rejected and $\ell>0$, the unique Markov equilibrium has no interruption and $P^*$ is unchanged. (c) If Europe chooses after the proposer is drawn but before offers, it interrupts in the rounds in which it proposes, at no cost on the equilibrium path; interruption then raises $P^*$ whenever $\beta(1-h)c_S>0$ and $t_U<\infty$, by at least $(1-\psi)\beta(1-h)c_Se^{-(r+\lambda)t_U/\beta}$, whatever the value of $\ell$.
\end{corollary}

In $D$, vulnerability is the comparison of $(1-h)c_S$ with $\ell$, and sensitivity is the discount $e^{-(r+\lambda)t_U}$, which rupture risk and impatience make steep. Variant (b) is the substantive restriction. Interruption helps only if a rule in place before offers decides it. Decided after a rejection, it is never used, because pausing it for one round saves Europe $\ell$ and changes nothing that determines the outcome. A government that wants this option must commit in advance to interrupt for as long as the dispute lasts. When one side chooses its disagreement action every round, complete-information bargaining generally has many subgame-perfect equilibria, some with costly delay \citep{fernandez1991striking, haller1990letter, busch1995perfect}. We characterize Markov perfect equilibria, in which strategies depend only on calendar time, the buffer stock and the current offer. Proofs are in \cref{app:proofs}.

Hold-up is nested too. ASML's US subsidiaries and the people they employ in the United States are US persons \citep{ecfr2026usperson, asml2026subsidiaries}, and ASML keeps offices next to US customer fabs \citep{asml2026locations}. No source we found names the entity that employs US field-service staff, but on this structure Washington could compel most servicing of US-sited fabs, so $h$ is close to one there. The scenario's 2029 measure, which restricts ASML's exports and servicing to US fabs in Arizona \citep{juijn2026europe}, is a game of this kind. ASML's entire US-sited revenue in 2025 was EUR 4.09 billion \citep{asml2026annual}, so Europe's own cost $\ell$ would be at most about EUR 0.34 billion a month, and its means of hurting those fabs would be limited to new optics, modules and software. A broad interruption covering the EUV fleet in Taiwan and Korea is a different game. There $h$ depends on Taipei and Seoul as well as on Washington, and Europe's own cost is larger, about EUR 1.9 billion a month of ASML revenue on a 2025 basis \citep{asml2026annual}. The flows that cannot be commandeered abroad include Zeiss optics and Trumpf drive lasers \citep{asml2026annual, varas2021strengthening}, so on our reading Germany would have to take part in any credible interruption.

The other parameters are poorly measured. The US buffer $t_U$ runs from about one month to two years or more. The short end takes at face value an anonymously sourced report that EUV tools need such frequent upkeep that ``without ASML's spare parts they quickly stop working'' \citep{bloomberg2024killswitch}, together with a collector lifetime of over 100 billion pulses demonstrated in 2017 \citep{fomenkov2017light}. The long end allows for stockpiled consumables and for parts and repair capacity seized locally. Because collector reflectivity declines by fractions of a percent per billion pulses \citep{fomenkov2017light}, $c_U(t)$ should rise smoothly, which \cref{prop:pain} allows. For the US cost of a broad interruption, TSMC's 2025 filing implies about US\$3.9 to 4.8 billion a month of wafer revenue from EUV nodes and US-headquartered customers \citep{tsmc2026annual}. With a downstream multiplier of 5 to 12, the upper end from the one-year Taiwan disruption scenario of \citet{varas2021strengthening}, and a post-buffer output loss that we assume lies between 10 and 70 percent, $c_S$ runs from about US\$2 billion to US\$40 billion a month, with a central value near US\$13 billion. These are revenues at risk, not welfare losses, and they omit Korean memory. With $\beta=1/2$ and central costs, interruption pays at $t_U=0$ only if $h$ is below about $0.85$. With an illustrative $r+\lambda$ of $0.55$ a year, $\bar t$ is about 26 months at $h=0.5$ and about 15 months at $h=0.7$. The answer turns more on $h$ than on $t_U$, and $h$ is the parameter with the least evidence behind it. Since $\bar t$ falls as $h$ rises, ASML's own plan to have 50 to 70 percent of service parts repaired locally \citep{asml2026annual} erodes Europe's hold-up option.

Servicing is not Europe's only hold-up instrument. In March 2026 Spain refused US use of jointly operated bases for the Iran war and closed its airspace to the aircraft involved \citep{roque2026spain}. Denial of access raises $c_U(t)$ during a dispute, and Washington cannot compel access to another state's territory the way it can compel US persons, so on our reading its $h$ is near zero. It is decided by one member state, outside the Council arithmetic, and its cost to that state includes the conditional part of US posture (\cref{sec:conditionality}). The timing condition applies as well: by variant (b), a denial improvised after a rejected offer would never be carried out.

Washington's dependence on ASML matters most here. In \cref{prop:master}, $V$ is the loss from China gaining the tools, and it reaches Europe's transfer only through agenda power under rupture risk and through $Q$. In \cref{prop:pain} the relevant loss is $c_S$, what Washington's own fabs lose without ASML, and it raises Europe's transfer without any rupture risk. The scenario's claim that Washington could outlast Europe through its Taiwanese partners is a claim that $t_U$ exceeds $\bar t$ (\cref{app:scenario}). For a measure confined to US soil the claim is plausible, since $h$ is near one there. For a broad interruption it depends on $h$, which the record does not pin down.
