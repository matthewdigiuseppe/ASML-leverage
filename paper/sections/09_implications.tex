\section{Implications and Scope}\label{sec:implications}

\subsection{Scope}\label{sec:scope}

Four conditions define the class of cases: (i) \emph{security dependence}, protection the holder cannot replace on the relevant horizon ($S_k>0$); (ii) \emph{a partly conditional guarantee}, some of which the patron can stake to withhold after exit, at an assurance cost ($\kappa S_k+d_p>0$; \cref{prop:conditionality}); (iii) \emph{a rival that bids} ($\omega>0$); and (iv) \emph{a nested chokepoint}, partly inside the patron's jurisdiction while the holder's licence still decides exit ($0<\theta<1$). The Netherlands, Japan and South Korea meet the first and fourth. On the second, the May 2026 posture order against Germany (\cref{sec:background}) suggests that $d_p$ is positive, while the conditionality of the core guarantee is largely untested (\cref{sec:record}). The third holds only weakly for all three: China spared Japan's leading equipment and photoresist makers while punishing a security statement (\cref{sec:cases}), but we found no Chinese offer for any holder's exit, one reason we think $\omega$ is small. Taiwan (threatened by China, not courted) and the Gulf partners (no treaty guarantee) fall outside. The model also assumes complete information about conditionality, unitary great powers and one rival (\cref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Implications for Europe}

Each implication holds in a region of parameter space. We place Europe at a nonpositive premium with little rupture risk: The Hague has received revocable statuses, which we do not code as transfers (\cref{sec:coding}), and has made no demand whose rejection risked realignment (\cref{sec:record}). That placement rests on jurisdiction.

\paragraph{Reduce the conditional part of dependence.} Against the NATO target of 5 percent of GDP by 2035 \citep{nato2025hague}, the model counts a euro only insofar as it replaces something Washington could withhold after exit: substitutes for posture and enablers more than duplicates of the statutory core (\cref{sec:security}), and AI capacity Washington cannot withhold, which lowers $d_{ai}$. While $Q<0$ a gain in premium removes slack one for one and brings no transfer (\cref{cor:rationing}). Where assurance is cheap at the margin, Washington answers it with a more conditional guarantee and pays nothing (\cref{prop:conditionality}(c)(i)). Europe gains only where assurance is already costly, slack already spent or conditionality at its ceiling. US statements tying protection to European conduct that multiply as European capability grows would place Europe in the regime in which assurance is cheap. For Washington, conditionality pays only if slack is spent, and untargeted drawdowns and rationing of loyal allies shrink it (\cref{sec:conditionality}).

\paragraph{Reduce the nesting.} Moving US-origin content and US-person servicing out of US jurisdiction lowers $\theta$, which raises $\omega$ only while China's cap does not bind (\cref{eq:omega}). It also raises $V$, which reaches the transfer directly only through agenda power under rupture risk (\cref{cor:master}(e)), a channel we think closed to Europe. The surer gain is a lower share $h$ of servicing that Washington can compel (\cref{sec:holdup}).

\paragraph{Interrupt servicing only under a rule that cannot be suspended.} Under the timing of \cref{cor:interrupt}, interruption raises Europe's transfer only if the US buffer is short and only for servicing Washington cannot compel. Under every timing we consider, it helps only if Europe chooses before offers and cannot revise the choice after an offer is rejected (\cref{sec:holdup}). On US soil we think $h$ is near one (\cref{sec:holdup}). The Union's current instruments allow no such commitment: each countermeasure the Union armed against Washington was suspended once Washington moved (\cref{sec:council,sec:holdup}). Under present institutions hold-up adds nothing to Europe's transfer, and Washington need not price a threat to interrupt. A credible option would need a legal basis, adopted before any dispute, that no EU body could suspend on a US offer. It would also need Germany, since Zeiss optics and Trumpf lasers cannot be commandeered abroad (\cref{sec:holdup}).

\paragraph{Expect a preventive demand.} Washington demands control when the premium is negative but expected to turn positive and its credible bite exceeds $\sigma+r^c$, or when it values control for its own sake (\cref{cor:control}(a), \cref{cor:windows}). Autonomy investments Europe cannot commit to forgo strengthen the motive and permit a purchase with no bite (\cref{cor:control}(b)). Rearmament raises the premium's drift and Chinese indigenization lowers it, and we cannot tell which dominates (\cref{sec:windows}), so we cannot say whether Europe is in a window. Demands for relocation, the most enforceable form (\cref{cor:control}(c)), board seats or a licensing veto that follow visible European investment would show that it is.

\paragraph{Bind, or keep the key.} Binding pays only when it pre-empts a demand that would otherwise come, and it pre-empts only if Washington values control little for its own sake; partial binding can invite a demand by raising slack (\cref{rem:bind}). Delegated Regulation 2025/2003 \citep{eu2025dualuse} bound Europe partway (\cref{sec:bind}). Whether that weakened the US motive turns on $v_c$. US demands shifting from alignment toward ownership or a licensing veto would indicate a high $v_c$, and binding would then have raised the bite and pre-empted nothing.

\paragraph{Chinese counter-threats.} De-risking, the aim of the Critical Raw Materials Act \citep{eu2024crma}, removes counter-threats. If Washington has a divisible sunk demand China cannot reach, Europe's loss stays at $B(J^c)$ and Washington weakly gains (\cref{prop:counter}(a)). We read the spending target and purchases of US equipment as such demands (\cref{sec:slackprinciple}), so for Europe de-risking pays mainly where China retaliates against Europe's own acts, as in Nexperia (\cref{sec:record}), and it weakens China's protection of European ownership (\cref{cor:control}(d)).

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

Complete information is the strongest assumption. If $\kappa$ were private, Washington would gain from overstating it, European beliefs would set $Q$, and the 2025 National Security Strategy would be partly cheap talk. Refusals and executed counter-threats could then come from misjudgment, the reading of Nexperia outside the model (\cref{sec:record}), and conditionality could exist only in the holder's beliefs, as Bonn's fears in 1975--77 suggest (\cref{sec:cases}). Because the autonomy investment is observable, the information problem of \citet{debs2014known} and \citet{bas2016dynamic} is absent. The assurance cost $H$ is a reduced form; if it is Washington's reputation with Tokyo and Seoul, conditionality toward Europe depends on what they observe. The great powers are unitary, although Congress can set stakes the executive would not, as the MATCH Act's 150-day deadline would (\cref{sec:background}). With one rival and holders bargaining alone, the model is silent on whether a coalition of the Netherlands, Japan and South Korea would help.
