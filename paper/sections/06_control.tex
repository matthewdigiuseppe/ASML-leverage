\section{Control Rights and Preventive Demands}\label{sec:control}

The static model takes Europe's control of the chokepoint as given and its exit premium as fixed. Yet the premium moves. Chinese credit or market offers raise $\omega$, European rearmament lowers Europe's stake in core defence $S_k$, and rationing of loyal allies would shrink the conditional part of the AI instrument; Chinese indigenization and stockpiling lower $W$ and push the other way. If Washington expects the premium to turn positive, it expects to pay rents that Europe cannot credibly promise to forgo. A transfer of control can remove them. This section asks when a patron demands control of a chokepoint, what it pays, and when the demand fails.

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:controlsetup}

There are two periods, $t\in\{1,2\}$, and a discount factor $\delta\in(0,1]$. In each period in which Europe holds control, the bidding contest of \cref{lem:bidding} is played at that period's premium $Q_t$, and Europe receives $\pos{Q_t}$. We assume that Washington retains Europe at every contest, including after a cut ($Q_1+d_1<V$ and $Q_1+d_1\le\bar m_U$, and the same in period 2 at the highest premium Europe can reach), and that payments are made at signing.

\paragraph{The ultimatum.} At the start of period 1, before the period-1 contest, Washington may issue an ultimatum at an issuing cost $\iota\ge 0$: Europe transfers control of ASML's customers, output and technology for both periods against a payment $T\ge 0$, or Washington withdraws a bundle $J$ of instruments for period 1. The bundle has unconditional part $u_1$, conditional part $d_1$ and direct ex post cost $g_1\ge 0$ to Washington, which is nonnegative because the baseline is Washington's ex post optimal provision to a loyal ally. If Europe refuses and the threat is carried out, Europe's premium rises to $Q_1+d_1$, and by \cref{prop:slack}(a) the refusal costs Europe the bite
\[
B_1=u_1+\min\{d_1,\Lambda_1\},\qquad \Lambda_1=\pos{-Q_1},
\]
net of the rent Washington must then pay. Carrying out the threat costs Washington $e=g_1+\pos{Q_1+d_1}-\pos{Q_1}$, its direct cost plus the extra rent. Under the commitment technology of \cref{sec:commitment} the threat is credible if and only if $e\le\bar m_U$; otherwise $B_1=0$. No other period-1 demand draws on the same bite, so the ultimatum has the whole budget of \cref{prop:slack}(c) to itself.

The bundle may contain any instrument of \cref{sec:security}. We do not expect the core guarantee to back such an ultimatum. On our reading, withdrawing protection from a Europe that stays aligned costs Washington its own stake in defending a loyal ally, so $g_1$ is large unless $s_A$ is small, and the threat is credible only if Washington can stake more than that. Posture, enablers and the AI instrument cost less to withdraw from one ally for one period.

\paragraph{Control.} A transfer lowers Europe's premium at both dates by $\Delta_c\in(0,\infty]$, the enforceability of control. With $\Delta_c=\infty$ Europe can never again choose exit. With $\Delta_c$ finite, a Europe whose premium later rises far enough could take back part of what it gave. What is transferred sets $\Delta_c$. Relocating capacity to US soil raises it. So do technical controls held in the United States: Bloomberg reported in 2024, citing anonymous sources, that ASML had told Dutch officials it could disable machines remotely \citep{bloomberg2024disable}, and a disabling capability held in Washington would be hard for The Hague to override. Control exercised through ASML's US subsidiaries, which are US-law entities \citep{asml2026subsidiaries}, sits under US jurisdiction. A controlling vote in a Dutch holding company has low $\Delta_c$, because it stays under Dutch law. In 2025 the Dutch state issued an order under the Goods Availability Act against Nexperia \citep{gerechtshof2025nexperia}, and on our reading a vote held by Washington would be exposed to the same kind of emergency power (\cref{sec:record}).

\paragraph{Values and costs.} Washington places an intrinsic value $v_c\ge 0$ on control, apart from the rents it saves. Europe bears a sovereignty cost $\sigma\ge 0$ if it transfers control. It includes the domestic political cost of surrendering a national champion and governance hurdles that are fixed before any demand is made: ASML's preference-share foundation and screening under the Vifo Act (\cref{sec:background}), and the Foreign Subsidies Regulation. Under that Regulation, a concentration involving an EU target with EU turnover of at least EUR 500 million must be notified when the parties received more than EUR 50 million in third-country financial contributions over three years, and the Commission may prohibit it after an in-depth review \citep{eu2022fsr}. That a US-funded vehicle taking control of ASML would be notifiable is our inference. Because these hurdles are public and set in advance, they raise $\sigma$ before Washington chooses whether to demand anything. China punishes a completed transfer with $r^c\ge 0$, which Europe bears; \cref{cor:control}(d) asks when that threat is credible.

\paragraph{Autonomy investment.} After the period-1 outcome, and before the period-2 contest, Europe can make an observable investment at cost $K$ that raises its period-2 premium by $\bar I$, for instance rearmament that lowers $S_k$. Holding a period-2 premium $q$, it invests if and only if $\delta(\pos{q+\bar I}-\pos{q})>K$.

\paragraph{Ties.} An indifferent Europe accepts; Washington issues only if it strictly gains (the limit $\iota\to 0^{+}$ when $\iota=0$); Europe invests only if that is strictly profitable. After a transfer Washington keeps its baseline provision, since withdrawing from a Europe that holds no control would cost it something and gain it nothing. The scope conditions of \cref{sec:scope} apply.

\subsection{Preventive control}\label{sec:preventive}

Let $I(q)=\mathbf 1\{\delta(\pos{q+\bar I}-\pos{q})>K\}$ indicate investment at period-2 premium $q$. Washington's discounted period-2 rent is $\mathrm{Rent}_U(q)=\delta\pos{q+\bar I\,I(q)}$, and Europe's period-2 value net of its investment cost is $\mathrm{Val}_H(q)=\mathrm{Rent}_U(q)-K\,I(q)$.

\begin{proposition}[Preventive control]\label{prop:control}
Let
\begin{align*}
\Delta R_U&=\pos{Q_1}-\pos{Q_1-\Delta_c}+\mathrm{Rent}_U(Q_2)-\mathrm{Rent}_U(Q_2-\Delta_c),\\
\Delta R_H&=\pos{Q_1}-\pos{Q_1-\Delta_c}+\mathrm{Val}_H(Q_2)-\mathrm{Val}_H(Q_2-\Delta_c).
\end{align*}
Let $B_1=u_1+\min\{d_1,\Lambda_1\}$ if the threat is credible ($e\le\bar m_U$, with $e=g_1+\pos{Q_1+d_1}-\pos{Q_1}$) and $B_1=0$ otherwise. In the subgame-perfect equilibrium, the United States issues the ultimatum, and Europe accepts, if and only if
\begin{equation*}
\Gamma\equiv v_c-\iota+\min\{\Delta R_U,\;(\Delta R_U-\Delta R_H)+B_1-\sigma-r^c\}>0 .
\end{equation*}
The payment is $T^*=\pos{\Delta R_H-B_1+\sigma+r^c}$, the United States gains $\Gamma$, and Europe's payoff is
$\max\{\Delta R_H-B_1,\,-\sigma-r^c\}+\pos{Q_1-\Delta_c}+\mathrm{Val}_H(Q_2-\Delta_c)$.
An ultimatum that is issued is accepted. The credibility condition $e\le\bar m_U$ does not depend on $\Delta_c$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is in \cref{app:proofs}. $\Delta R_U$ is the rent that control saves Washington and $\Delta R_H$ is what Europe gives up by losing it; they differ only by the investment cost that a transfer spares Europe. Washington gains the value of control net of the issuing cost, plus the smaller of the rent saved and what it can extract by force: the bite, less the deadweight cost $\sigma+r^c$, plus the investment cost saved. Europe is paid for the rents it surrenders and for the deadweight cost, less the bite. A refused ultimatum never pays Washington, so the threat is never carried out on the equilibrium path. Its credibility turns on what carrying it out would cost against a Europe that keeps control, which is why $\Delta_c$ does not enter.

In the special case $v_c=\iota=0$, $\Delta_c=\infty$ and $\bar I=0$, the gain is $\Gamma=\min\{\pos{Q_1}+\delta\pos{Q_2},\,B_1-\sigma-r^c\}$, and if $Q_1<0$ it is $\Gamma=\min\{\delta\pos{Q_2},\,B_1-\sigma-r^c\}$. Washington then demands control if and only if Europe's future premium is positive and the bite exceeds the deadweight cost of the transfer. The first condition holds only because Washington values control at exactly zero and does not issue when indifferent. With $v_c>\iota$ it disappears, and the general statement is a threshold. Since $B_1\le u_1+\Lambda_1$, a purely conditional bundle bites by more than $\sigma+r^c$ only if $Q_1<-(\sigma+r^c)$: the demand comes while Europe is weak and is expected to grow stronger.

\begin{corollary}[Motive, investment, enforceability, China]\label{cor:control}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Preventive motive.} $\Gamma>0$ if and only if $\Delta R_U>\iota-v_c$ and $B_1+(\Delta R_U-\Delta R_H)>\sigma+r^c+\iota-v_c$. If $v_c\le\iota$, issuance requires $\Delta R_U>0$ and $B_1+(\Delta R_U-\Delta R_H)>\sigma+r^c$. For $\Delta_c>0$, $\Delta R_U>0$ if and only if $Q_1>0$ or $Q_2+\bar I\,I(Q_2)>0$: Europe's premium is positive at date 1, or its premium after its own investment is positive at date 2.
\item \emph{Autonomy investment.} $\Delta R_U-\Delta R_H=K\,[I(Q_2)-I(Q_2-\Delta_c)]\ge 0$. Both arguments of the minimum in $\Gamma$ are weakly larger than when Europe has no investment option ($\bar I=0$), so an autonomy investment that Europe cannot commit to forgo weakly raises $\Gamma$. With investment, the United States can obtain control with no bite at all: if $B_1=0$, the ultimatum is issued if and only if $\Delta R_U>\iota-v_c$ and $K[I(Q_2)-I(Q_2-\Delta_c)]>\sigma+r^c+\iota-v_c$, and the transfer then saves Europe's investment cost.
\item \emph{Enforceability.} Holding $\sigma$, $r^c$, $v_c$ and $\iota$ fixed, $\Gamma$ and $T^*$ are nondecreasing in $\Delta_c$; $B_1$ and the credibility condition do not depend on $\Delta_c$.
\item \emph{Counter-threats against a transfer.} Before the United States decides, China announces a punishment $r\in[0,\bar r]$ of a transfer and a stake $m_C\le\bar m_C$. Carrying out $r$ after a transfer has ex post value $-c_C(r)$ to China, where $c_C$ is continuous and increasing with $c_C(0)=0$; the threat is credible if and only if $c_C(r)\le m_C$. China gets $H_C\ge 0$ if Europe keeps control, and the ex post payoff of its punishment decision ($-c_C(r)$ if it punishes, $-m_C$ if it does not) if control passes. Let $\Gamma(r)$ be $\Gamma$ with $r^c=r$ and $r_{\max}=\max\{r\le\bar r:c_C(r)\le\bar m_C\}$. In every subgame-perfect equilibrium:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item if control is transferred, China does not punish: on the path $r^c=0$, the payment is $\pos{\Delta R_H-B_1+\sigma}$ and $\Gamma(0)>0$;
\item a credible punishment $r>0$ is announced only if it deters, $\Gamma(r)\le 0$;
\item if $H_C>0$, control is transferred if and only if $\Gamma(r_{\max})>0$; otherwise China deters with a credible $r\ge v_c-\iota+\Delta R_U-\Delta R_H+B_1-\sigma$ whenever $\Gamma(0)>0$;
\item if $H_C=0$ and $\Gamma(0)>0\ge\Gamma(r_{\max})$, both the transfer and its deterrence are equilibrium outcomes.
\end{enumerate}
If punishing is self-rewarding ($c_C(r)<0$ for some $r$), (i) fails: China may let the transfer happen and punish it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

When $v_c=0$, the transfer is inefficient. The rents it avoids are transfers between Washington and Europe, while the sovereignty cost, China's punishment and the issuing cost are destroyed. Washington pays $\sigma+r^c+\iota$ in joint surplus to stop paying rents it would otherwise owe. That is the logic of preventive war \citep{fearon1995rationalist, powell2006war}, with a coerced transfer of control in place of a war. Under complete information nothing breaks down, because the object that shapes future bargaining power can itself be transferred, which is close to the case in which bargaining over such an object is efficient in \citet{fearon1996bargaining}, an unpublished manuscript. What our setting adds is the ceiling on coercion. The conditional part of the bite cannot exceed Europe's current slack, so the patron must act while the holder is weak, and the holder's slack is what it spends. \citet{benson2023commitment} find that the conditions that make prevention attractive also make it unlikely to be carried out. Our version is starker: the threat behind a successful demand is never carried out, so its cost matters only through its credibility.

Part (b) connects the demand to Europe's own choices. An investment in future bargaining power that Europe cannot commit to forgo invites the demand, as an observable nuclear programme invites prevention or a deal in \citet{debs2014known}, \citet{bas2016dynamic} and \citet{spaniel2019bargaining}. The investment raises the rents a transfer saves Washington, and it also creates a surplus that the two can share, because a transfer spares Europe an investment cost it would sink only to secure rents. The second effect permits a purchase with no bite at all. Take $\delta=1$, $Q_1=1$, $Q_2=-\tfrac12$, $\bar I=3$, $K=2$, $\Delta_c=\infty$, $\sigma=1$ and $r^c=v_c=\iota=0$, with no threat. Europe would invest, so Washington faces a period-2 rent of $2.5$ on top of the period-1 rent of $1$. It buys control for $T^*=2.5$ and gains $\Gamma=1$. Advice that Europe should rearm to raise its premium therefore carries a cost that the static model misses: rearmament Europe cannot commit to forgo can prompt an offer for the chokepoint itself.

Part (c) answers the question of what makes a transfer bind. A transfer that Europe could partly undo removes only $\min\{\pos{Q_t},\Delta_c\}$ of the rent at each date, so the commitment problem returns in period 2 whenever $Q_2-\Delta_c>0$. Washington then gains less and pays less. \citet{harstad2016market} reaches a similar conclusion about conservation: leasing beats buying when the seller's consumption value and the buyer's cost of protecting what it owns are large. The arrangements listed in \cref{sec:controlsetup} differ mainly in that cost. A demand to relocate production is the high-$\Delta_c$ end. Washington floated one for Taiwan in 2025, when the Commerce Secretary proposed that half of the chips the United States uses be made in the United States, and Taipei said it would not agree \citep{younger2025fiftyfifty, focustaiwan2025fiftyfifty}; \cref{sec:cases} compares the cases.

Part (d) concerns China. Its threat to punish a transfer is never carried out on the equilibrium path. If control passes, a credible punishment would cost China something ex post and would have deterred nothing, so China does better by announcing none. The threat therefore protects European ownership only by deterring the demand, which requires that China can stake enough to make $r$ credible and that $\Gamma(r_{\max})\le 0$. Sovereignty costs work the same way. A Netherlands that would pay a high price for surrendering ASML is harder to coerce into doing so, which makes the foundation, Vifo screening and the Foreign Subsidies Regulation part of Europe's protection against a demand.

\subsection{Windows}\label{sec:windows}

\Cref{prop:control} allows one ultimatum and a one-period cut. We now allow both at several dates.

\begin{lemma}[Spent and self-limiting cuts]\label{lem:selflimit}
Let dates $s=1,\dots,N$ have premia $Q_s$, slack $\Lambda_s=\pos{-Q_s}$ and discount factor $\delta$. A cut over a set $S$ of dates withdraws at each $s\in S$, before the date-$s$ contest, a bundle with unconditional part $u_s$, conditional part $d_s$ and direct ex post cost $g_s\ge 0$ to the United States. Suppose that at every $s\in S$ the United States still retains Europe after the cut ($Q_s+d_s<V_s$ and $Q_s+d_s\le\bar m_U$), and that the cut does not change the premium at any other date. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item the bite of the cut, Europe's loss net of the rents the United States must then pay, is $\sum_{s\in S}\delta^{s-1}\big(u_s+\min\{d_s,\Lambda_s\}\big)$;
\item the cost to the United States of carrying it out is $\sum_{s\in S}\delta^{s-1}e_s$ with $e_s=g_s+\pos{Q_s+d_s}-\pos{Q_s}$; with a separate stake at each date, the date-$s$ withdrawal is carried out if and only if $e_s\le m_s\le\bar m_U$;
\item extending a cut into a date with $Q_s\ge 0$ adds $\delta^{s-1}u_s$ to its bite and $\delta^{s-1}(g_s+d_s)$ to its cost to the United States. A purely conditional instrument ($u_s=0$) extended into such a date adds nothing to the bite.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

Both scope conditions matter. If a cut drives Europe out at some date, Europe's loss exceeds the formula. If Europe responds to a cut it learns of in advance, for instance with the autonomy investment of \cref{sec:controlsetup}, the loss falls short of it. Part (iii) governs threats of permanent exclusion. Whether such a threat is credible depends on how much Washington can stake, but a longer cut adds no bite once Europe's future premium is positive. At those dates a conditional cut hands Europe exit as its alternative and Washington pays the difference as rent.

\begin{corollary}[Windows]\label{cor:windows}
Consider $N$ dates with $v_c=0$ and $\Delta_c=\infty$. At any date $t$ at which Europe still holds control, the United States may issue, at cost $\iota>0$, an ultimatum: transfer control for all remaining dates against a payment $T\ge 0$, or face a cut at date $t$ only, with bite $b_t=u_t+\min\{d_t,\Lambda_t\}$ and execution cost $e_t=g_t+\pos{Q_t+d_t}-\pos{Q_t}$. Call $t$ \emph{eligible} if $e_t\le\bar m_U$, $b_t>\sigma+r^c$, and $Q_{t'}>0$ for some $t'\ge t$. There is $\bar\iota>0$ such that for every $\iota\in(0,\bar\iota)$, the United States issues an ultimatum on the equilibrium path if and only if some date is eligible. Every ultimatum issued is accepted, and the first one comes no later than the last eligible date (possibly at a date that is not eligible). The same holds at $\iota=0$ when the United States issues only if it strictly gains. If $u_t=0$ at every date, the condition $t'\ge t$ can be replaced by $t'>t$.
\end{corollary}

\Cref{fig:windows} draws the two windows. We say that Washington's threats still bite at date $t$ when the cut is credible and $b_t>\sigma+r^c$. In panel (a) that holds through $t=5$, and the premium turns positive at $t=9$. Washington acts at or before $t=5$, long before Europe's window opens. It can act earlier still: a Europe that refuses remains exposed to a later demand, so an ultimatum at a date where the bite alone would not justify one can pay. Of the 20,199 simulated games with an ultimatum in \cref{app:numerics}, 1,202 issued it at a date that was not itself eligible. In panel (b) the premium also rises, toward $-0.4$, but never crosses zero. With $v_c=0$ Washington has nothing to prevent. With $v_c>\iota$ it would demand control anyway whenever the bite covered the deadweight cost net of $v_c-\iota$, as \cref{cor:control}(a) shows for two periods.

The timing differs from the preventive-war models. In \citet{powell2006war} war requires a shift in power that is large and rapid relative to what the declining state can be offered within the period. \citet{krainin2017preventive} shows that slow but persistent shifts can also produce preventive war, possibly after a delay. Here, under complete information, the speed of the shift affects when Washington acts and what it pays, and not whether it acts; what matters is whether a date at which the threat still bites precedes a date with positive premium. The difference comes from treating control as a commitment device. Transferring it removes every future rent at once, so the patron need not offer within one period an amount that matches the whole future shift. That holds only when $\Delta_c=\infty$. With finite enforceability a transfer removes at most $\Delta_c$ of rent per date, a shift far above $\Delta_c$ leaves rents that no transfer can reach, and size and speed matter again, much as in Powell's account. \citet{bas2016dynamic} find that an observer that monitors imperfectly waits until a shift looks imminent. Our patron, under complete information, acts before its own window closes, which may be long before Europe's opens.

The windows also make the direction of drift decisive. A demand requires that Europe's premium be expected to rise. Rearmament and rationing push it up. Chinese indigenization and stockpiling push it down by lowering $W$, and with them the US motive for a preventive demand. Which effect dominates is an empirical question, and \cref{sec:predictions} states what each answer implies.

\subsection{Promises or threats}\label{sec:bind}

Europe could try to remove the commitment problem itself. Allied or Europeanized export controls, or controls entrenched in treaty, would bind Europe against exit without transferring control. Binding raises Europe's slack and with it the bite of Washington's threats.

\begin{remark}[Self-binding and dual key]\label{rem:bind}
Before period 1, Europe can adopt a binding rule (Europeanized or treaty-entrenched controls) that lowers its premium at both dates by $\Delta_b\in(0,\infty]$ at cost $\sigma^b$, drawing a Chinese punishment $r^b$; Europe keeps the key. The game of \cref{prop:control} is then played with premia $Q_t-\Delta_b$, and the bite of the US threat is computed at the new slack.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item With $\Delta_b=\infty$, control removes no rents, and the bite becomes $B_1^b=u_1+d_1$ if $g_1\le\bar m_U$ (and $0$ otherwise). The United States then issues if and only if $\Gamma^b=v_c-\iota+\min\{0,\,B_1^b-\sigma-r^c\}>0$, so binding pre-empts the ultimatum if and only if $v_c\le\iota$ or $v_c-\iota+B_1^b\le\sigma+r^c$. If it does not, the United States demands control and pays $\pos{\sigma+r^c-B_1^b}$.
\item Let $Q_1<0$, $\Delta_c=\infty$, no investment and $\Delta_b=\infty$. Europe's payoff is $-\sigma^b-r^b-\mathbf 1\{\Gamma^b>0\}\min\{\sigma+r^c,B_1^b\}$ if it binds, and $\max\{\delta\pos{Q_2}-B_1,\,-\sigma-r^c\}$ if it does not bind and $\Gamma>0$, or $\delta\pos{Q_2}$ if it does not bind and $\Gamma\le 0$. In particular, if the United States would issue the ultimatum without binding and binding pre-empts it, Europe strictly prefers to bind if and only if $-\sigma^b-r^b>\max\{\delta\pos{Q_2}-B_1,\,-\sigma-r^c\}$. If the United States would not issue without binding, Europe never strictly prefers to bind.
\item A partial binding ($\Delta_b<\infty$) lowers the rents control would remove but raises the slack, hence the bite, so it can make $\Gamma$ positive. Example: $\delta=1$, $Q_1=-1$, $Q_2=10$, $u_1=0$, $d_1=5$, $g_1=0$, $\sigma=1.5$, $r^c=v_c=\iota=0$. Without binding $\Gamma=\min\{10,\,1-1.5\}<0$. With $\Delta_b=3$, $\Gamma=\min\{7,\,4-1.5\}=2.5$ and the United States demands control at $T^*=4.5$.
\item Dual-key licensing is a partial control transfer with enforceability $\Delta_d<\infty$, sovereignty cost $\sigma_d<\sigma$, value $v_d$ and Chinese punishment $r^d$. If the United States can demand either arrangement, it demands the one with the larger of $\Gamma_{\mathrm{full}}$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{dual}}$ (each given by \cref{prop:control} with that arrangement's parameters), provided it is positive. Because $\sigma_d<\sigma$, the United States can obtain a dual key when the bite is too small to obtain full control.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

Binding pre-empts a demand only if Washington values control little for its own sake. Full binding gives Europe unlimited slack and so maximizes the bite, $B_1^b=u_1+d_1$, which makes control cheaper to coerce for a patron with $v_c>\iota$. By part (b), binding pays Europe only when it pre-empts a demand that would otherwise come, and only when its own cost is below what the demand would cost Europe. Part (c) runs against intuition. A rule that binds Europe partway removes some of the rents Washington fears and adds to the slack its threats can draw on, and the second effect can turn a demand that would not pay into one that does.

Part (d) separates a dual key from binding. Under binding Europe holds the only key and lowers its own premium. Under a dual key Washington holds a veto over China-bound licences, which is a transfer of a control right over customers, priced by \cref{prop:control} with its own enforceability and sovereignty cost. The Dutch government's answer to the MATCH Act, that every country is responsible for its own export-control legislation \citep{tk2026match}, suggests to us that $\sigma_d$ in The Hague is not far below $\sigma$.

We read the European steps of 2024 and 2025 (\cref{sec:background}) as partial binding. In September 2024 The Hague took over licensing of the NXT:1970i and NXT:1980i from Washington, a change that ASML said ensures that the Dutch government ``is the sole licensing authority'' \citep{asml2024license}. That step lowered Europe's premium and kept the key in Europe, the reverse of a control transfer. The December 2024 exclusion of exporters in the Netherlands and other listed countries from the new SME licence requirements rested on the authority of those governments to ``control key SME items of concern'' \citep{bis2024fdpr}, and \cref{sec:record} codes it as a reward for self-binding. When Delegated Regulation 2025/2003 put the most advanced immersion tools on the EU list in November 2025, the licence decision stayed in The Hague \citep{eu2021dualuse, asml2026annual}. On our reading the listing raised $\Delta_b$, because removing the entry now takes an act at the Union level that the Netherlands cannot adopt alone. The August 2025 joint statement, in which the EU said it planned to adopt technology-security requirements in line with those of the United States and Washington said it would then facilitate AI chip exports \citep{whitehouse2025jointstatement}, is a statement of intent with small $\Delta_b$. None of these steps comes close to $\Delta_b=\infty$. By part (c), they could have strengthened the US motive for a demand as easily as weakened it, depending on how far Washington expected Europe's premium to rise.

\subsection{Private sovereignty costs and breakdown}\label{sec:breakdown}

Under complete information an ultimatum is either accepted or never issued. Breakdown requires that Washington be uncertain about what a transfer would cost Europe. We think that uncertainty is real. Since February 2026 the Dutch government has been a coalition of D66, CDA and VVD with minority support in parliament \citep{nltimes2026coalition, omroepbrabant2026kabinet}, so the domestic price of a transfer would depend on whichever majority the cabinet could find.

\begin{proposition}[Breakdown]\label{prop:breakdown}
Assume the conditions of \cref{prop:control} with $\Delta_c=\infty$, no investment, $Q_1<0$ and $e\le\bar m_U$, and let $\sigma$ be Europe's private information, distributed with CDF $F$ on $[0,\bar\sigma]$, density $f>0$ on $(0,\bar\sigma)$, and $F$ log-concave. Let $R=\delta\pos{Q_2}$, $\tilde b=B_1-r^c$ and $\Pi(s;A,x)=F(s)(A-s)-(1-F(s))x$. For $A+x>0$ let $s^*(A,x)$ be the unique solution in $(0,\bar\sigma)$ of $A+x-s=F(s)/f(s)$ if one exists and $\bar\sigma$ otherwise; let $s^*(A,x)=0$ if $A+x\le 0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Threat-backed ultimatum.} With payment $T$, Europe accepts if and only if $\sigma\le T+\tilde b-R$. The optimal cutoff for the United States is $\hat s=\max\{\tilde b-R,\,\min\{\bar\sigma,s^*\}\}$ with $s^*=s^*(\tilde b+v_c,e)$, its payment is $\hat s-\tilde b+R\ge 0$, and its gain is $\Pi_B=\Pi(\hat s;\tilde b+v_c,e)-\iota$. Conditional on this ultimatum, Europe refuses and the threat is carried out with probability $P_b=1-F(\hat s)$. For uniform $F$, $s^*=(\tilde b+v_c+e)/2$.
\item \emph{Purchase offer.} If the United States instead offers to buy control without staking the threat, Europe accepts if and only if $\sigma\le T-r^c-R$, the optimal cutoff is $s_0=s^*(v_c-r^c,0)$, and the gain is $\Pi_0=\Pi(s_0;v_c-r^c,0)-\iota$. If $v_c\le r^c$, then $\Pi_0\le -\iota$.
\item The United States makes an offer if and only if $\max\{\Pi_B,\Pi_0\}>0$ and backs it with the threat if and only if $\Pi_B\ge\Pi_0$. If $v_c\le r^c$ (in particular if $v_c=0$), it issues if and only if $F(\hat s)(\tilde b+v_c-\hat s)-(1-F(\hat s))e>\iota$, and every offer is threat-backed.
\item Across parameter values at which the United States issues a threat-backed ultimatum, $P_b$ is nonincreasing in $B_1$, $e$ and $v_c$, nondecreasing in $r^c$ and $R$, and independent of $\iota$. These comparative statics hold for every continuous $F$ (not necessarily log-concave) when $\hat s$ is the largest, or the smallest, maximizer.
\item If $v_c>r^c$ and the United States chooses between (a) and (b), then across parameter values at which it makes an offer the refusal probability is nonincreasing in $B_1$ and $v_c$, and the breakdown probability (refusal followed by the executed threat) is nonincreasing in $e$ and nondecreasing in $R$. The refusal probability is nondecreasing in $r^c$ on any range where the threat-backed payment is positive. The other directions can fail: the refusal probability can rise with $e$ and fall with $R$, and the breakdown probability can rise with $B_1$ and $v_c$ and fall with $r^c$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Breakdown is the outcome in which Europe refuses, loses what the threat withdraws, and keeps the chokepoint. Given a threat-backed ultimatum, it is more likely when China's punishment of a transfer is credible and large, when Europe's future premium is high, and when the threat is cheap for Washington to carry out. The last is the usual risk-return trade-off \citep{fearon1995rationalist, powell1999shadow}: a patron that loses little from a refusal makes a tougher offer and is refused more often. A larger bite makes refusal costlier for Europe, and a higher value of control makes Washington willing to pay more; both lower the chance of refusal. When $v_c>r^c$, a patron whose threat is expensive to carry out may prefer to offer money alone, since a refused purchase offer costs it nothing, and a rise in the bite can then move it to the threat and raise the probability of breakdown (part (e)). Unlike the complete-information case, a credible Chinese punishment is now carried out on the path with positive probability, because the types that accept are punished.

The result rests on a one-shot ultimatum, which is a commitment assumption. If Washington could not commit to walk away after a refusal and could renew its offer quickly, it would compete with its own later offers. With one-sided private information and offers made by the uninformed side, that side loses most of its power to screen as the time between offers shrinks \citep{fudenberg1985infinite, gul1986foundations}. A patron that cannot commit to a single demand would extract less, and we expect delay to replace some of the refusals that \cref{prop:breakdown} counts as breakdown. Each refusal would also execute a cut, which brings the repeated game closer to a war of attrition; we do not solve it. The probabilities in \cref{prop:breakdown} are best read as those of a patron that can commit to one public demand, a commitment that a staked promise to make no further offer could supply (\cref{sec:slackprinciple}).
