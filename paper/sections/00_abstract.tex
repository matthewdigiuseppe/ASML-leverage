Since 2019 the Netherlands, home of ASML, sole maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, has aligned its export controls with US demands for little in return. So has Japan since 2023. Yet both resist limits on servicing in China. In our model a security-dependent chokepoint holder (Europe) bargains with its patron while a rival bids for it. The holder's exit premium, the rival's credible offer net of the credible costs of exit, is a floor on what it obtains; unless the holder makes proposals in talks that can rupture for long, or can hurt the patron while they bargain, its leverage is the positive part of that premium. Whatever bargaining sets the holder's price, its surplus over exit bounds what the patron's conditional threats can extract. Demands compete for that budget only where the patron enforces them through the alliance itself. Refusals need a patron unsure of what a demand costs and are likeliest when the premium is near zero. Any surplus that a transfer of control would remove gives the patron a preventive motive to demand control. US jurisdiction over ASML's inputs and staff can keep Europe's premium negative without any security term. Beyond that, its sign turns on China's credible promises and on any credible US threat to withhold frontier AI, which on our orders of magnitude outweighs the conditional part of the US guarantee.
