Since 2019 the Netherlands, home of ASML, the only maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, has aligned its export controls with US demands while extracting little in return, as has Japan. Yet both resist some demands, such as limits on servicing tools installed in China. We explain the pattern with a game-theoretic model in which a security-dependent holder of a chokepoint bargains with its patron while the patron's rival bids for the chokepoint. Every threat is sequentially rational. We verify each result by brute-force computation. A holder's leverage is bounded by its exit premium: the rival's credible offer, net of the costs the patron can credibly impose. The patron's demands are bounded by the holder's slack, and its conditional threats share that one budget. Conditionality is the price of concessions: a patron makes its guarantee more conditional than deterrence requires only to create slack it can spend. When slack is wasting, the patron has a preventive motive to demand control of the chokepoint. Europe's leverage over Washington therefore depends on the conditional part of its dependence and on China's credible promises. Washington's need for ASML matters only through named channels such as rupture risk and hold-up over servicing.
